Part II · Constructing the arithmetic inputs›Ch. 7 · The arithmetic balance›§7.1 An identity before an estimate

A bound for a centered prime sum can survive a surprisingly large error in a single arithmetic measurement. The proof takes measurements at many places and along a chain, then divides by their total weight. This chapter follows that division and accounts for each power of the scale that it costs.

7.1An identity before an estimate

Write H for the finite abelian group used to label the translations, E = (ℤ/2ℤ)² × H, and Φ for the subgroup whose first coordinate is zero. The character χC equals +1 on Φ and −1 on its complement. The index 0 in a sum over additive characters denotes the character identically equal to one. The map ℓ assigns a complex number to each character. At this stage it is input data. Its identification with logarithmic derivatives belongs to the arithmetic realization.

Choose a > 0, a translation τ ∈ H, weights wρ > 0 for ρ ∈ Φ, and an integer cutoff N ≥ a/wρ for every such ρ. The host data distinguish a prime d and a finite set S containing 2 and the auxiliary parameter q1, with d outside S. Outside S and d inertia is trivial. At primes of S, both inertia and the specified Frobenius element lie in Φ. At d, the inertia elements are exactly zero and the involution τ₀ = ((1,0),0). Inertia here records the local symmetries invisible on the residue field. Frobenius describes raising residues to the residue-field size.

Write A for the weighted prime-power count at one sample and one chain stage. To assign a weight to g outside Φ, pair it with ρ = ((1,1),0) + g + ((0,0),τ) inside Φ. At n = pi the weight is (a/n − wρ)+, where the subscript means the positive part. At an unramified prime the character sum is 2 if g equals the ith Frobenius power and zero otherwise. Its contribution is therefore twice log p times the weight when that power lies outside Φ. At a prime of S the Frobenius coset lies inside Φ and contributes zero. At d the inertia subgroup has order two, so averaging replaces 2 by 1 at each matching element outside Φ. Each such weight contributes log d times its value. These three calculations identify the count with the finite character expansion.

For v = wρ the identity (a/n − v)+ = v(a/(v n) − 1)+ changes the scale to y = a/v. Terms beyond the cutoff vanish. Write Gψ(y) for the centered ramp. For a nontrivial ψ it sums the von Mangoldt weight, multiplied by the local character value and y/n − 1, over 1 ≤ n ≤ y, then adds y ℓ(ψ). For the trivial character it uses the centered zeta ramp. Substitution gives the following main term, where γ is Euler’s constant.

M = a log a − (1 + γ)a − (a/|Φ|) ∑ρ ∈ Φ log wρ + a ℓ(χC).

The trivial character gives the first three terms. For the other characters the centering contributes −a ℓ(ψ). Summing the conjugate phases over the complementary half of E kills the nonconstant characters except χC, whose sum is −|Φ|. This accounts for the plus sign. The pairing of the two halves permutes the weight indices, giving precisely the average of their logarithms. The remainder A − M is the same character average with centered ramps in the summands. This proves the analytic identity at fixed host data. Identifying the degrees, periods, heights and covolumes of the geometric construction with this particular M is an additional theorem.

At one translation the lattice calculation uses the sum over all sampled indices t and stages j. Its total amplitude was denoted by A = ∑t at. Here the object being summed is the count At,j just computed, and its main term is Mt,j. The two identifications together make the geometric residual exactly ∑t,j (At,j − Re Mt,j). This equality is what allows the character average to use the geometric bound.

7.2Which characters survive?

Let A0 be a subgroup of H. For ψ a character of E, put Sψ = ∑u ∈ A₀ ψ(0, −u). Translation by any v ∈ A0 permutes this sum and multiplies it by ψ(0, −v). Thus the sum vanishes whenever one of these multipliers differs from one. Otherwise it equals |A0|. For e1(τ) = ((1,0),τ) and s ∈ A0, the corresponding shifted sum acquires the phase ψ(e1(τ)). Summing over s ∈ T2 ⊆ A0 and averaging over subgroup translations therefore contributes a density δ2/2 with δ2 = |T2|/|H|. The two in the denominator comes from |Φ| = 2|H|.

For example, take H = ℤ/4ℤ and A0 = {0, 2}. On H the four characters are ψj(h) = exp(2πijh/4) for j = 0, 1, 2, 3. Averaging ψj over A0 gives (1 + (−1)j)/2. The odd indices cancel and the even ones survive with value 1. If T2 contains one element, its contribution occupies one of the eight positions in Φ. The factor 1/8 comes from that count, independently of which surviving character is evaluated.

At a scale x ≥ 3, fix a loss ε > 0, a real sampling weight c with |c| ≤ 1, and a finite sample T ⊆ H. The survivors form the annihilator Ψ of A0. A further selection K ⊆ Ψ retains characters used in the target. These characters satisfy ψ(τ₀) = 1, and the selection includes the trivial character. Among retained characters, a character trivial on all of H receives weight 1. The others receive c. Call these weights ωψ. For retained characters nontrivial on H, the sum over T of ψ(0,τ) differs from c|T| in norm by at most M0x−1+ε|T|, where M0 also bounds |Ψ|. The constants bounding ℓ on retained nontrivial and discarded characters are respectively K0 and CE log x. The sum of norms of the discarded centered ramps is denoted Σ. Orthogonality by itself leaves this last error to be paid.

7.3Dividing the error by the observed weight

Write n = |H|, t = |T|, v = |T2|, m for the length of the chain and b for its positive weight scale. At a translation τ the measurement uses an amplitude aτ with |aτ/b − x| ≤ 1. In the realized chain aE is the chain’s size parameter, and the adjustable positive parameter k is real. For each subgroup translation, Err bounds the sum, over all t samples and m chain positions, of A − Re M.

For each subgroup translation u, the local comparison keeps the indices ((0,1),s + u) with s ∈ T2. Call these the thick indices. At those indices b ≤ w ≤ b + η + 1 with η ≥ 0. Replacing w by b changes the weighted ramp by at most 3(x + 1)(η + 1). At each other index in Φ, w ≤ 2b′. The parameters satisfy b′, X′ > 0 and b′X′ ≤ xb, so aτ/w ≥ X′/3. On this range assume ‖Gψ(y)‖/y ≤ κ with κ ≥ 0. Omitting that term costs at most (x + 1)bκ. With f = |Φ|, the normalized character sum multiplies the sum of these two allowances by at most 2f. The conditions 6f(x + 1)(η + 1) ≤ b/x and 2f(x + 1)κ ≤ 1/x give a total allowance of 2b/x per measurement.

How much does moving the weight change one ramp?

Fix one measurement, with amplitude a > 0. As its weight w varies, both the scale a/w and the multiplier in front of the centered ramp change. Write Fψ(w) = wGψ(a/w). For all positive w, w′ and every character ψ, the variation satisfies

|Fψ(w) − Fψ(w′)| ≤ (3a/min(w, w′)) |w − w′|.

Assume w ≤ w′, and set Δ = w′ − w. At index n, multiplying the original ramp by w gives (a/n − w)+. Raising the weight by Δ shortens this ramp by at most Δ, even if it crosses zero. Both ramps vanish for n > a/w. Thus we can compare their sums over the same finite set.

For a nontrivial ψ, the coefficient of this ramp is Λ(n) times a local character value of absolute value at most one. At a ramified prime this value is an average of roots of unity. The centering contributes w(a/w)ℓ(ψ) = aℓ(ψ) at either weight, so it cancels. By the triangle inequality the absolute change is at most Δ ∑n≤a/w Λ(n), hence at most 3(a/w)Δ by the following estimate.

Why is the von Mangoldt sum at most three times the cutoff?

Put Ψ(y) = ∑1≤n≤y Λ(n). For an integer m ≥ 1, each prime power pj in (m,2m] contributes log p to Ψ(2m) − Ψ(m). It also contributes one to the exponent of p in the central binomial coefficient. Indeed, counting multiples in (2m)!/(m!)² gives the exponent as the sum over j ≥ 1 of ⌊2m/pj⌋ − 2⌊m/pj⌋. Every summand is zero or one, and every power in that interval contributes one. Therefore Ψ(2m) − Ψ(m) ≤ log binom(2m,m) ≤ 2m log 2. The last inequality follows by summing all binomial coefficients of degree 2m.

If y > 1, choose the integer k with 2k−1 < y ≤ 2k. Adding the estimates at m = 1, 2, …, 2k−1 along the powers of two gives Ψ(y) ≤ (2k+1 − 2) log 2 < (4 log 2)y < 3y. For 0 ≤ y ≤ 1 the sum vanishes.

For the trivial character the zeta centering leaves −a log(a/w) + (1 + γ)a. Let D be the uncentered ramp sum at w minus its value at w′. Since every coefficient is Λ(n) ≥ 0, the preceding argument gives 0 ≤ D ≤ 3(a/w)Δ. The centered difference is D − a log(w′/w). The quantity subtracted belongs to the same interval, since log(1 + Δ/w) ≤ Δ/w. The distance between two numbers in that interval is at most its length. This proves the stated constant for ζ as well. Interchanging the weights treats w′ < w.

For example, take a = 12 and change w from 3 to 4. In the zeta sum the term at 2 drops by log 2 and the term at 3 drops by log 3, reaching zero. All other terms have zero change. Thus D = log 6. The centered difference is log 6 − 12 log(4/3), about −1.66. The sign change comes from the logarithmic correction.

The cost per measurement

At a thick index the target weight is b, the actual weight lies in [b, b + η + 1], and |a/b − x| ≤ 1. The smaller weight in the denominator of the variation bound is therefore b. Substituting the two upper bounds gives

|Fψ(w) − Fψ(b)| ≤ 3(a/b)(w − b) ≤ 3(x + 1)(η + 1).

There are f = |Φ| possible positions, each with 2f characters in the Fourier sum, and the sum is divided by f. Since its phases have absolute value one, the total charge for changing thick weights is at most 6f(x + 1)(η + 1) ≤ b/x. The allowance for the omitted thin positions is another b/x. Adding these two contributions produces the 2b/x per measurement used in the averaged inequality.

Dividing by the total weight of the retained characters

At the central scale write B(x) for the retained weighted sum after paying for the discarded characters, ∑ψ ∈ K ωψ Re Gψ(x) − Σ, where Σ = ∑ψ ∈ Ψ ∖ K ‖Gψ(x)‖. This quantity varies with the measurement scale and can have either sign. The positive multiplicity at a pole depends on the point ρ. In the estimates below abbreviate B(x) to B and put Q = t m b. The estimate takes the form

Q (v/2n)(B − 4X) ≤ Err + 2Q/x,   X = x1/2+ε.

For positive observed weight Qv/(2n), direct division shows what an error costs. The upper bound for B is 4X + (2n/v)(Err/Q + 2/x). In the four-translation example v/(2n) = 1/8, so a unit of normalized error Err/Q increases the allowance by eight. A large negative B already satisfies the inequality. Its use later in the proof therefore requires the specific linear combinations of characters that recover the desired upper bound.

For each surviving character, the source bounds the real part of its phase-weighted sample sum from below, comparing each aτ/b with x. After summation the lower bound is t(B − 4X). Averaging the local upper bounds over the subgroup introduces the factor v/(2n). The signed lower and upper estimates then give the displayed inequality. The norms in Σ permit the discarded characters to contribute with either sign.

Assume x ≥ 3, ε > 0, C ≥ 0, b > 0, n ≥ 1, 0 ≤ aE ≤ 2x b. The scalar size hypothesis bounds Err by xCε times the sum of four quantities. Set Y = x1/2+13ε/8. The last column gives the bound under the size assumptions below.

Together with the upper bounds on m and k, these give the last column. Since Y ≥ 1, adding the four rows gives Err/Q ≤ 10xCεY. Both bounds on k must hold at the same scale.

Since 2/x ≤ 1 and xCεY ≥ 1, the scalar inequality gives (v/n)(B − 4X) ≤ 22xCεY. Divide by the positive density, whose reciprocal is at most xε. Then B ≤ 26x1/2+(C+21/8)ε. The last threshold absorbs 26 and gives the exponent 1/2 + (C + 3)ε. The bound controls the signed value of B that occurs in the averaged inequality.

7.4What does one arithmetic measurement buy?

Fix a level, a scale x in its arithmetic window and the sampling data at that scale. At translation σ, subtract the predicted main term from the prime-power count and sum over the samples and chain stages. Call this real difference Δσ. The lattice argument gives an upper bound Err for each Δσ. The character calculation reads the same differences in the opposite direction. Write B for the weighted sum of the retained real parts after subtracting the allowance for discarded characters.

Set X = x½+ε and write Q for the total weight. It is the product of the number of samples, the number of stages and the weight per measurement. The average runs over the translation subgroup. At each translation the calculation keeps v of the 2n weight positions, a fraction v/(2n). Replacing their individual weights by a common weight and bounding the omitted terms costs the additive 2Q/x. Upper bound for one translation · The averaged comparison

Q · [v/(2n)](B − 4X) ≤ meanσ Δσ + 2Q/x ≤ Err + 2Q/x.
Which part of this chain can a normalized theta coefficient justify?

In a differentiated theta series, the coefficient of a monomial of degree j in one variable is multiplied by j! when its jth derivative is evaluated at zero. After the section and its coordinates have been normalized, a valuation estimate on that derivative controls divisibility of a lattice-map entry. This contributes to the upper bound on Δσ. The other use of theta, constructing nonzero global lifts, supplies the Galois row used to select a window. There the weighted cohomology lengths fit within √b, with b the weight per measurement. Setting Err equal to √b would drop the other costs of the lattice comparison. The factorial in the entry · How the lifts supply the row · Where the length bound enters

If the total allowance is 60, how far above 4X can B lie?

For an arithmetic check on the division, take Q = 80, v/(2n) = ¼ and Err + 2Q/x = 60. The observed weight is 20, giving B ≤ 4X + 3. A value 100 below 4X also satisfies the inequality. Recovering the fixed family sum requires adding back the allowance for discarded characters. The analytic test then uses the positive part of that family sum. Recovery of the level sum · The positive part in the pole test

Would measurements at both ends of the interval suffice?

A continuous positive bump between the endpoints can have any prescribed integral while vanishing at both ends. The arithmetic construction provides estimates throughout one interval for the same level sum. Passing to u = log x gives the integral bound on that interval, and arbitrarily distant such intervals are available. Carry the bound across the whole interval.

7.5Recovering the level sum

For a level Λ of order o, apply the preceding argument with c = 1/(3o + 1) and M0 = 4o. Use the local character estimates and the compatible chain data at the chosen scale. In particular eD + eL ≥ 0 and the error exponent C can be taken as eD + eL + 3. Each discarded character contributes at most x1/2+ε in norm. Counting the entire annihilator bounds Σ by 4o x1/2+ε. Thus GΛ is at most (1 + 4o)x1/2+(eD+eL+6)ε. Choosing x large enough that xε ≥ 1 + 4o gives

GΛ(x) ≤ x1/2+(eD+eL+7)ε.

The exponent applies to scales carrying the required data. A fixed ε allows thresholds depending on the level and constants, uniformly in the later choices of host and sample. The window construction supplies such scales.

The third-place construction used by the final theorem chooses eD = 2 and eL = 0. This specialization gives 1/2 + 9ε.
