Part III · From estimates to zeros›Ch. 10 · Reflection and the final statement›§10.1 Removing an imprimitive level

Once every Dirichlet character has a zero-free right half-plane, the passage to classical RH is short. Two small points do most of the work. A change of modulus introduces finite Euler factors, and reflection changes the character.

10.1Removing an imprimitive level

The conductor f of χ modulo N is the least divisor through which its character on units factors. Write ψ for the primitive character at that conductor. Changing to modulus N suppresses Euler factors at primes dividing N. On the common domain of the change-of-level identity, this gives

L(s,χ) = L(s,ψ) ∏p∣N (1 − ψ(p) p−s).

For a prime dividing f, ψ(p) = 0 and the displayed factor equals one. For every other prime its absolute value before subtraction satisfies |ψ(p) p−s| ≤ p−Re s < 1 whenever Re s > 0. Each factor is therefore nonzero. The formula initially follows from Euler products and extends where the continued identity applies. The formal change-of-level lemma asks that ψ be nonprincipal or s ≠ 1. If χ is nonprincipal then ψ must be nonprincipal too, since inducing a principal character would give χ principal. Thus the exception χ = 1 and s = 1 is accounted for.

A change of modulus you can calculate. Let ψ be the character modulo 3 that takes −1 at 2. Induce it to χ modulo 6. On the odd integers the two agree, and the change removes the even terms. Thus L(s,χ) = (1 + 2−s)L(s,ψ). The factor at 3 is one because ψ(3) = 0. When Re s > 0, the added term 2−s has absolute value strictly below one. At the boundary point s = πi/log 2 it equals −1 and the finite factor vanishes. This locates precisely where the zero-transfer argument uses strict positivity of the real part.

10.2The strip theorem for characters

Assume 0 < Re s < 1 and L(s,χ) = 0. The finite factors transfer the zero to ψ. Its completed function is obtained by multiplying by the real gamma factor appropriate to the parity of ψ. The gamma arguments have positive real part in this strip, so the factors are finite and nonzero. In particular the completed function vanishes too. The functional equation for ψ−1, evaluated at s, gives a zero of the completed function for ψ−1 at 1 − s. On units the inverse character is its complex conjugate. The arguments s and 1 − s avoid 0 and 1. Returning to the uncompleted function gives L(1 − s, ψ−1) = 0.

If Re s < ½, then Re(1 − s) > ½, contradicting the window bound with the exponent chosen after the alleged zero, applied to the inverse primitive character. If Re s > ½, nonvanishing for χ already contradicts the original zero. This leaves Re s = ½. It proves the strip statement for arbitrary moduli.

Follow a hypothetical zero of a complex character

Modulo 5, the residue 2 generates the unit group. The rule ψ(2) = i therefore fixes a primitive character and ψ−1(2) = −i. If L(¼ + 2i,ψ) vanished, the completed functional equation would give a zero for ψ−1 at ¾ − 2i. The modulus is still 5. The character has changed, so the right-half-plane theorem must cover the inverse as well. Conjugating this latter zero would give a zero for ψ at ¾ + 2i. This last operation explains why the same-character symmetry in a plot is a reflection across a vertical line, even though the functional equation uses 1 − s.

10.3Why classical RH excludes the negative even integers

Take the unique character of modulus one. Every positive integer represents the identity in its unit group, so the character takes the value 1 on every term of its Dirichlet series. For Re s > 1 the series is therefore ∑n ≥ 1 n−s = ζ(s). Uniqueness of meromorphic continuation gives the same identity throughout the critical strip and at every regular point outside it. Thus the character theorem gives ζ(s) ≠ 0 for Re s > ½ with s ≠ 1. DLMF, Dirichlet series for a character[279].

Suppose ζ(s) = 0 with s ≠ 1 and s outside the negative even integers. The right-hand case is impossible immediately. For Re s < ½, put w = 1 − s. Since ζ(0) = −½, s ≠ 0 and w ≠ 1. We have Re w > ½, so ζ(w) ≠ 0, and w avoids all nonpositive integers. In the normalization used by this source the functional equation reads

ζ(1−w) = 2(2π)−w Γ(w) cos(πw/2) ζ(w).

The power of 2π is nonzero and Γ has no zero for Re w > 0. If the cosine vanished, w would be an odd integer, so s would be even. Positive even integers contradict Re s < ½, zero has already been excluded, and negative even integers are exactly the excluded trivial zeros. Every factor on the right is therefore nonzero, contradicting ζ(s) = 0. This proves the implication from the nonvanishing input to the exact classical target.

Why can the same argument accommodate ζ(−2) and ζ(0)?

For s = −2 one has w = 3. The gamma and zeta factors on the right are finite and nonzero, but cos(3π/2) = 0. The equation gives ζ(−2) = 0. For s = 0 one has w = 1. Now the cosine vanishes while ζ(w) has a pole, so value-by-value multiplication at w = 1 is unavailable. Writing w = 1 + h, cos(πw/2) = −πh/2 + O(h³) and ζ(w) = 1/h + O(1). Their product tends to −π/2. The remaining multiplier tends to 2(2π)−1Γ(1) = 1/π. This recovers ζ(0) = −½.
