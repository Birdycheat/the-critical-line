1What the Riemann Hypothesis says

A single vertical line is supposed to contain every nontrivial zero of a function built from all the positive integers. Why should estimates for primes have anything to say about a point in the complex plane?

1.1From a series to a function

Write s = σ + it, where σ and t are real and i² = −1. In the half-plane σ > 1 the sum ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 exp(−s log n) converges absolutely, since the absolute values sum to ∑ n−σ. Multiplying the geometric series in p−s over primes lists each integer exactly once by unique factorization. This gives the Euler product. Its logarithm converges there, so its exponential, ζ, is nonzero. The same function extends meromorphically to the complex plane with a simple pole at 1. This continuation is the function whose zeros enter RH. DLMF §25.2[1]

ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 n−s = ∏p prime (1 − p−s)−1   (Re s > 1).

1.2Which zeros?

The zeros at −2, −4, −6, … are called trivial. The remaining zeros lie in the strip 0 < σ < 1. Reflection pairs zeros across the line σ = ½. A pair at σ = 0.4 and σ = 0.6 would respect this symmetry, so symmetry alone leaves the main question open. DLMF §25.10(i)[2]

If ζ(s) = 0, s ≠ 1 and s ∉ {−2, −4, −6, …}, then Re s = ½.

This is Mathlib’s proposition RiemannHypothesis. A Lean function from complex numbers to complex numbers has a value at every input. Ordinary assertions about a meromorphic function at its pole concern a punctured neighborhood. The explicit exception at 1 lets us read the theorem in that ordinary sense. At zero the value ζ(0) = −½ eliminates another possible endpoint. Two public zeta theorems[3]

1.3Why the book follows characters

A Dirichlet character modulo a positive integer N is a multiplicative character on its invertible residue classes, extended by zero on nonunits. Its values on units are roots of unity. Inserting χ(n) in the series for ζ weights each residue class by a phase. The resulting function L(s,χ) agrees with ζ when N = 1. The principal character at a larger modulus drops all integers with a common factor with that modulus. For example at modulus 2 its series is (1 − 2−s)ζ(s) for σ > 1.

The proof first obtains right-half-plane nonvanishing for every χ and every nonzero modulus. Formally, σ > ½ and (χ ≠ 1 or s ≠ 1) imply L(s,χ) ≠ 0. Fixing the modulus-one character gives right-half-plane nonvanishing for ζ. The functional equation deals with the other side. The hard part is producing the prime-sum inequalities that lead to this nonvanishing. Character averages enter those inequalities before the specialization to ζ. Exact scopes[4] · Proof bodies[5]

2Prime powers and the Mellin transform

A prime contributes once to an Euler product, but contributes at every one of its powers to the logarithmic derivative. Follow the contribution from one prime power through a weighted sum and an integral. At the end, the multiplicity of a zero can be read from a single coefficient.

2.1How a prime power enters the sum

Write p for a prime. The von Mangoldt function Λ assigns log p to each positive power of p and zero to every other positive integer, including 1. Thus Λ(8) = log 2, while Λ(6) = 0. All logarithms here are natural. A Dirichlet character χ of modulus N ≥ 1 is a homomorphism from the multiplicative group of invertible residue classes modulo N to the nonzero complex numbers, extended periodically to the integers and set to zero on the nonunits. Its nonzero values are roots of unity because the group is finite. The principal character takes the value 1 on every unit. At modulus one the unique character takes the value 1 on every integer.

For real x ≥ 1, write t+ = max(t, 0). The smoothed sum is

Sχ(x)=∑n=1⌊x⌋Λ(n)χ(n)(xn−1)+(2.1)

The contribution from n begins at zero when x = n. After that point its slope is Λ(n)χ(n)/n. This gives a continuous, piecewise linear function on every bounded interval. The ordinary weighted count of prime powers would jump by Λ(n)χ(n) at n. The slope and the continuity here determine the two factors that will appear in the transform.

At x = 6. For the modulus-one character the active prime powers are 2, 3, 4 and 5. Their respective weights are 2, 1, 1/2 and 1/5. The sum is (5/2) log 2 + log 3 + (1/5) log 5. For the nonprincipal character modulo 3, the values on residues 0, 1 and 2 are 0, 1 and −1. In the same order the four terms now have character factors −1, 0, 1 and −1. The sum becomes −(3/2) log 2 − (1/5) log 5. Characters can remove a contribution, change its sign, or for a complex character rotate it.

2.2The transform of one ramp

Set s = σ + it, where σ and t are real, and define xs = exp(s log x) for x > 0. We use the Mellin convention ℳf(s) = ∫1∞ f(x) x−s−1 dx. With u = log x, the measure dx/x becomes du and x−s becomes exp(−su). The transform therefore studies growth on multiplicative scales as an ordinary Laplace transform in u.

One-ramp identity. Let n ≥ 1 be an integer and σ > 1. Then

∫1∞(xn−1)+x−s−1dx=n−ss(s−1)(2.2)

The integrand vanishes up to n. Above n it is the difference of x−s/n and x−s−1. Both are absolutely integrable since σ > 1. Their integrals over [n, ∞) are n−s/(s − 1) and n−s/s respectively, by evaluating the power antiderivatives at the endpoints. Subtracting gives (2.2). At s = 2 and n = 2, the two areas are 1/4 and 1/8, leaving 1/8.

The half-plane matters. For large x, the absolute value of the integrand is asymptotic to x−σ/n. The integral of its absolute value diverges for σ ≤ 1. The formula on the right continues meromorphically beyond the integration domain. In particular a later evaluation of its continuation in the critical strip does not assert convergence of this integral there.

2.3From one ramp to a logarithmic derivative

For σ > 1 define the Dirichlet L-function by the absolutely convergent series L(s, χ) = ∑n≥1 χ(n)n−s. Expanding geometric series at finitely many primes counts each integer supported on those primes exactly once. Letting the finite set grow gives the Euler product ∏p(1 − χ(p)p−s)−1, by absolute convergence. Its logarithm may be taken as the convergent series ∑p,j≥1 χ(p)jp−js/j, with p running over primes. Exponentiating recovers the product and shows it is nonzero there. NIST DLMF §25.15(i), equations 1–2[9].

Differentiate this logarithm with respect to s. The derivative of p−js supplies −j log p, cancelling j in the denominator. On every closed half-plane σ ≥ 1 + h, h > 0, the sum of absolute derivative bounds is at most ∑n≥2(log n)n−1−h < ∞ by the integral test. The differentiated series converges locally uniformly. Writing Dχ(s) = L′(s, χ)/L(s, χ), we obtain

−Dχ(s)=∑n=1∞Λ(n)χ(n)n−s(2.3)

To sum (2.2) under the integral, replace s by σ there. Since |χ(n)| ≤ 1 and the ramp is nonnegative, the sum of the integrated absolute values is bounded by

1σ(σ−1)∑n=1∞Λ(n)nσ<∞(2.4)

Indeed 0 ≤ Λ(n) ≤ log n for positive n. Fubini's theorem now permits the exchange of sum and integral. Equations (2.2) and (2.3) give the uncentered transform

ℳSχ(s)=−Dχ(s)s(s−1)(2.5)

2.4Why the correction terms have these signs

The raw transform has a factor 1/(s − 1). For ζ the logarithmic derivative has a pole at 1 as well. To detect zeros by their poles, we first remove the poles due to these main terms. This calculation only needs the primitive principal character (modulus one) and nonprincipal characters of positive modulus. A character is primitive if it is not induced from a character of any proper divisor of its modulus. The principal character at a higher modulus is induced from modulus one. The family used below excludes such higher-modulus principal characters.

2.5The modulus-one case

Here L = ζ. Let γ = limk→∞(∑j=1k 1/j − log k), the Euler–Mascheroni constant. Put z = s − 1. The Laurent expansion ζ(1 + z) = 1/z + γ + O(z) gives an analytic function A(z) = zζ(1 + z) across 0 with A(0) = 1 and A′(0) = γ. Thus D1(1 + z) = −1/z + A′(z)/A(z) = −1/z + γ + O(z). Multiplying its negative by 1/[(1 + z)z] gives the principal part 1/z2 − (1 + γ)/z. NIST DLMF §25.2(ii), equations 4–5[12].

Which functions of x have precisely these transforms? For Re z > 0, ∫0∞ exp(−zu) du = 1/z. Integration by parts, with u exp(−zu) vanishing at infinity, gives ∫0∞ u exp(−zu) du = 1/z2. Undo the substitution u = log x. These are the transforms of x and x log x. Hence set

G1(x)=S1(x)−xlogx+(1+γ)x(2.6)

For σ > 1 its transform is −D1(s)/[s(s − 1)] − 1/(s − 1)2 + (1 + γ)/(s − 1). The two negative-power coefficients cancel. The continuation is holomorphic at 1. This calculation uses the Laurent expansion around the pole and never substitutes a numerical value for ζ at its pole.

2.6The nonprincipal case

A nonprincipal character of positive modulus has an entire L-function, and the classical nonvanishing theorem at 1 gives L(1, χ) ≠ 0. Thus cχ = Dχ(1) is finite and Dχ(1 + z) = cχ + O(z). Equation (2.5) has principal part −cχ/z. Adding cχx to the sum cancels it. Analytic continuation[9] and the use of nonvanishing at one[13].

Gχ(x)=Sχ(x)+cχx(2.7)
ℳGχ(s)=scχ−Dχ(s)s(s−1)(2.8)

The numerator in (2.8) vanishes at s = 1. Dividing it by s − 1 leaves a holomorphic function there. This identifies the cancellation even when cχ happens to vanish. Neither centering formula estimates the size of the remaining error at large x.

2.7What a zero leaves in the transform

Let ρ lie in the strip 0 < Re ρ < 1, and suppose L(ρ, χ) = 0. The function is holomorphic near ρ and is not identically zero since its Euler product is nonzero for σ > 1. Its Taylor series has a first nonzero coefficient, of degree m ≥ 1. Factoring it gives L(s, χ) = (s − ρ)mh(s) with h holomorphic and h(ρ) ≠ 0. Differentiation and division give Dχ(s) = m/(s − ρ) + h′(s)/h(s). The last quotient is holomorphic on a sufficiently small disk about ρ.

Only the first term contributes a pole. In both centered formulas its multiplier is −1/[s(s − 1)], which is holomorphic at ρ since ρ differs from 0 and 1. Its value at ρ gives the coefficient of (s − ρ)−1, the residue of the continued transform Φχ.

Ress=ρΦχ=−mρ(ρ−1)(2.9)

A zero of any positive multiplicity therefore gives a simple pole. For ζ the factor s − 1 used to regularize it at 1 is a nonzero analytic factor at ρ, so the multiplicity here is unchanged.

2.8Why taking real parts preserves a pole

The arithmetic estimate later controls a real sum. Let I be a finite index set. For each i in I, let χi be a character of positive modulus Ni with Ni = 1 or χi nonprincipal, and fix a real weight ai > 0. Define GF(x) = ∑i∈I ai Re Gχᵢ(x). For a character χ, its inverse χ⁻¹ on units is its complex conjugate, and both characters vanish on nonunits. Conjugating the Dirichlet series first for σ > 1 gives L(s, χ⁻¹) = conj(L(conj(s), χ)) by analytic continuation. In particular the two derivatives and values at the real point 1 are conjugate for nonprincipal χ. The ramp has real weights. Therefore Gχ⁻¹(x) is the conjugate of Gχ(x), including in the modulus-one case.

Writing Re z = (z + conj(z))/2 before taking the transform gives the holomorphic expression ∑i(ai/2)(Φχᵢ(s) + Φχᵢ⁻¹(s)) away from its poles. Taking the real part of Φχ(s) would instead conjugate the complex integration kernel and lose this identity.

Fix a point ρ in the strip. Let mi and mi− be the orders at this same point of the functions for χi and its inverse, assigning order zero at a nonzero value. Set

B(ρ)=∑i∈Iai2(mi+mi−)(2.10)

The family has residue −B(ρ)/[ρ(ρ − 1)]. At a member of the formal zero set there is a witness i with a zero for χi at ρ or at its conjugate. The latter gives a zero for the inverse character at ρ. At least one order is then positive. Every other contribution is nonnegative in B(ρ), so the residue is nonzero. At real 0 < ρ < 1 it is positive because ρ(ρ − 1) < 0. This is the sign of a Laurent coefficient. The example modulo 3 already shows why signs of arithmetic summands require separate attention.

Strict positivity of the weights alone does not produce a pole. The abstract family may even have no indices. A pole requires the witness in the zero set. In the arithmetic application the retained set includes the modulus-one character. Its weight is 1. In the finite-group coordinates of the comparison, the comparison group is a product E0 × H. A retained character has weight 1 if it is trivial on the entire factor {0} × H, and 1/(3o + 1) otherwise, where o is the level parameter Λ.o. The averaging subgroup A0 can be smaller than H. Triviality on A0 suffices to survive averaging and does not by itself give unit weight. The factor 1/2 in (2.10) has the separate origin Re z = (z + conj(z))/2.

2.9What the next theorem needs

In logarithmic coordinates set g(u) = GF(eu) for u ≥ 0. The packaged theorem Deduction.poleData uses a rough bound |g(u)| ≤ C e2u. To see why such a bound is available, each uncentered sum is bounded by x∑n≤x(log n)/n. Since (log n)/n ≤ 1, this is at most x2. On x ≥ 1 the correction terms are also bounded by a character-dependent constant times x2. Each ramp is continuous, and only finitely many contribute on every bounded interval, so the resulting g is measurable. A finite weighted sum preserves that bound. The corresponding formal proof is in Deduction.Gchi_growth[26].

This bound alone justifies absolute convergence of the Laplace integral in Re s > 2. The formal PoleData interface asks for the integral identity on this smaller half-plane, analyticity away from the zero set in Re s > 0, isolated poles there, and their nonzero residues with positive sign at real poles. The sharper Mellin identities above hold already in Re s > 1. Every point of Re s > 0, including a point outside the zero set, has a punctured neighborhood containing no family zeros. This follows by intersecting the finitely many zero-free punctured neighborhoods for the analytic members, using the regularized function in the modulus-one case. The modulus-one singularity at 1 has been removed explicitly. For the formal set defined using the totalized riemannZeta, the source also uses its assigned nonzero value at 1. Nonvanishing on the boundary[27].

The remaining task is to exclude a pole to the right of a chosen θ with 0 < θ < 1. The window argument accepts an upper bound on selected intervals, with gaps allowed. Precisely, fix δ > 0. For every η > 0 and starting threshold Y0, it needs some Y ≥ Y0 and a function R on [Y, Y1+δ] such that R ≥ 0, GF ≤ R there, and R(y)y−θ−1 is integrable on the interval with integral at most Yη. One can take the threshold at least 1. What this chapter supplies is the transform and its pole data. Obtaining these windows is an arithmetic problem. Showing that the windows force each pole to have real part at most θ takes a further analytic argument. Definition of the required windows[28] and the theorem using them[29].

2.10Two checks on the argument

1. Suppose a weight is changed to (1 − n/x)+. Compute its single-term transform for Re s > 1. Where do the denominator factors move?

Show the calculation

On [n, ∞) subtract n∫ x−s−2 dx from ∫ x−s−1 dx. The result is n−s[1/s − 1/(s + 1)] = n−s/[s(s + 1)]. In fact this calculation works for Re s > 0. Using this weight in (2.2) would change both the denominator and the centering calculation.

2. Suppose χ is real and its L-function has a hypothetical zero of order three at a real ρ with 0 < ρ < 1. For a singleton family with weight 2, what is B(ρ)?

Show the calculation

The inverse equals χ because its nonzero values are real roots of unity. Both multiplicities in (2.10) are 3, so B(ρ) = (2/2)(3 + 3) = 6. The residue is 6/[ρ(1 − ρ)]. The two appearances of χ are compensated by the half in the real-part identity.

3From formal logarithms to divisibility

Differentiating a power series multiplies its coefficients by factorials. At a prime p, those factorials contain powers of p. To use this gain one must know how many powers were lost in changing coordinates.

3.1A height-two change of coordinate

The valuation vp(a) counts the powers of p in a nonzero rational a, allowing negative counts for denominators. Set vp(0) = +∞. The inequality v(a + b) ≥ min(v(a), v(b)) will carry estimates through finite coefficient sums. An integral series has coefficients of nonnegative valuation. In formal power series, questions about composition at zero can be answered degree by degree, without an analytic radius of convergence.

A formal group describes a group law by its power series near the identity. A formal logarithm converts its composition law into addition. Complex multiplication (CM) provides endomorphisms beyond integer multiplication. For a complex elliptic curve written as a quotient of the plane by a lattice, a CM scalar preserves that lattice. In the source the scalar is (1 + i√d)/2. At a suitable inert prime, the logarithm used for comparison has the following sparse shape. Put q = p².

ℓ(T) = ∑j≥0 Tqʲ/pj = T + Tp²/p + Tp⁴/p² + ⋯ .

Formalism.L2.Th1.CM.ltLog defines this series. The proposition CM3 specifies what the comparison has to deliver. It assumes squarefree d ≡ 3 mod 4, CM by this scalar, prime p ≥ 5 with p ∤ d, and no square root of −d modulo p. A good model and a field identification with the completed algebraic closure of ℚp are supplied. The conclusion gives a unit ε in the closed maximal unramified field such that all coefficients of the model’s series uSer composed with εℓ belong to that field and have norm at most one. The scalar and the direction of this composition matter. CM and its local field[30] · Series and good-model assumptions[31]

3.2Proving the one-variable estimate

Here is an independent one-variable derivation of the loss. Take any characteristic-zero valued field extending the p-adic valuation, normalized by v(p) = 1. Since ℓ starts with T, it has a unique compositional inverse B with zero constant term and linear coefficient 1. Its coefficient bn satisfies

v(bn) ≥ −(n − 1)/(q − 1),   n ≥ 1.

Indeed B = T − ∑j≥1 Bqʲ/pj. To form degree n in a power with r = qj factors, choose positive indices summing to n. They are all smaller than n. Induction bounds their product after division by pj from below by −(n−r)/(q−1) − j. This is at least −(n−1)/(q−1) because 1 + q + ⋯ + qj−1 ≥ j. For fixed n only finitely many terms occur, and addition preserves the lower bound.

If A has integral coefficients, the degree-n coefficient of Br loses at most (n−r)/(q−1). For n ≥ 1 the constant term of A contributes nothing, so the same bound with r = 1 applies to A(B). Its n-th derivative at zero is n! times this coefficient. The exact factorial valuation is ∑j≥1⌊n/pj⌋. Each multiple of pj contributes one count at level j. Equivalently the sum is (n−sp(n))/(p−1), by expanding n in base p and summing each digit's geometric series. Since each digit is at most p−1, the deviation from n/(p−1) is at most 1 + logp n.

1/(p−1) − 1/(p²−1) = p/(p²−1) = p⁻¹ + p⁻³ + p⁻⁵ + ⋯ .

3.3What mixed terms cost

In the multivariable estimate the derivative orders form a multi-index κ. The coefficient is multiplied by ∏i κi!. Set γp = p/(p²−1). With r coordinates and all κi ≤ a, a ≥ 1, the lower bound for the normalized valuation has the form

∑i κi(γp − σi) − r(logp a + 1).

The nonnegative corrections σi pay for mixed terms of an exponential factor. Each offending monomial with exponents pα and pβ at coordinates i,j within κ requires pασi + pβσj ≥ 1. Increasing either correction helps attain feasibility and reduces the final gain by κiσi or κjσj. The source first controls the series at radii 1/(p²−1), increases them by the corrections to control the exponential, multiplies the two bounds, and finally uses the factorial inequality. 3.4Choosing correction radii on a cycle

The correction attached to a coordinate has two parts to its construction. First assign one price σs to each row label s. Then adjust individual coordinates at the rows distinguished by the frame. The first problem has an exact optimum. For the adjustment, an explicit choice with a bounded total cost suffices.

Take integers p ≥ 3 and even d ≥ 2. The optimization itself imposes no primality condition on p. Labels belong to ℤ/dℤ. Give each label a real capacity Ds ≥ 0. For each odd integer 1 ≤ j < d, an amount xs,j ≥ 0 sent from s to s + j consumes pj units of capacity at its starting point and one unit at its endpoint per unit sent. Thus the load at s is ∑j (pjxs,j + xs−j,j). Require this load to be at most Ds. Write V for the largest total ∑s,j xs,j. The zero flow is feasible. After setting unused coordinates to zero, every used coordinate lies between 0 and ∑s Ds. The continuous objective therefore attains its maximum on a closed subset of a compact box. Variables, loads and objectives[36] · Truncation and attainment[37]

A feasible price vector has 0 ≤ σs ≤ 1 and pjσs + σs+j ≥ 1 on every edge. Multiply each edge inequality by the amount sent, sum, and translate the index in the incoming sum. This gives ∑s,j xs,j ≤ ∑s σs load(s) ≤ ∑s Dsσs. Equality of the optimal values yields the gain used in the coefficient estimate, with γ = p/(p²−1). The weighted load identity[38]

F(D) = γ ∑s Ds − V = maxfeasible σ ∑s Ds(γ − σs).

To prove equality, let C consist of pairs (b,t) for which a nonnegative flow has load ≤ b in every row and value ≥ t. This is a convex cone. It is closed because for a converging sequence of pairs, their capacities stay bounded. Truncate the witnessing flows, take a convergent subsequence in a common box, and pass the load and value inequalities to the limit. For η > 0, the point (D,V + η) lies outside C. Strict separation gives a linear functional a·b + c t which is nonnegative on the cone and negative at that point. Independent positive capacity increments belong to the cone, so as ≥ 0. Since D ≥ 0 and V + η > 0, negativity forces c < 0. Test a unit flow on each edge. Its load and value give pjas + as+j + c ≥ 0. Dividing by −c supplies prices of cost < V + η. Closedness of the feasible cone[39] · Separating functional and edge tests[40]

Clipping each price at 1 preserves every edge inequality. If either endpoint price was clipped, that endpoint alone now contributes at least 1 since pj ≥ 1. Clipping can only lower the cost because D ≥ 0. The feasible prices form a closed subset of the unit cube containing the all-ones vector, so the minimum exists. It is below V + η for every η > 0 and at least V by the load identity. Finally, every positive odd exponent j reduces modulo the even period d to another odd exponent r < d. The endpoint is unchanged and pjσs ≥ prσs. Thus the same optimizer satisfies the inequalities at all odd levels. For example, let p = 3, d = 2 and D = (1,1). Send ¼ along each of the two edges of length one. Each load is 3/4 + 1/4 = 1. The prices (¼,¼) attain equality on both edges and cost ½, proving V = ½ and F = 2(3/8) − ½ = ¼. If D = (0,1), both flows must vanish because both use the first row. The feasible prices (1,0) have zero cost. The formula still works with a zero capacity.

3.5From a row price to a coefficient bound

In the frame, a coordinate has label s, row index k < N0, and a further index below N1. At each distinguished label s in a set T, the coordinates with k = 0 form its top row. Their total derivative order is ν1(s). The remaining orders sum to Ds, which equals size(s) − ν1(s) on T and size(s) elsewhere. Assume 0 ≤ ν2 ≤ ν1 ≤ size. Define the row cap Cs as ν2(s) on T and ν1(s) elsewhere. A special non-top row carries an origin u ∈ T and a signed odd shift j with s = u + j. The total order assigned the same pair (u,j) is at most Cs. Positive shifts require pj ≤ ν1(u), and negative shifts require p|j| ≤ Ds. Row data and top distances[44] · Exact assembly hypotheses[45]

Let εu denote the correction on the top row at u. An ordinary non-top coordinate at s gets the optimal price σs. A special one gets max(σs, 1 − pjεu) for j > 0, and max(σs, p−|j|) for j < 0. 10Reflection and the final statement

Once every Dirichlet character has a zero-free right half-plane, the passage to classical RH is short. Two small points do most of the work. A change of modulus introduces finite Euler factors, and reflection changes the character.

10.1Removing an imprimitive level

The conductor f of χ modulo N is the least divisor through which its character on units factors. Write ψ for the primitive character at that conductor. Changing to modulus N suppresses Euler factors at primes dividing N. On the common domain of the change-of-level identity, this gives

L(s,χ) = L(s,ψ) ∏p∣N (1 − ψ(p) p−s).

For a prime dividing f, ψ(p) = 0 and the displayed factor equals one. For every other prime its absolute value before subtraction satisfies |ψ(p) p−s| ≤ p−Re s < 1 whenever Re s > 0. Each factor is therefore nonzero. The formula initially follows from Euler products and extends where the continued identity applies. The formal change-of-level lemma asks that ψ be nonprincipal or s ≠ 1. If χ is nonprincipal then ψ must be nonprincipal too, since inducing a principal character would give χ principal. Thus the exception χ = 1 and s = 1 is accounted for. From primitive to all characters[295]

10.2The strip theorem for characters

Assume 0 < Re s < 1 and L(s,χ) = 0. The finite factors transfer the zero to ψ. Its completed function is obtained by multiplying by the real gamma factor appropriate to the parity of ψ. The gamma arguments have positive real part in this strip, so the factors are finite and nonzero. In particular the completed function vanishes too. The functional equation for ψ−1, evaluated at s, gives a zero of the completed function for ψ−1 at 1 − s. On units the inverse character is its complex conjugate. The arguments s and 1 − s avoid 0 and 1. Returning to the uncompleted function gives L(1 − s, ψ−1) = 0.

If Re s < ½, then Re(1 − s) > ½, contradicting right-half-plane nonvanishing for the inverse primitive character. If Re s > ½, nonvanishing for χ already contradicts the original zero. This leaves Re s = ½. It proves the strip statement for arbitrary moduli. Primitive transfer, reflection and strip conclusion[296]

10.3Why classical RH excludes the negative even integers

The public zeta theorem follows a slightly different route. Specialize right-half-plane nonvanishing to the unique character of modulus one. Suppose ζ(s) = 0 with s ≠ 1 and s outside the negative even integers. The right-hand case is impossible immediately. For Re s < ½, put w = 1 − s. Since ζ(0) = −½, s ≠ 0 and w ≠ 1. We have Re w > ½, so ζ(w) ≠ 0, and w avoids all nonpositive integers. In the normalization used by this source the functional equation reads

ζ(1−w) = 2(2π)−w Γ(w) cos(πw/2) ζ(w).

The power of 2π is nonzero and Γ has no zero for Re w > 0. If the cosine vanished, w would be an odd integer, so s would be even. Positive even integers contradict Re s < ½, zero has already been excluded, and negative even integers are exactly the excluded trivial zeros. Every factor on the right is therefore nonzero, contradicting ζ(s) = 0. This proves the implication from the nonvanishing input to the exact classical target.
