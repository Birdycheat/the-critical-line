How does the minus class count control the plus classes?

Let K be a CM field containing a primitive ℓth root of unity ζ, with ℓ an odd prime. Write C = Cl(K) additively, let J be complex conjugation, and put E = (1 − J)C. The tower estimate bounds the number of elements in E[ℓ], where brackets denote the subgroup killed by ℓ. To see which classes still need to be counted, first divide C/ℓC by the image of E. In the resulting vector space Q = C/(ℓC + E), each class coincides with its conjugate. In fact Q identifies with the plus eigenspace of C/ℓC by sending a representative x to (x + Jx)/2. The division takes place in 𝔽ℓ.

Odd-prime reflection. Let C[ℓ]− consist of the classes y with ℓy = 0 and Jy = −y. Then

|Q| ≤ ℓ · |C[ℓ]−|.

Count the linear maps χ from Q to 𝔽ℓ. Choosing their values on a basis shows that there are |Q| of them. By Artin reciprocity each gives a character of the absolute Galois group of K unramified at every finite prime. These characters satisfy χ(c⁻¹gc) = χ(g), where c is any lift of J to the algebraic closure. Indeed the Artin map carries conjugate primes to conjugate Frobenius elements and χ has the same value on a class and its conjugate.

Fix ζ for the rest of the argument. For each character choose a nonzero β satisfying g(β)/β = ζχ(g). For a nonzero χ, let L/K be the cyclic extension fixed by its kernel. A generator acts by a scalar ζχ(g) of norm one, so Hilbert’s Theorem 90 gives such a β in that extension. For the zero character take β = 1. Then a = βℓ lies in K×. At every finite prime the extension is unramified. Its normalized integer valuation therefore restricts to the normalized valuation on K. Evaluating βℓ gives ℓ ∣ ord𝔭(a) at every prime 𝔭. The fractional ideal (a) is consequently the ℓth power of a unique fractional ideal 𝔞.

Send χ to the class [𝔞]. A different choice of β differs by a factor from K×, so 𝔞 changes only by a principal ideal. Products of radicals give sums of characters, making this a homomorphism. Its image is killed by ℓ. To find its sign, apply g to βc(β). The factor from β is ζχ(g). The factor from c(β) is c(ζχ(c⁻¹gc)) = ζ−χ(g), because conjugation inverts ζ. Their product is 1, so d = βc(β) lies in K× and a · J(a) = dℓ. Taking the unique ideal ℓth root gives 𝔞J(𝔞) = (d), hence J[𝔞] = −[𝔞]. This is where a plus character produces a minus ideal class.

Where does the extra factor ℓ come from?

Suppose [𝔞] = 0. Taking a generator b of 𝔞 gives a = u bℓ with u a unit of K. The relation a · J(a) = dℓ implies that u · J(u) is an ℓth power in K. Also u/J(u) is an algebraic integer with absolute value one at every complex embedding. By Kronecker’s theorem it is a root of unity. Thus u² = (u/J(u)) · u · J(u) is represented by a root of unity modulo ℓth powers. Since ℓ is odd, raising this equality to the power (ℓ + 1)/2 shows that u itself has that property.

Finally, the class of a modulo ℓth powers determines χ. If two such classes coincide, the quotient of the two radicals, after division by an element of K×, is an ℓth root of unity and therefore belongs to K. The two characters agree. The kernel thus injects into the image of the roots of unity in K×/(K×)ℓ. The roots of unity in a number field form a finite group. Under a complex embedding they are contained in the cyclic group of roots of Xm − 1, where m is their number, so they form a cyclic group themselves. Its quotient by ℓth powers has at most ℓ elements. Multiplying this kernel bound by the size of the minus subgroup containing the image proves the reflection inequality.

Why is the bound squared in the tower?

If y belongs to C[ℓ]−, then (1 − J)((ℓ + 1)y/2) = (ℓ + 1)y = y. Here (ℓ + 1)/2 is an ordinary integer. Thus each such y belongs to E[ℓ]. The map C/ℓC → Q has kernel E/(E ∩ ℓC). This kernel is a quotient of E/ℓE, which has size |E[ℓ]| by counting the kernel and image of multiplication by ℓ on the finite group E. Hence

|C/ℓC| = |E/(E ∩ ℓC)| · |Q| ≤ ℓ · |E[ℓ]|².

The two copies of |E[ℓ]| have different origins. One counts what was lost in forming the quotient Q. The other bounds the possible images of characters of Q under reflection. For a check of this multiplication, take ℓ = 3 and the abstract group C = ℤ/9 ⊕ (ℤ/3)², with J negating the first coordinate and fixing the other two. Here E is the entire first factor, |E[3]| = 3, and |Q| = 9. Multiplication by 3 on C has image of size 3, so |C/3C| = 81/3 = 27, exactly 3 · 3². For n ≥ 1 in the cyclotomic tower, the layer K = ℚ(μfℓⁿ) contains the primitive ℓth root ζfℓⁿfℓⁿ⁻¹. The uniform bound |E[ℓ]| ≤ BFN now gives ℓBF2N.
