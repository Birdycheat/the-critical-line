What does a zero force a finite sum to do?

Fix a modulus m ≥ 3 and a height T ≥ 2. We count pairs (χ,ρ) with χ a character modulo m, L(ρ,χ) = 0, σ ≤ Re ρ < 1 and |Im ρ| ≤ T. Assume ¾ ≤ σ < 1. Two distinct zeros of the same character in this list must have ordinates at least one apart. The strip argument explains how to put the multiplicities back afterwards. The problem here is to bound the number of these separated pairs.

Take q = ⌈mT⌉, V = q³, J = 8(⌊log₂ q⌋ + 1), and N = 2J. Thus q⁸ ≤ N ≤ 256q⁸. For a character χ let PN(s) = ∑n≤N χ(n)n−s. Multiply it by MV(s) = ∑d≤V μ(d)χ(d)d−s. Here μ is the Möbius function. It is zero on integers divisible by a prime square and equals (−1)k on a product of k distinct primes.

In the product the coefficient of χ(n)n−s is bN(n) = ∑dk=n, d≤V, k≤N μ(d). This equals 1 at n = 1. For 1 < n ≤ V every divisor occurs, and their Möbius sum is ∏p∣n(1 − 1) = 0. The product is consequently 1 + DN, where the remainder runs over V < n ≤ VN. Partition it into J sums on doubling intervals. With U = V2j their common form is

BN,U(χ,ρ) = ∑U<n≤2U bN(n)χ(n)n−ρ.

Nonprincipal detector. At a zero of a nonprincipal character, |PN(ρ)| ≤ 8qN−Re ρ. Indeed, complete periods of χ sum to zero, so partial sums on every interval are bounded by 2(m + 1). Partial summation of the tail of the L-series bounds it by 2(m + 1)(1 + |ρ|/Re ρ)N−Re ρ, and 1 + |ρ|/Re ρ ≤ 3T. These bounded partial sums give locally uniform convergence on Re s > 0, so the series there agrees with the continued L-function. Since L(ρ,χ) vanishes, the finite sum equals the negative of the tail. Also |MV(ρ)| ≤ V. Thus |1 + DN| ≤ 8q · q−6 · q³ = 8/q² ≤ ½. The remainder must have absolute value at least ½. At least one of its J blocks therefore has absolute value ≥ 1/(2J).

Check the cancellation before grouping the blocks

Use small cutoffs V = 3 and N = 4 just to check the coefficients. At n = 2 and 3 they are 1 − 1 = 0. At n = 6 the admissible pairs (d,k) are (2,3) and (3,2). Each contributes −1, so the coefficient is −2. The cutoff has removed the pairs (1,6) and (6,1) that would complete the divisor sum. These surviving coefficients are exactly what the detector measures.

How is the principal character detected?

A zero of the principal character in Re ρ > 0 is a zero of ζ, since the removed Euler factors 1 − p−ρ are nonzero there. In this paragraph use the character of modulus one in P, M and D. The alternating sum up to 2N is P2N(ρ) − zPN(ρ) with z = 21−ρ. Its infinite sum is (1 − z)ζ(ρ) = 0. At s = 1 the factor 1 − 21−s cancels the simple pole of ζ and the alternating sum is log 2. The zeros used in the detector satisfy Re ρ < 1. Alternating signs have bounded partial sums, giving the tail bound 4(1 + |ρ|/Re ρ)N−Re ρ. Multiplying by the same Möbius sum as before gives

1 − z = (P2N − zPN)MV − D2N + zDN.

The first term on the right has absolute value at most 12/q². A principal zero cannot be the exceptional zero in the Dirichlet zero-free region. Hence 1 − Re ρ > c₀/(2 log q) for its fixed constant c₀ > 0, using m(|Im ρ| + 2) ≤ q². By the reverse triangle inequality, |1 − z| ≥ 21−Re ρ − 1 ≥ (1 − Re ρ)/2. For sufficiently large q we also have 12/q² ≤ c₀/(8 log q). Since |z| ≤ 2, at least one of |DN| and |D2N| exceeds c₀/(24 log q). Breaking that remainder into at most J + 1 blocks detects the zero at threshold c₀/[24(J + 1) log q]. This extra log q is the price of the principal character.

How many separated zeros can one block detect?

Fix a block (U,2U] and its truncation N or 2N. Let R be the number of pairs it detects at threshold θ. Split each coefficient vector into a common vector b(n) = bN′(n)n−σ and a row with entries χ(n)n−(ρ−σ), where N′ is this block’s truncation. Write LU = 1 + log(2U). The squared norm of the common vector is at most A = 2U1−2σLU³. In fact |bN′(n)| is at most the number of divisors of n. To sum the squares of these divisor counts up to x, count pairs of divisors a,b of the same multiple. There are at most x/lcm(a,b) such multiples. Writing a = gu, b = gv with gcd(u,v) = 1 and bounding each harmonic sum by 1 + log x gives a total at most x(1 + log x)³.

Form the Gram matrix of the rows. In a row belonging to χ, pairing with a different character gives a nonprincipal character sum with bounded partial sums. Its absolute contribution per pair is at most K = 8(m + 1)(2 + 2T) by partial summation. For two rows with the same χ, the product character is principal. Summing its mean by integration gives an additional contribution of at most 4U/|Im ρ − Im ρ′| for unequal ordinates. This is where separation matters. On either side of one ordinate, the kth nearest ordinate is at distance at least k. The reciprocal distances sum to at most 2(1 + log(2T + 1)). For example, for ordinates −2, 0, 1, 3 of one character, the reciprocal distances from 0 sum to ½ + 1 + ⅓ = 11/6. A second character may also have an ordinate at 0 because its pairing uses the nonprincipal character sum. The diagonal is bounded by U. Including all errors, the sum of the absolute values in each row is at most UG + RK with G = 9 + 8 log(2T + 1).

A Hermitian matrix has operator norm bounded by its largest absolute row sum, as follows by applying 2|xy| ≤ |x|² + |y|² to each term of its quadratic form. Applied to this Gram matrix, the squared block values sum to at most A(UG + RK). Each of the R selected values has squared absolute value at least θ². For sufficiently large q, AK ≤ θ²/2. Indeed, U ≥ q³ and σ ≥ ¾ give U1−2σ ≤ q−3/2, while K is at most a constant times q. The resulting q−1/2 pays for all logarithms, including those in θ. Moving the term ARK to the left gives

Rθ² ≤ A(UG + RK),   R ≤ 4U2−2σLU³G/θ².

For sufficiently large q every block used here has 2U ≤ 2VN ≤ 512q11 ≤ q12. Hence U2−2σ ≤ q24(1−σ). To count all the zeros, sum the bound over the possible blocks. There are at most a constant times log q. This last sum costs one logarithm, LU³ costs three, and G costs one. The inverse squared threshold costs two more logarithms for nonprincipal characters and four for the principal one. Their totals are seven and nine. Since mT ≤ q ≤ 2mT, both contributions are bounded by a fixed constant times (mT)24(1−σ)(log(mT))10. For ½ ≤ σ < ¾, the simple bound m(2T + 1) already fits under this estimate, and a larger constant covers bounded q. This proves the fixed-modulus density input used to count the zero strips.
