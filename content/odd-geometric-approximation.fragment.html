Why impose large gaps on one auxiliary weight?

Fix an odd positive rank m. To approximate a Hecke system by one arising from geometry, the construction allows the weight to change at two auxiliary places u and v above ℓ. Their primes in the maximal real subfield E⁺ are distinct. At one embedding inducing v, the new dominant weight is a nonincreasing list λ₀, …, λm−1. Its desired filtration jumps are the integers jk = k − λk, with indexing from zero. Consecutive jumps differ by at least one. The additional separation imposed by the approximation theorem is

jk+1 − j₀ > m(jk − j₀)   (0 ≤ k < m−1).

This inequality ensures that any two disjoint nonempty subsets of the jumps have different arithmetic means. The rank-extraction theorem below uses precisely this property, called lacunarity. To prove it, subtract the smallest jump from every jump. This translates both means equally. Let M be the largest shifted jump in the union of the two sets and call the set containing it A. For every entry b of the other set B, the gap inequality gives mb < M. The other entries of A are nonnegative, so the mean of A is at least M/|A| ≥ M/m. Every entry of B is smaller than that lower bound.

A rank-three weight. The weight (0,0,−2) has jumps {0,1,4}. The largest jump divided by three is 4/3, already greater than the next jump 1. For the unshifted weight (0,0,0) the jumps {0,1,2} satisfy mean{0,2} = mean{1}. At the conjugate embedding the dual weight has coordinates −λm−1−k. Its jumps are the reflected set (m−1)−J, here {−2,1,2}. Reflection sends every mean b to (m−1)−b, preserving unequal means. This explains why the construction may use either prime of E above the auxiliary real-subfield prime.

Which part of cohomology carries the auxiliary Hecke system?

Let U′ denote one of the auxiliary automorphic representations. Fix an algebraic closure Ω of the CM field E and write Γ = Gal(Ω/E). The geometric input supplies a smooth projective variety with finite étale correspondences over E, the eigenvalue θh prescribed for each correspondence h, and an algebraic Hecke character κ. The existence of these data with the required automorphic eigenvalues and local comparisons uses a holomorphic Lefschetz formula. Start with the sum of the étale cohomology groups of the geometric fibre, with coefficients extended to an algebraic closure of ℚℓ. Only finitely many degrees contribute, their dimensions are finite, and Γ acts continuously. Milne, Lectures on Étale Cohomology, Theorems 15.1 and 19.2. Brinon and Conrad, Example 1.2.3.

The holomorphic Lefschetz input has a concrete local term. Over ℂ let c₁ and c₂ be the finite étale maps of the correspondence to a smooth projective variety Z. Suppose they agree at only finitely many points y. At each one, use c₁ as local coordinates, let Ay be the derivative of the resulting local map c₂ ∘ c₁−1, and assume det(I−Ay) ≠ 0. For each holomorphic degree a, the theorem equates the alternating sum over b of the traces of c1∗c₂∗ on Hb(Z, Ωa) with the sum of the fixed-point contributions. At y the contribution is the coefficient of ta in det(I+tAy) divided by det(I−Ay). On a complex elliptic curve take c₁ to be the identity and c₂ multiplication by two. Only the origin is fixed. Its derivative is 2, so the two contributions are −1 and −2 for a = 0 and 1. This illustrates the Hodge trace calculation required in the construction of the geometric data. Atiyah and Bott, A Lefschetz fixed point formula for elliptic complexes II, the holomorphic fixed-point formula for a map.

On the étale cohomology space H, take the simultaneous eigenspace of the correspondence operators Th. Pullback and trace along a finite étale correspondence defined over E commute with the Galois action, since acting on the geometric fibre carries each of the two correspondence maps to itself and permutes the points in each trace. Consequently, Th(g x) = g Th(x) = θhg x whenever x has the prescribed eigenvalues.

Hθ = ⋂h ker(Th − θhI),   g Hθ = Hθ   (g ∈ Γ).

Make this representation semisimple as follows. Starting at zero, choose an invariant space of smallest possible dimension strictly larger than the previous space and containing it. Dimension increases at each step, so the process reaches Hθ. The induced representation on each successive quotient is irreducible, since an intermediate invariant space would contradict minimality. In a basis extending the flag one step at a time, the matrices are block upper triangular. Taking their diagonal blocks respects multiplication and inversion, so the direct sum of the quotient actions is a representation. Its entries are entries of the original matrices in a fixed basis and are continuous. The determinant of a block triangular matrix is the product of the determinants of its diagonal blocks. Thus every group element has the same characteristic polynomial before and after passing to the quotients.

For a two-dimensional example inside a three-dimensional space, take the operator diag(3,3,7). Its eigenvalue 3 selects the first two coordinates. Let g ∈ ℤℓ act there by the matrix with diagonal (1,1), entry g above the diagonal and zero below. On the third coordinate let it act trivially. Both the first coordinate line and the quotient of the selected plane by that line are trivial representations. The associated graded action on the plane is therefore the identity for every g. Before and after, the characteristic polynomial on that plane is (X−1)².

A rank-one twist restores the normalization used by U′. Fix a spherical good prime w away from ℓ and outside the finite exceptional set for this realization. Put R = (Nw)(m−1)/2Bw, where Bw is the Satake multiset. The local geometric comparison gives a polynomial with roots R/κ(w) that annihilates inverse Frobenius on the semisimple piece. The ℓ-adic character attached to κ multiplies that matrix by κ(w) under the chosen coefficient identification. For a monic polynomial P of degree m, set Q(X) = cmP(X/c). Then Q(cM) = cmP(M) = 0, and the roots of Q are those of P multiplied by c. At an embedding τ, a geometric jump prescribed at j+tτ moves to j, since the character line has jump −tτ and tensoring with a filtered line adds its jump. As a numerical check on both normalizations, roots {1,3/2,5/2} with κ(w) = 2 become {2,3,5}. Raw jumps {3,4,7} with tτ = 3 become {0,1,4}. The dimension D of the resulting representation ρ is still the dimension of the cohomological piece. Recovering rank m takes one more theorem.

How can a cohomological piece of unknown dimension determine rank m?

Rank-extraction theorem used here. Let ρ be a continuous semisimple geometric representation of Γ of dimension D. At some place over ℓ and some inducing embedding, its filtration jumps contain a lacunary set of m distinct integers. Let ψ be a continuous character. Suppose that for a dense set of g ∈ Γ there is a monic degree-m polynomial Q with Q(ρ(g)) = 0 and (−1)mQ(0) = ψ(g). Then there exists a continuous semisimple representation r of dimension m, with injective equivariant maps in the following two directions, for some integer k. The set of g where r(g) has m distinct eigenvalues is open and dense. The derivation below passes from the local jumps to a matrix in the Lie algebra of the global image, then from its eigenspaces to the two injections. The argument in each open coset supplies the simple-spectrum condition. The step extending the block structure to the entire group is stated with its hypotheses.

r ↪ ρ,   ρ ↪ r⊕k.

Here the dense set consists of inverses of arithmetic Frobenius lifts at eligible spherical primes, after finite exclusions. The geometric comparison supplies Q(X) = ∏α ∈ R(X−α), transported to the ℓ-adic field. The determinant character attached to the central character with the Satake normalization takes value ∏α there. Thus the constant-term hypothesis holds, including its sign. The auxiliary weight and the Hodge comparison give the required set of jumps. These facts verify the hypotheses for the cohomological piece.

The first injection passes annihilation to r. Indeed, for the injection A one has AQ(r(g)) = Q(ρ(g))A = 0. It also passes trivial inertia to r. At a tested element of simple spectrum, each of the m distinct eigenvalues of r(g) is a root of Q, by applying the annihilation identity to an eigenvector. Since Q is monic of degree m, it equals the characteristic polynomial there. On that same dense set the determinant of r agrees with ψ, hence it agrees everywhere by continuity.

Different prescribed places may require different geometric pieces. Extract r once from one piece and r′ from another. The two open dense simple-spectrum conditions can be imposed together. In every nonempty open subset of Γ, intersect with those two open dense sets and then choose an inverse Frobenius outside the union of the two finite exceptional sets. Both characteristic polynomials there equal the same Satake polynomial, since both pieces concern the same U′. Continuity gives equality everywhere, and Brauer–Nesbitt identifies the two semisimple representations. Thus the injections for the new piece can be written using the original r. The trace argument for Brauer–Nesbitt.

How do the two injections recover the exact data at a prescribed prime?

Consider first a prescribed spherical good prime w away from ℓ. Choose a geometric piece with the annihilation identity at this very prime. For every inverse Frobenius lift there the first injection gives a matrix of size m annihilated by the polynomial with roots R. Its multiset of eigenvalues has m entries, all from R, and its determinant gives the product of the entries. To recover all the multiplicities we use the genericity test imposed at the prescribed prime. For a list α₁, …, αm of its normalized Satake entries it gives

∑ ei = m,   ∏ αiei = ∏ αi   ⇒   e₁ = ⋯ = em = 1.

Indeed, each nonnegative ei is at most m, so there are finitely many exponent lists to test. The genericity polynomial on the unnormalized Satake entries contains a factor given by the difference of these two products for each list other than the all-ones list. A nonzero value excludes every such equality. The common Satake normalization multiplies both products by the same nonzero scalar to the power m. Apply the test to the multiplicities of the eigenvalues to obtain the prescribed characteristic polynomial. For roots {2,3,5}, the determinant 30 forces each prime factor to appear once. With roots {1,2,4}, both diag(1,2,4) and 2I have determinant 8 and are annihilated by (X−1)(X−2)(X−4). Their traces are 7 and 6. This is the collision that the genericity test removes.

At a prescribed spherical w over ℓ outside the two auxiliary real-subfield primes, take a piece that is crystalline there and whose jumps contain the desired m distinct integers. The first injection makes r crystalline at w. Indeed it is a direct summand of the globally semisimple ρ, and each summand of a crystalline representation is crystalline. For the jumps, use the second injection. The space of k copies of r is semisimple as a global representation, so the injection of ρ into it also splits. After restriction to the local group, taking filtered periods preserves these direct sums. Every jump occurring in ρ must therefore occur in r, since the k copies only repeat its jumps. At each inducing embedding the period space of r has dimension m. The m prescribed distinct jumps already use that entire dimension, so each occurs exactly once. Brinon and Conrad, Theorem 5.2.1 and §6.3 on filtered de Rham periods.

A further geometric piece can supply the Weil polynomial at the same w. For a Weil element of degree −1, the characteristic polynomial on this piece divides a power of the Satake polynomial. The inclusion of r implies the same divisibility for its Weil polynomial. Thus every root belongs to R. Compatibility of the Weil–Deligne construction with determinants gives the prescribed product, and the same genericity test fixes the multiplicities. Both Weil representations are unramified here, the first by crystallinity and the second by its spherical construction. Each factors through the integer degree map. The eigenvalues at an arbitrary degree are the corresponding powers of those at degree −1, giving equality of the characteristic polynomials at every Weil element.

The geometric input requires the two auxiliary primes to agree on the imaginary quadratic subfield K = ℚ(√(−d)). For the local comparison at w above ℓ choose each auxiliary prime, or its conjugate, to restrict to w|K. There are two primes of K above the split prime ℓ and complex conjugation exchanges them. This choice preserves the underlying primes of E⁺, sphericity, and the cuspidal local type (changing prime identifies the two local unitary groups and transports their compact subgroups). Duality preserves lacunarity of the weight. The preceding uniqueness argument places the comparisons from these choices on one representation attached to U′. The next step needs simultaneous congruences of the eigenvalues to approximate the original system using such representations.
