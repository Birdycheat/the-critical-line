How a determinant bounds one coordinate

A vector can have small energy and large coordinates if the form measuring its energy is nearly singular. The determinant measures the loss we must allow here. Let G be a positive semidefinite Hermitian N × N matrix with every diagonal entry at most 1. If a complex column c has energy c*Gc ≤ 1, then for each coordinate a,

|det G| · |ca| ≤ N! ≤ NN.

Write ⟨x,y⟩ = x*Gy. Positivity gives |⟨x,y⟩|² ≤ ⟨x,x⟩⟨y,y⟩. Indeed, subtracting ⟨x,y⟩x/⟨x,x⟩ from y leaves a vector of nonnegative energy. If x has zero energy, the energy of y + tx can stay nonnegative for all complex t only if ⟨x,y⟩ = 0. Apply the inequality first to two coordinate vectors and then to a coordinate vector and c. It gives |Gij| ≤ 1 and |(Gc)i| ≤ 1.

Replace column a of G by Gc. This new column is the sum of the original columns weighted by the coordinates of c. Expand the determinant in that column. Every term except the ath has a repeated column, so the determinant equals ca det G. Each entry of the new matrix has absolute value at most 1. Its determinant is a signed sum of N! products, each of absolute value at most 1. This proves the bound even when G is singular. To bound |ca| itself we now need a positive lower bound for the determinant. Gilbert Strang, MIT 18.06, Lecture 20, Cramer’s rule.

Why the integral coordinates lie in one box

Fix a nonsingular integral frame A over the totally complex field F. A vector y is nearby if one feasible family of forms h makes both y and every column of A minimal. Each y may have its own family. Set r = |NormF/ℚ(det A)| and use the integral matrix B = rA−1. For n = [F : ℚ], d = nN, D = |disc F| and J = DNdd, every such y satisfies

|σw((By)a)| ≤ R,   R = NNJ²,

for every coordinate a and one embedding σw in each complex conjugate pair. Its conjugate gives the same absolute value. To see the bound, let Hw be the local matrix of the chosen family and put Aw = σw(A), Gw = Aw*HwAw. The diagonal entries of Gw are nonnegative. For each column their sum over the places is the energy of that column, exactly 1. Thus each diagonal entry is at most 1. The frame estimate, using the discriminant bound, gives both 1 ≤ r ≤ J and det Gw ≥ 1/J at every place.

Let cw = σw(By)/r. Since AB = rI, multiplying cw by Aw recovers σw(y). The resulting local energies cw*Gwcw are nonnegative and sum to the energy of y, exactly 1. Apply the preceding coordinate estimate at each place. Dividing by det Gw costs at most one factor J. Multiplying by r to recover the integral coordinate costs at most another. This gives the stated R. The map y ↦ By is injective, because multiplying its output by A recovers ry. The count of nearby vectors can therefore count integral columns in this fixed box.

A minimal Gaussian vector with a denominator in frame coordinates

Over ℤ[i] take the form q(u,v) = |u + (1 + i)v/2|² + |v|²/2. Every nonzero integral pair has energy at least 1. If v = 0 this follows from |u| ≥ 1. If |v|² ≥ 2 the second term suffices. For a Gaussian unit v, the real and imaginary parts of (1 + i)v/2 are both half-integers, so the first term is at least 1/2.

Take frame columns (1,0) and (−i,1 + i). They are orthogonal for this form and each has energy 1, so their Gram matrix is the identity. Their determinant is 1 + i and r = 2. The vector y = (0,1) also has energy 1. Solving Ac = y gives c = ((1 + i)/2,(1 − i)/2), of squared length 1. Multiplication by r gives the integral column By = (1 + i,1 − i). Multiplying that column by A gives (0,2), as required.
