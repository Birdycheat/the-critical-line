Which directions have positive mixed Casimir?

Fix the compact type V of highest weight L = μ+ρn−ρc in U(r,s). Put n = r+s. The coordinates of μ are strictly decreasing, and any two of opposite signs differ by at least h > nrs+1. A derivative in one noncompact position has a matrix index and a coefficient in V. The mixed Casimir measures the interaction of their two compact actions. We need its sign when one position of a longer word varies and every other index stays fixed.

The highest-weight calculation for one position puts each irreducible summand at weight L+ea−eb, with a,b of opposite signs. The Casimir formula gives the mixed eigenvalue θab = μa−μb+(ρn)a−(ρn)b+1−n/2. Throughout this calculation, the order of the two indices refers to their order in μ.

Suppose a < b. Let Ua count opposite-sign indices after a and Db those before b. Both counts are at least one because each includes the other endpoint. The total number of indices of sign opposite to a plus the analogous number for b is exactly n. Subtracting the two half-sum coordinates therefore gives (ρn)a−(ρn)b = Ua+Db−n/2 ≥ 2−n/2. Inserting this lower bound in the two possible orders yields

θab ≥ h+3−n ≥ h−n+1,   θba ≤ −h−1.

When both signs occur, rs ≥ 1 and the first bound is positive. If one sign is absent there are no such directions. The two sums of irreducible summands are orthogonal by compact unitarity. Thus on the positive sum the mixed expectation is at least (h−n+1) times the squared norm. In the U(1,1) example, the gap is 40. The two directions have eigenvalues 41 and −41, and the positive bound used in the layer estimate is 39.

What happens when a negative direction acts on the compact type?

The evaluation map Φ sends an array ∑ Eab ⊗ vab to the vector ∑ Eabvab in the representation. At this step assume that the full Casimir acts on V by c = ‖μ‖²−‖ρc+ρn‖². Centrality carries the same scalar to all derivative words. We show that Φ sends the whole negative sum to zero.

Evaluation commutes with the compact action by the product rule. Hence an irreducible summand with nonzero image would supply an isomorphic compact type in the image. By the sign calculation, a negative summand would give a nonzero compact highest vector of weight γ = L−α for α = ea−eb, a < b of opposite signs. Pair the full Casimir with this vector w. A same-sign pair contributes (γi−γj)‖w‖² for i < j because its raising derivative kills w. For an opposite-sign pair, use [Eij,Eji] = Eii−Ejj and Eij* = −Eji. The contribution becomes −(γi−γj)‖w‖²−2‖Ejiw‖². Add the diagonal contribution ‖γ‖²‖w‖² and discard the final negative squares. Dividing by ‖w‖² gives

c ≤ ‖γ+ρc−ρn‖²−‖ρc−ρn‖².

For γ = L−α the right side minus c is 2−2(μa−μb)+4⟨ρc,ρn⟩. To bound the last term, twice the absolute value of each compact half-sum coordinate is at most n. Twice the absolute value of each noncompact half-sum coordinate is at most its number of opposite-sign indices. Those numbers sum to 2rs, counting each opposite-sign pair twice. It follows that 2⟨ρc,ρn⟩ ≤ nrs. The difference is at most 2(1−h+nrs) < 0. This contradicts the ceiling and proves the one-position vanishing.

For a word of length k ≥ 1, suppose every slice at a specified position belongs to the negative sum. If that position acts directly on the coefficient, fixing the other letters factors the evaluation through Φ, so it vanishes. To bring any specified position next to the coefficient, interchange adjacent letters. Each error is a commutator, a linear combination of single matrix units replacing two letters. It therefore belongs to Fk−1. This proves that the original evaluation is zero in Fk/Fk−1, exactly the quotient tested in the highest-weight argument.

In U(1,1) the hypothetical lower weight is (20,−19). The ceiling would bound the full scalar 800 by 20²+19²−39 = 722. In the two-letter word E₂₁E₁₂e₀ the lowering letter acts last in time. Reversing the order gives zero because E₂₁e₀ = 0. The original word equals the commutator on e₀, namely −41e₀. It vanishes in the new quotient while retaining a nonzero value in the original type.

How quickly can repeated noncompact derivatives grow?

Translation by exp(Y) will be recovered from the derivatives in direction Y. For this argument V may be any finite-dimensional compact-stable space of smooth vectors with a common real full Casimir scalar c. Let Fk be its layers of derivative words. Choose R ≥ 0 bounding ‖ν+ρc‖ for the finitely many diagonal weights ν of V. A noncompact matrix unit shifts a weight by ea−eb, of length √2. Acting on a weight basis of V therefore spans Fk by vectors whose shifted weights have norm at most R+√2k.

Every compact highest weight in this finite layer satisfies the same bound. Split the layer orthogonally into irreducible compact types and apply the compact Casimir formula on each one. For x in the layer, the compact Casimir expectation is at most ((R+√2k)²−‖ρc‖²)‖x‖². Subtract the full expectation c‖x‖². The compact terms cancel, and every opposite-sign pair has adjoint sign −1, giving a sum of squared norms. With d = ‖ρc‖²+c, this proves

∑εa ≠ εb ‖Eabx‖² ≤ ((R+√2k)²−d)‖x‖².

Let Y be a Hermitian off-block matrix in the real Lie algebra, with Frobenius norm S = (∑|Yab|²)1/2. Write T = ∑ YabEab on smooth vectors. The triangle and Cauchy–Schwarz inequalities bound ‖Tx‖ by S times the square root of the displayed energy. Put A = R+√max(0,−d). The square root is at most (A+√2k)‖x‖, as one sees by squaring. Since T sends Fk into Fk+1, iteration gives, for v ∈ Fk,

‖Tmv‖ ≤ Sm ‖v‖ ∏0 ≤ j < m (A+√2(k+j)).

For the unit vector e₀ of weight (21,−20) take R = 29. At depth zero the energy bound is 29²−800 = 41. Directly, only E₁₂ contributes, with squared norm 41. The growth in later layers enters one factor at a time in the product.

How do local Taylor expansions control an entire group translate?

Let W be the Hilbert closure of the union of the derivative layers. Write U(t) = τ(exp(tY)) for the unitary one-parameter group in the direction above. The desired assertion is U(1)W = W. The argument uses one step size for vectors from every layer.

Fix v ∈ Fk. Differentiating U(t+δ)v = U(t)U(δ)v at δ = 0 gives U(t)Tv. Repetition gives the mth derivative U(t)Tmv, of constant norm ‖Tmv‖ by unitarity. To bound the Taylor remainder after degree m−1, pair it with its own unit direction using the real part of the Hilbert inner product. The scalar Taylor remainder bound gives the vector remainder norm at most |δ|m‖Tmv‖/m!. A zero remainder already satisfies the bound. MIT 18.014, Chapter O, Theorem 2, Taylor’s formula with Lagrange remainder.

Multiply the product bound by |δ|m/m!. Each majorant is the preceding one times |δ|S(A+√2(k+m−1))/m, which tends to |δ|S√2. When this is below one the majorants eventually decrease at least geometrically, even if the first several grow. The remainder tends to zero. The limit of the multiplier is independent of the initial layer k.

U(t+δ)v = ∑m ≥ 0 (δm/m!) U(t)Tmv,   |δ|√2S < 1.

Choose an integer N > √2S and set δ = 1/N. At time zero all algebraic vectors belong to W. Suppose that at time j/N every algebraic vector has its translate in W. In the Taylor series for the next time each coefficient is such a translate, because every Tmv belongs to a finite layer. Finite sums and their norm limit belong to the closed subspace W. Induction reaches time one. Continuity extends the inclusion to all of W. Applying the same argument to −Y gives equality.

In U(1,1) take Y with both off-diagonal entries one and diagonal zero. Then S = √2 and N = 3 works. Each step has limiting multiplier 2/3.

For a general group element, one can find the required direction directly. Let J be the diagonal signature matrix and set P = g*g. The defining equation g*Jg = J gives Jg*J = g−1, hence JPJ = P−1. Diagonalize the positive definite matrix P and put Y = (log P)/2, taking real logarithms of its eigenvalues. Conjugating by J exchanges P and its inverse, so JYJ = −Y. Thus Y is Hermitian with zero same-sign blocks. Its exponential preserves J because exp(Y)J = J exp(−Y). Now k = g exp(−Y) preserves J and satisfies k*k = exp(−Y)P exp(−Y) = I. It follows that k commutes with J and lies in the block compact group. This proves the polar decomposition g = k exp(Y). Compact translation preserves each derivative layer because it conjugates off-block matrices to off-block matrices and preserves V. Thus τ(g) preserves W, supplying the last step in the translated orthogonality argument.
