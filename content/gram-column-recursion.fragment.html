What remains after the first vector is fixed?

Suppose the ambient Hermitian space represents the first target length t ∈ F×. Split off a rational vector of that length. In the resulting coordinates the ambient form is ⟨t⟩ ⊕ J, with J a nonsingular form of dimension r. The prescribed Gram matrix is ⟨t⟩ ⊕ T, with T nonsingular of size n. Thus there are n columns still to choose. Work at a completion R of F with K = R ⊗F E. Use coordinate Haar measures throughout, from one additive Haar measure on R and one F-basis of E.

Let e be the first coordinate vector. A first column y with length t can be written hye for a unitary isometry hy. When K is a field, Witt’s extension theorem extends the isometry between the two lines of length t. At a split place a vector is a pair consisting of a vector u and a linear functional ℓ with ℓ(u) = t. Complete u to a basis with the other vectors in ker ℓ. A change between two such bases carries both the vector and, by its inverse dual, the functional. This gives the required isometry there as well. The remaining columns are orthogonal to y. Applying hy−1 puts these columns into the fixed complement. There they have the form (0,Xⱼ), where the smaller tuple X has Gram matrix T for J. Write ι(X) for the tuple with first column e and remaining columns (0,Xⱼ).

The set of tuples is now described, but the conditional measure still needs its normalization. For a nonnegative measurable test φ, let mt1 be the one-column measure in the full space, and mT⊥ the n-column measure in the complement. The local recursion states

∫ φ dm⟨t⟩⊕T = cv(t)n ∫ (∫ φ(hy ι(X)) dmT⊥(X)) dmt1(y).

The inner integral is independent of the isometry because two choices differ on the right by an isometry fixing e. Its restriction is unitary on the complement and preserves mT⊥. The one-vector orbit is the quotient of the locally compact second-countable unitary group by the closed stabilizer of e. The measurable-section theorem for this quotient supplies a measurable choice y ↦ hy and hence a measurable outer integrand.

Where does the factor for each remaining column come from?

Allow the target to move, so that we can use the defining integration formula for the regular fibre measures. Write its upper-left entry as x, its first row after that entry as b, and its lower-right Hermitian block as B. Near x = t the local square-root branch gives x = a²t, with a = 1 at the centre. In the frame carried by hy the first vector is ae, and each remaining vector is (cⱼ,Xⱼ). Computing their inner products gives

b = at c,   GramJ(X) = B − t c*c = B − b*b/x,   det S = x det(B − b*b/x).

Here S is the full moving target and * is conjugate transpose. The determinant identity follows by subtracting the first row times the appropriate multiple from each lower row. Thus, as long as x is invertible, the smaller Gram matrix is nonsingular exactly when S is. For fixed x and b, passing between the two lower-right blocks is a translation, which preserves their additive Haar measure.

The first row changes volume. Normalize |·|v by the rule that multiplication by u scales the volume of a set in R by |u|v. A coordinate of K has two R-coordinates, so pushing its measure forward by z ↦ at z multiplies the measure by |at|v−2. Indeed, the mass assigned to a target set is the volume of its inverse image. There is one such factor for each of the n entries of b. The first-vector measure also varies with a. Under y ↦ ay, source volumes scale by |a|v2(r+1) and its scalar length by |a|v². Hence the measure at x is |a|v2r times the image of the measure at t.

Integrating first over the column coordinates, or first over the entries of the moving target, now gives the same integral. For a continuous compactly supported test the resulting fibre integrals are continuous on this regular neighborhood. In the moving frames the unitary section can be chosen with image in a compact set when y ranges over a compact part of the orbit. For fixed y the smaller regular fibre family gives continuity in the target coordinates under the integral. Compact control of the frames bounds these integrals by a common integrable function of y. Dominated convergence then gives continuity of the outer integral. By pointwise uniqueness, the two measures therefore agree at the specified block target. There a = 1, b = 0, and the complement target is T. The only remaining factor is cv(t)n with cv(t) = |t|v−2. Axler, Theorem 3.31 on dominated convergence.

Why is the global normalization one?

Over F = ℚ take E = ℚ(i), the ambient form diag(2,1) and two columns. At the real place fix the first column (1,0) and take the second to be (1 + i,1). Its pairing with the first column is b = 2 + 2i, and its length is 5. Subtracting |b|²/2 = 4 leaves the complement length 1. The full Gram determinant is 2·5 − 8 = 2. Multiplication by 2 in the complex b-coordinate multiplies area by 4, so pushing the original area forward gives the factor ¼. At the place 2, |2|₂ = ½ gives the factor 4. Every other finite place gives one. These two columns in dimension two illustrate the local coordinates and the product of the scalar factors. The global induction below requires at least one extra ambient dimension.

For every rational length t ∈ F×, the product formula gives ∏v|t|v = 1. Hence the product of the local factors cv(t)n is one as well. At almost all finite places the standard lattice test also separates into the standard tests for the first vector and the complement. This latter assertion uses integral isometries at good places. The global induction uses the local recursion in the strict range n + 1 ≤ r, with the full binary space anisotropic when r = 1. The local measure identity itself needs the represented nonzero line and the indicated regular targets. Sutherland, Theorem 13.21 on the product formula.
