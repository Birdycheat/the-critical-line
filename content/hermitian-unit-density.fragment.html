How many vectors represent a unit at a finite place?

Fix a finite place of F. Write O for its valuation ring, π for a uniformizer and k for its residue field of size Q. The quadratic algebra is unramified. Its residue algebra is either k × k (put η = +1) or the field of Q² elements (put η = −1). Write h for the Hermitian form, with norm h(x,x), and let Λ be a self-dual Hermitian lattice of rank M ≥ 1 and let β be any unit of O, in particular either sign in the rank comparison. Normalize additive Haar measure to give volume one to Λ and to O. The density at β is the mass of vectors with norm in β + πjO, divided by the volume Q−j of that target ball, as j increases.

Start with one coordinate. At a split place its norm is uv. There are Q−1 ways to make any prescribed nonzero value and 2Q−1 ways to make zero. At an inert place the norm is zQ+1. On the cyclic multiplicative group of order Q²−1 it has image of order Q−1 and kernel of order Q+1. Only zero has norm zero. In both cases the number for a prescribed value b is Q−η, with an extra ηQ when b = 0. Keith Conrad, Finite Fields, Theorem 5.7 (trace and norm).

The reduction of Λ is nondegenerate by self-duality. In the split case it is a pair of spaces in perfect duality, so dual bases give the sum of M products. In the field case it has a vector with nonzero norm. Indeed, if all norms vanished, expanding the norm of x + az for every scalar a would give Tr(a h(x,z)) = 0 for every a. The nondegenerate field trace pairing would then force h = 0. Scale a vector of nonzero norm to norm one by surjectivity of the field norm, split off its orthogonal complement, and repeat. In either case the reduced form is a sum of M copies of the one-coordinate norm.

Let ZM count the vectors of norm zero, including the origin. With the first M coordinates fixed, the last norm has to cancel their sum. Each choice gives Q−η possibilities for the last coordinate, plus the correction ηQ on ZM choices. Hence Z₀ = 1 and ZM+1 = Q2M(Q−η) + ηQZM. Substitution gives QZM = Q2M + (Q−1)(ηQ)M. Multiplying all coordinates by a scalar of prescribed nonzero norm permutes the nonzero fibres. Dividing the remaining Q2M−ZM vectors by Q−1 therefore gives the count for one nonzero target.

BM = Q2M−1 − ηMQM−1.   Density at β = BM/Q2M−1 = 1 − ηMQ−M.

Why does the first residue digit suffice? Given a solution x modulo πj, write a lift as x + πjz. For j ≥ 1 the next digit of its norm changes by the linear functional Tr h(x,z) on the 2M residue coordinates. Here the next digit means the coefficient of πj, viewed in k. The quadratic term in z vanishes modulo πj+1. At an inert place x is nonzero modulo π, so nondegeneracy makes h(x,·) onto the residue extension, and the trace onto k is surjective even in characteristic two. At a split place the functional is u·δv + v·δu, which is nonzero at a unit value u·v. Thus exactly Q2M−1 lifts hit each prescribed next digit. The density ratio stays fixed at every level. This extends the good-place lifting calculation for one unit target to primes above 2. In rank one, successive lifting and completeness also give an integral scalar of any prescribed unit norm. Scaling by this scalar preserves Λ and its Haar measure, so the unit fibres have equal density. This also recovers the answer by dividing the total mass of vectors of unit norm by the volume 1−1/Q of the units.

Take the base field ℚ₂ and M = 3. In the split case, each of the seven nonzero triples u admits four triples v with u·v = 1 over 𝔽₂. In the inert case every nonzero element of 𝔽₄ has norm one. An odd number of the three coordinates must be nonzero, giving 3·3 + 3³ = 36 possibilities. Thus the counts modulo 2 are 28 and 36 among 64, respectively. Modulo 4, a prescribed lift of 1 has 28·32 = 896 or 36·32 = 1152 representations among 4096 vectors. Dividing those proportions by the target mass 1/4 gives 7/8 and 9/8 again. The inert density exceeds one because the mass is measured per unit of target volume.

How do the local densities give the discriminant in the mean?

Return to a totally real field F of degree f, its CM extension E unramified at every finite place, and a lattice self-dual at all those places. In rank M ≥ 3 the one-vector mean identity expresses the normalized average for the target ±1 as a product of densities. Its global proportionality constant is fixed to one by the genus-mass argument. It initially applies to smooth compactly supported archimedean tests. The rank comparison uses nonnegative tests and then exhausts the Gaussian. Each local density uses Fourier self-dual measures for the same additive character on both the vector space and the scalar target. To use the count above, we need the volume of each of the two integral lattices in these measures. At a finite place u, define the integer tᵤ by declaring the annihilator of Oᵤ under the character pairing to be π−tᵤOᵤ. Put Dᵤ = Qᵤtᵤ. The integral trace pairing of an unramified quadratic algebra is perfect, so Hermitian self-duality gives the vector annihilator π−tᵤΛ. For a compact open additive subgroup A, the Fourier transform of its indicator equals vol(A) on its annihilator and zero elsewhere, by translation invariance. Fourier inversion at zero gives vol(A) vol(A⊥) = 1. Enlarging or shrinking by π−tᵤ scales the volume by Dᵤ in one base dimension. The vector lattice has 2M base dimensions, the scalar lattice one. Their respective volumes are therefore Dᵤ−M and Dᵤ−1/2. Dividing the vector volume by the scalar volume gives the corrected density.

αu(β) = Dᵤ1/2−M(1 − ηuMQᵤ−M).

At a real place v, let λv be the nonzero frequency of the character relative to exp(2πit). In the signed diagonal frame the trace pairing on ℂM has real diagonal entries ±2λv, each complex coordinate supplying two real entries. Fourier inversion gives the square root of the absolute determinant as the self-dual volume multiplier, namely 2M|λv|M. The real scalar line contributes |λv|1/2. The local factor for the test function is thus 2M|λv|M−1/2 times its ordinary real density dᵥ at the chosen sign.

All these characters are components of one character trivial on the diagonal copy of F. The adelic character theorem expresses it as the standard trace character scaled by some b ∈ F×. The real frequencies are the real embeddings of b. At finite places the trace-dual of the integers is the inverse different. Thus tᵤ is the valuation of the fractional ideal (b)𝔇 at u, where 𝔇 is the different of F/ℚ. Taking ideal norms gives ∏uDᵤ = |disc(F)| ∏v|λv|, with only finitely many Dᵤ different from one. Bjorn Poonen, Tate’s Thesis, §5.2, Corollary 5.4.

The finite Euler factors left in αu multiply to 1/LM for M ≥ 2 by the split and inert Euler product. Thus every frequency cancels. Since the relative different of the unramified quadratic extension E/F is the unit ideal, the discriminant tower formula gives |disc(E)| = |disc(F)|². Equivalently |disc(F)| = (2νE)f. Andrew Sutherland, Definition 12.2, Theorem 12.26 and Proposition 12.27 (different and discriminant in towers).

m = 2Mf|disc(F)|1/2−M/LM = (2νE)f/2νE−Mf/LM.

For instance, rescaling the character by the rational number 2 multiplies ∏v|λv| and ∏uDᵤ by 2f. Their powers M−1/2 and 1/2−M cancel. With the original quotient volume X, the arithmetic evaluation of the integral in the rank comparison is now Xm ∏vdᵥ.

To return to the period maps, compare that mean with its evaluation by unfolding a marked unit vector. This gives a quotient of Hermitian volumes and thereby the denominator in the short-flag probability bound. Controlling the volume lost to those flags enters the upper bound on the available rows. The lower bound on the rank detected by the period maps also needs estimates for the diagonal and mixed entries of their Gram matrices. Those estimates are separate inputs to that dimension argument.
