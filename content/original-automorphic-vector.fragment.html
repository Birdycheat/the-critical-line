How does a scalar automorphic form give a vector in the dual coefficient space?

Start with the irreducible automorphic subspace U for the definite unitary group of odd rank over the CM field. The type at w₀ supplies a compact open subgroup J, a character χ, and a nonzero vector on which J acts by χ. Choose the compact weight W whose finite part occurs in U, and denote its representation space by VW. An element of this finite part is an intertwining map f from VW into the scalar automorphic functions in U. A nonzero intertwiner has an open stabilizer, hence a level with a finite set of exceptional places. We change the intertwiner so that it is fixed by the full integral group at each eligible split spherical place away from the distinguished pair, and transforms by χ at w₀. Write S for the remaining exceptional places.

The following evaluation is the reason for taking the dual. At each argument g, set Af(g)(v) = f(v)(g). It is a linear functional of v. For b in the compact group at infinity, the intertwining property gives

Af(gb)(v) = Af(g)(W(b)v).

Thus right translation by b applies the dual action W∨(b−1) to the value. If the evaluation vanished at every finite adelic argument u, the factorization g = ub and this equation would force f to vanish everywhere. Its restriction to finite arguments is therefore nonzero. On the finite similitude group these arguments form the subgroup with multiplier one. For a rational element γ of multiplier one, rational invariance gives Af(γfu) = Af(u) ∘ W(γ∞−1), by moving the infinite component to the right. This is precisely the required rational action on a dual functional.

At a split spherical place away from S and its conjugate, every value f(v) is fixed by the integral local group. Why does it have the required Hecke eigenvalues? The copy of the given spherical constituent in U, together with its translates by the centralizer of the local group, spans U. Indeed, this span is invariant under both groups, hence under the entire group by the split factorization, and is nonzero. Averaging over the integral subgroup writes any fixed vector as a sum of images of local fixed vectors. On each of them a spherical Hecke operator acts by the same Satake character. It thus acts by this scalar on f(v) for every v, giving the eigenvalue equation on the dual evaluation.

Why can the local type be imposed without losing the vector?

At a split place let H be the embedded local general linear group. For a compact open subgroup J and a character χ occurring in the representation, let Pχ average the right translations R(j) with weight χ(j)−1 and total Haar mass one. The character kernel is open. For a nonzero vector of type χ it is the preimage in J of its open stabilizer. On finitely many smooth vectors the average is therefore a finite sum over one common quotient.

The average could kill the chosen intertwiner. Consider the subspace of U consisting of the vectors a for which PχR(h)a vanishes for every h ∈ H. It is stable under H by translation and under every element commuting with H because these elements commute with the average. At a split place each group element factors into its local component and an element commuting with H. The subspace is therefore invariant under the whole group. A nonzero vector of type χ lies outside it, since its average is itself. Irreducibility forces the subspace to be zero.

Choose v with f(v) ≠ 0. For some h the map PχR(h)f is then nonzero at v. The local operators commute with the infinite group and with the level away from the conjugate pair, so the new map is still an intertwiner and retains the earlier invariances. A split spherical constituent supplies the trivial type on the integral subgroup at either conjugate place: its nonzero local intertwiner is injective by irreducibility, and inverse conjugate transpose preserves the integral group. Use this step first with trivial character to remove the finitely many eligible exceptions, then with the prescribed character at w₀.

How does the extended form acquire a central character?

Replace J at w₀ by ker χ and call the resulting finite level K′. For the rational coefficient action ξ₀ use, at each infinite place v, the shift mv = ∑i|Wv,i| and central exponent j = ∑v,i(Wv,i + mv). For a rational similitude γ, a multiplier-one adele u, and k ∈ K′ define F(γfuk) = ξ₀(γ)Af(u). Extend by zero elsewhere. To compare two factorizations, take their multipliers. The ratio of the rational multipliers is a positive rational number and a unit at every finite place, so it equals one. The remaining rational change is in the unitary subgroup and the remaining compact change is in its level. The two covariance laws of Af now give the same value.

Taking γ = k = 1 recovers Af on the multiplier-one subgroup, so F ≠ 0. The type translations and the spherical sums preserve the character-kernel level K′. Each summand has multiplier one. On a point γfuk in the support of the extension rule, level invariance removes k and rational covariance removes γ, leaving the original eigenvalue equation at u. On the complement all terms vanish: right multiplication by an element of multiplier one preserves the multiplier class defining that complement. These equations persist under central translation, which commutes with all the right translations in the sums.

Now translate F by the images ζ(y) of the finite ideles of E under the central norm map. Put O = ζ−1(K′), an open subgroup. Only finitely many translates are needed up to scalars. Indeed, the valuations of an idele give a fractional ideal. The Minkowski bound gives finitely many ideal classes, since only finitely many integral ideals have bounded norm. Choose one idele for each class. Dividing by the chosen representative and a principal idele leaves an element of ∏w𝒪w×. This product of compact local unit groups is compact, so its cover by open cosets of O has a finite subcover. The level part fixes F, and the principal idele acts through a rational scalar in the coefficient representation. The translates thus span a nonzero finite-dimensional complex vector space.

If one central translation acts nonscalarly on this space, an eigenspace for it is nonzero, proper and preserved by all the others. Repeating on a space of smaller dimension produces a nonzero simultaneous eigenform Φ. It carries the original type and spherical eigenvalues. Its central eigenvalues form a character ψ because translations compose and are invertible. A linear combination of translates can change where the form vanishes. From here on, choose the nonzero evaluation point for Φ itself.

Why choose the nonzero argument after the central eigenspace?

Let an involution exchange the two elements in each of two disjoint pairs. Give a function F values (1,1) on the first pair and (1,−1) on the second. The projection (I − R)/2 onto its negative eigenspace gives values (0,0) and (1,−1). The projected function is nonzero, yet it vanishes at either point of the first pair. Knowing one nonzero value of the intermediate extension does not specify a nonzero value of the final eigenform.

Where are the nonidentity components of the central idele?

Write K = ℚ(x) for the imaginary quadratic subfield of the CM field E, with x² = −d and d a positive rational integer. Write c for CM conjugation. For a finite idele y of E, first take its norm to K, include that idele back into E, and conjugate. With this scalar t(y) the central map is

t(y) = c(ιK,ENE/Ky),   ζ(y) = (t(y)In, NE/ℚy).

These maps are continuous homomorphisms of finite idele groups. The norm from the quadratic field multiplies a scalar by its conjugate. Transitivity of norms gives c(t)t = ιℚ,ENE/ℚy, exactly the similitude equation for a scalar matrix, and scalar matrices commute with the entire group. For a principal idele k, each embedding τ takes t(k) to the product of σ(k) over the embeddings σ restricting to the conjugate of τ on K. This is the condition σ(x) = −τ(x) in the archimedean weight of the central character.

Now let y have a single component at w over v in K. The idelic norm is a product of local norms at the places over v, so its only possible nonidentity component is at v. Including in E and conjugating moves that support to the places u such that ū lies over v. At such a place the normalized valuation is

ordu(t(y)) = e(ū/v) f(w/v) ordw(yw).

The residue degree f enters when taking the local norm and the ramification index e enters when extending the valuation back to E. A local unit therefore gives an integral invertible scalar everywhere. For a uniformizer, suppose v ≠ c(v). The rational prime q below v then splits in K, so N(v) = q and ordv(q) = 1. Since N(w) = qf(w/v), the valuation above equals that of the scalar N(w) at u. This is the comparison that places the quotient of the two central elements in the integral level. Two places of the Gaussian field

For E = K = ℚ(i), a single local uniformizer 2 + i at w = (2 + i) gives scalar 2 − i at the conjugate place. The desired scalar there is N(w) = 5. Their quotient is 2 + i, a unit at (2 − i). At the inert place (3), conjugation fixes the place. The image of the uniformizer 3 has valuation one, whereas the scalar N((3)) = 9 has valuation two. The latter ratio fails the integral-inverse condition.

Why does the inverse central character appear in the spherical test?

Let the central map ζ take the finite ideles of the CM field E into the centre of the finite similitude group. Write ψ for the eigencharacter of the selected automorphic vector, so Φ(gζ(y)) = ψ(y)Φ(g). The subgroup on which the type is trivial fixes Φ. Its inverse image under ζ is open, and evaluating at a point where Φ is nonzero shows that ψ is one on that inverse image. Thus ψ has open kernel.

For k ∈ E× the central image of the principal finite idele kf comes from a rational similitude. Its scalar action on the coefficient space is M(k) = ∏ττ(k)bτ. The exponent bτ starts at the multiplier exponent j. Each chosen infinite embedding v with v(x) = −τ(x) contributes −∑iWv,i because the coefficient representation acts on the dual. Left rational covariance and right central translation now compute the same nonzero vector times a scalar, hence ψ(kf) = M(k). Extend the displayed monomial to the infinite components and denote it by M∞. Then

κ(y) = ψ(yf)−1 M∞(y∞).

The two factors cancel on every principal idele. The finite factor is locally constant by the open-kernel calculation, while the infinite factor is continuous. Their product therefore defines a continuous character of the idele class group. At a single finite place w the infinite factor is one, so κw(t) = ψ(ιw(t))−1, where ιw inserts t at w and one elsewhere.

To pass from this identity of characters to the actual local central element in the observed operator, put K = ℚ(x) and D(w) = {u : ū and w restrict to the same place of K}. Assume D(w) misses the finite level support S. Local units at w have integral central image and give the identity at every place of S, so κw is unramified. Assume also w ∉ D(w). For a uniformizer ϖw, the central image z₀ = ζ(ιw(ϖw)) has the valuation of Nw at every place of D(w). An eligible element z in the spherical test has scalar matrix Nw there. Both elements have identity matrix component off D(w). Thus the matrix of z₀−1z is integral with integral inverse at every place and equals the identity on the entire level support. Its multiplier is also a unit. The similitude equation applied to the integral matrix and to its integral inverse makes the multiplier and its reciprocal integral (the rank is positive). The quotient belongs to the character-kernel level. This gives Φ(gz) = ψ(ιw(ϖw))Φ(g) = κw(ϖw)−1Φ(g). Taking the ith power explains the inverse exponent in the spherical eigenvalue.

The local reciprocal at a Gaussian prime

Over ℚ(i), set ψ(yf) = ∏w(Nw)ordw(yw). On a principal finite idele it equals k k̄, so the two infinite exponents are one. At w = (2 + i) the norm is five. The finite eigenvalue ψ(ιw(ϖw)) is 5 and κw(ϖw) is 1/5. Right translation therefore acts by the reciprocal of 1/5.

Why is there a nonzero value at an argument integral above the geometric prime?

First choose g₀ with Φ(g₀) ≠ 0. The entries of its matrix and its inverse are finite adeles, so both sets of entries are integral at all but finitely many places. Let T be the union of their exceptional places. At any w outside T the matrix belongs to GLn(𝒪w). Add the rational primes below S ∪ T and below the selected observations to the exclusion set in the geometric prime lemma. It gives a good prime p split in ℚ(x) and larger than the defect integer of the arithmetic datum. All bad level places avoid p, and the same g₀ is integral with integral inverse at every place above p. The transport through the frame can therefore retain this value. For a prime fixed in advance, integrality at a nonzero argument would be an additional hypothesis. The prime supporting the prescribed type has already been fixed. The regular-support prime is chosen later.

An integral matrix whose inverse has a denominator

Over ℚ(i), the scalar matrix 2I has similitude multiplier 4 for the standard definite form. Its entries are integral at every finite place. The inverse (1/2)I is integral precisely away from the place over 2. A finite argument with this matrix is therefore in the integral matrix group above 3 but fails the same test above 2. Knowing only that the entries of the argument are integral would miss the excluded prime.
