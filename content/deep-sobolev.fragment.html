How an integral controls the starting value

The mean-zero argument starts with a bound for evaluation at every point y in a neighborhood of a deep point x. The available norm is an integral over the whole quotient. To pass from that norm to a value at y, we first average along short curves through y, then count how often their coordinate neighborhood can cover the same point of the quotient.

Evaluation along one curve. For a continuously differentiable complex function h on [0, s], integration of its derivative and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality give |h(0)| ≤ |h(t)| + √s (∫ |h′|²)1/2 for every t in that interval. Square using (a + b)² ≤ 2a² + 2b² and average over t. The resulting endpoint estimate is

|h(0)|² ≤ (2/s) ∫0s |h(t)|² dt + 2s ∫0s |h′(t)|² dt.

There are N = n²f real directions, an orthonormal Lie algebra basis of size n² at each of f places. Write Fw for the continuous representative of one automorphic image of a test vector w. Along the jth one-parameter subgroup the derivative of Fw is F∂ⱼw. Differentiating raises the allowed order of the vector by one. Suppose the integral of |Fw|² on the full parameter cube is at most C(y)‖w‖² for all vectors through order P + N, and ‖∂ⱼw‖ ≤ β‖w‖ at the orders where a derivative is taken.

Use the subgroups in a fixed product order. For fixed values of the first N − 1 parameters apply the endpoint estimate in the last one, then integrate in the first N − 1. The two full-cube integrals are bounded by C(y)‖w‖² and C(y)‖∂Nw‖². Thus removing one parameter costs at most 2/s + 2sβ². The bound on the smaller cube holds through order P + N − 1. After N removals the cube is the point y, and all vectors through order P are still covered. With s = 1/β each removal costs 4β. The stated estimate rounds (4β)N up to (6β)N.

How often does the cube cover a quotient point?

Write t(y) = min(1, τ(y)), where τ is the least normalized sublattice size. Let P∞ = ∏v cv be the product of the gauge constants that specify real-place Haar measures, and let kg be the positive Haar volume of the finite level subgroup Kg.

Change of variables in the small cube. For 12n³s ≤ 1 and 8n⁵s ≤ 1, the quantitative exponential-coordinate theorem bounds the parameter integral of any nonnegative measurable function by efP∞ times its Haar integral on a set S at the real places. Any two matrices in S differ multiplicatively by a matrix of operator norm at most 2 at each place. The factor ef allows a Jacobian loss of at most e at each place.

Extend S by Kg at the finite places. The squared representative is invariant under right translation by Kg, so this extension multiplies its integral by kg. When we fold the translate y(S × Kg) into a fundamental domain, each point z is counted once for each rational translate γz inside it. Fix one such γ₀ if any exist. The ratios γγ₀−1 are distinct rational transformations that preserve the finite lattice at y. In coordinates at y their operator norms are at most 2 at each real place.

The lattice separation inequality gives a lower bound t(y)² for the geometric mean over the real places of the operator-norm distances between two distinct such matrices. To see the two factors, take the difference of the corresponding rational transformations. It sends the lattice at y into itself and has nonzero image. For such a map between lattices, the geometric mean of its local operator norms is at least the least size of the target divided by the largest size of the source. Here the target has least size τ(y). The Hermitian lattice is self-dual, so duality reverses the filtration and replaces each size by its reciprocal. The largest size of the source is 1/τ(y). Their quotient is τ(y)², which is at least t(y)² after truncation. At least one local distance is therefore ≥ t(y)². In the maximum of the local norms, the open balls of radius t(y)²/2 about the matrices are disjoint. They lie in the ball of radius 2 + t(y)²/2. Volumes scale with exponent 2N, the real dimension of f spaces of complex n × n matrices. Their volume ratio bounds the multiplicity by (1 + 4/t(y)²)2N. Enlarging 4 to 4√n gives the bound used in the count. Dividing by the finite volume introduced above gives the cube constant

C(y) = ef (1 + 4√n/t(y)²)2N P∞/kg.
The initial bound for all the basis functions

Under the hypotheses of the indefinite count, choose P = 8qN and β = √n b² + (3/2)n3/2 + 3(P + N). The discrete-series derivative estimate gives ‖∂ⱼw‖ ≤ (√n b² + (3/2)n3/2 + 3p)‖w‖ at order p for every unit basis direction. Taking p ≤ P + N gives the common β. Since n ≥ 8 and n19 ≤ b², the chosen s = 1/β satisfies both small-cube conditions. Each of the D isometric automorphic maps sends a vector u to a function of quotient norm ‖u‖. Sum the D scalar evaluation bounds. For their vector of values vu(y) this gives

‖vu(y)‖² ≤ (6β)N D C(y) ‖u‖².

The lattice-size comparison on a unipotent neighborhood of radius at most log 2 gives t(y) ≥ t(x)/2 = τ(x)/2 throughout the deep-point neighborhood. Since 0 < τ(x) < 1/2, we have 1 + 4√n/t(y)² ≤ 17√n/τ(x)². The bound now holds with one constant throughout the neighborhood. Taking its square root gives the initial M in the mean-zero iteration.
