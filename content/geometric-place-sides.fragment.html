How can one tell whether two test places choose the same quadratic root?

Let K = ℚ(x), with x² = −d and d positive, inside the CM field E. Two places of E can induce the same prime ideal of the integers of K. This matters at a rational prime q where the construction must choose one of two local roots. We need a test that works even when the chosen integral generator ω generates a proper subring of the integers of K.

Quadratic residue test. Suppose K = ℚ(ω), with ω integral of trace t, norm n and discriminant Δ = t² − 4n. Let q be a rational prime coprime to Δ for which X² − tX + n has a root modulo q. For every place w above q, there is a unique one of the two residue roots r for which ω − r belongs to w. A second place u restricts to the same prime of K exactly when it lies above q and ω reduces to r there. Complex conjugation exchanges the two roots.

In the residue field at w the quadratic factors as (X − r)(X − (t − r)). Its roots differ because (2r − t)² ≡ Δ modulo q. Reducing ω singles out one root. Since the conjugate of ω is t − ω, the conjugate place singles out the other. To see why testing this one generator detects every integral element y of K, write y = α + βω over ℚ. The traces of y and ωy are integers. Indeed they are rational sums of algebraic integers, and a rational root of a monic integer polynomial has denominator one. Evaluating the two traces gives the linear system

(2α + tβ,   tα + (t² − 2n)β) = (Tr(y), Tr(ωy)).

The determinant of this system is Δ. Eliminating α and β shows that Δy = A + Bω with A, B integers. Since Δ is invertible modulo any place above q, membership of y in that prime is equivalent to A + Br ≡ 0 modulo q. The criterion depends only on the residue root, proving the assertion for the whole ring of integers. Conversely the contraction records both q and whether it contains ω − r.

For example, take K = ℚ(i), ω = 2i and q = 5. The order ℤ[2i] misses i, but Δ = −16 is a unit modulo 5. The two roots for ω are 1 and 4. At a place where ω reduces to 1, the missing generator i reduces to 3, since 2 · 3 ≡ 1 modulo 5. At the conjugate place it reduces to 2. This explains why several distinct places of E can be assigned the same residue parameter in the integer level construction.
