How do good primes identify a character on the entire group?

Fix ℓ and write Γ = Gal(Ω/E), where Ω is an algebraic closure of the CM field E. The two lifts give continuous semisimple representations ρ₁ and ρ₂ of dimension n+t over an algebraic closure k of ℚℓ. Call their respective lists of auxiliary Galois characters A and B. Every character in A differs from every character in B as a function on Γ. Write D(A) for the direct sum of its character lines, and LA(g) for the product of their linear factors X−χ(g).

At a good split place the two Frobenius multisets consist of the same normalized source parameters together with the entries of their respective lists. Add the other list to each side. Now both sides contain the common parameters, every entry of A and every entry of B, with multiplicities. In the local–global application the places belong to the set of good split places just constructed. Its inverse Frobenius elements, allowing all choices of place above each prime, remain dense in Γ after removing any finite set and the primes above ℓ. The argument using finite Galois quotients gives this density for the specified set, and the common local comparison supplies its polynomial identity outside finitely many places. Sutherland, Theorem 28.9.

Let Pρ(g) = det(X − ρ(g)). The embedding k ≃ ℂ identifies the polynomials at these Frobenius elements. For any x ∈ k, evaluating both polynomials at x gives continuous k-valued functions of g, since determinants and finite products are continuous. Equality on the dense set extends to every g. Varying x in the infinite field k gives

Pρ₁(g) LB(g) = Pρ₂(g) LA(g)   for every g ∈ Γ.

Brauer–Nesbitt in characteristic zero. Two finite-dimensional semisimple representations of any group over an algebraically closed field of characteristic zero are isomorphic if their characteristic polynomials agree on every element. Indeed, their traces agree on the group and therefore on its group algebra by linearity. Take the finitely many distinct irreducible constituents of both representations. By the density theorem for algebras, one group-algebra element can act by a rank-one projection on any chosen constituent and by zero on the others. Its trace counts the copies of that constituent. Characteristic zero recovers these counts as integers. Equal counts for every constituent give an isomorphism of the two direct sums. Etingof et al., Theorem 2.5(ii) and Theorem 2.17(i).

Apply the theorem to the augmented representations. Fix one isomorphism from ρ₂ ⊕ D(A) to ρ₁ ⊕ D(B), and take a basis vector on a line χ in A. Its image is nonzero and transforms by χ under every g. Write its coordinates as (v, (bγ)γ∈B). Projection onto each auxiliary coordinate gives

γ(g) bγ = χ(g) bγ   for every g ∈ Γ.

If bγ were nonzero, γ and χ would agree on all of Γ, contradicting cross-list disjointness. All auxiliary coordinates vanish. Thus v is a nonzero vector in ρ₁ spanning a Γ-invariant line on which the action is exactly χ.

Four group elements. On Γ = (ℤ/2ℤ)² put α(a,b) = (−1)a and β(a,b) = (−1)b. Let ρ₁ = α ⊕ 1 and ρ₂ = β ⊕ 1. Both characters take the value −1 at (1,1), and both take the value 1 at (0,0). At (1,0) their values are −1 and 1. The two original polynomials there are (X+1)(X−1) and (X−1)². Adding β on the first side and α on the second gives (X+1)(X−1)² on each side. For every element the augmented representations have the same three summands, in different order. Removing α from ρ₁ and β from ρ₂ leaves the trivial character in both.

What survives after one character line is removed?

Let V carry the current first representation ρ and let k v be the invariant line just constructed. Semisimplicity means that every invariant subspace has an invariant complement. Choose such a complement W to k v. Put a basis of W followed by v into the columns of a matrix M. Writing σ for the action on W gives a single change of basis for every group element.

M−1ρ(g)M = diag(σ(g),χ(g)),   dim W = dim V − 1.

The complement is semisimple. Given an invariant subspace U of W, take an invariant complement Y to U in V. Then W = U ⊕ (Y ∩ W). For continuity, observe that the matrix entries of σ are entries of the fixed conjugate above, and its inverse at g is σ(g−1).

Delete the chosen occurrence of χ from the first list and call the shorter list A′. The block formula factors Pρ(g) by X−χ(g), and the same factor appears in LA(g). It is a nonzero monic polynomial, so it cancels in the integral domain k[X], even when another eigenvalue has the very same value at g. The complement therefore satisfies the shortened identity.

Pσ(g) LB(g) = Pρ₂(g) LA′(g)   for every g ∈ Γ.

At each step both the dimension and the list length drop by one. If χ appears twice in A, the second occurrence remains available at the next step. Only equality across lists was excluded. After all t steps a continuous semisimple representation s of dimension n remains, with ρ₁ ≃ s ⊕ D(A). This removes precisely the prescribed number of copies. The source itself may have further copies of the same character.

Which local statements pass to the rank-n representation?

Fix the two lifts. The residual representation s has already been chosen when we choose a finite place w. Suppose the first lift has prescribed parameters and that each removed character has its own known local parameter ci. Above ℓ, also prescribe the filtration jump mi(τ) of each character for every embedding τ of E into k inducing w. If the first lift has the following multisets, then s has the remaining parameters S and jumps J(τ).

S ⊎ {c1,…,ct},   J(τ) ⊎ {m1(τ),…,mt(τ)}.

Away from ℓ, inertia acts trivially on each block when it acts trivially on the first lift. The block determinant removes one Frobenius factor at a time. Above ℓ, the filtered de Rham period module of a direct sum is the direct sum of the filtered period modules. The de Rham condition therefore passes to each block, and the dimensions of each graded piece add. This subtracts precisely one copy of each prescribed jump at each embedding. Berger’s monodromy theorem makes these de Rham representations potentially semistable. Their Weil–Deligne parameters also form a direct sum. The crystalline condition on the whole gives trivial Weil–Deligne inertia and zero monodromy on both blocks, hence crystallinity of the remainder. For a Weil element of degree d, the cancelled polynomial is X−cid. Berger, Représentations p-adiques et équations différentielles and Fontaine, Exposé VIII, §§2.1.1 and 2.3.3.

To recover the original normalization, choose a place w₀ ∈ Q. The two-lift theorem supplies a twist κ and first-list Hecke characters unramified there. For a source Satake multiset at that place, let S₀ be its algebraically normalized multiset and J₀(τ) its predicted jumps. If κ has infinity exponent mτ at the complex embedding corresponding to τ, subtraction leaves the data on the left.

s   (κ(w₀)−1S₀, J₀(τ)+mτ)   ⟶   s ⊗ κℓ   (S₀, J₀(τ)).

Here scaling or translating a multiset acts on every entry. The ℓ-adic character κℓ multiplies each local parameter by κ(w₀) and contributes the jump −mτ. The second list was needed for the global comparison. The local subtraction at w₀ uses only the first list.

Changing w₀ can change the auxiliary lifts. For fixed ℓ, their twisted remainders are all weakly attached to the original automorphic representation. This means their normalized Frobenius polynomials agree with its Satake data at all but finitely many places of Q. Density and Brauer–Nesbitt identify any two such semisimple representatives. Their isomorphism transports the local conclusion at each w₀ to one rank-n representation. This is the even-rank input to descent for the prescribed set Q. When the odd-rank input holds on all split places, one may take that whole set for Q. In each formulation the local conclusion applies wherever the original representation has the stated Satake data.
