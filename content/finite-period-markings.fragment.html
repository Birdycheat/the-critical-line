How do the two finite markings recover an integral lattice?

A point has two finite coordinates because the level treats the prime p separately. Fix the reference lattice Λ₀ with its order action, its rational space V = ℚ ⊗ Λ₀ and an integer N > 0 prime to p. A marked lattice consists of a full lattice Λ with the prescribed polarisation form E and a map t from Λ₀ to Λ whose residue modulo N gives the level structure. Place Λ in V by an order-linear injection j spanning V, and write L = j(Λ). The rational alternating form on V pulls back to E up to a positive rational factor.

Away from p, the coordinate b reads V in the reference finite adelic space. Write Rb for this map, obtained by the chosen trivialisation of the rational form followed by b−1. Write Λ̂₀p for the product of the reference lattices at the primes different from p. A vector x belongs to L[1/p] exactly when Rb(x) belongs to Λ̂₀p. The same coordinate reads the level by the congruence Rb(j(t(a))) ≡ a modulo NΛ̂₀p, for every a ∈ Λ₀. Here a enters diagonally. Two such readings give the same right coset modulo the principal level group Kp(N). At p, the second coordinate bp specifies the local lattice, represented inside V by L(p) = L ⊗ ℤ(p). Its stabilizer is the prescribed integral subgroup Kp. Together these readings determine L by intersection.

L = L[1/p] ∩ L(p)   inside V.

Indeed, a vector x in the intersection satisfies prx ∈ L and mx ∈ L for some m prime to p. Bézout’s identity gives integers A, B with Apr + Bm = 1. Thus x = A(prx) + B(mx) ∈ L. The other inclusion follows by taking both multipliers equal to one.

Finite marking criterion. For the admissible lattices the markings extend compatibly to every level prime to p, preserving the reference form up to a unit at each such level. At p the integral form is twice a form that becomes perfect over ℤ(p). Let y be a rational similitude of the form with positive multiplier, commuting with the given order action. Give each of two admissible marked lattices its own placement and finite representatives. The stabilizer of the lattice at p gives the second test below. The same y must pass both tests

b₁−1 yp b₂ ∈ Kp(N),   b1,p−1 yp b2,p ∈ Kp.

These conditions hold exactly when there is an order-linear isomorphism w ∶ Λ₂ → Λ₁ preserving the alternating forms, with j₁w = y j₂ and w t₂ ≡ t₁ modulo NΛ₁. Indeed the conditions identify both localizations, so their intersection gives y j₂(Λ₂) = j₁(Λ₁). This forces w = j₁−1y j₂ on the two lattices. For each a, let d = w t₂(a) − t₁(a). The away-from-p congruence puts j₁(d)/N in L₁[1/p]. Since N is a unit in ℤ(p), the same vector lies in (L₁)(p). The intersection gives d ∈ NΛ₁.

Why does the rational similitude preserve the integral polarisation exactly?

Suppose E₁(wu,wv) = sE₂(u,v) with s positive rational. The values of each integral form generate a nonzero ideal of ℤ. In the prescribed genus, the level isomorphisms at every prime power away from p identify divisibility of all values up to a unit. At p the half-form in the local genus condition is perfect, which fixes the same p-part of the two ideals, including when p = 2. Thus their positive generators agree. Since w is onto, the first ideal is also s times the second, giving s = 1. Conversely, a marked isomorphism identifies both localizations and the level residues. The two finite coset tests follow from their stabilizers.

Which multiples does each coordinate see when p = 3?

In the rational line take L = 6ℤ. Away from 3 one sees 2ℤ[1/3], which contains 2. At 3 one sees 3ℤ(3), which contains 3. Their intersection is 6ℤ. Indeed a rational number in both has no prime in its reduced denominator and its numerator is divisible by both 2 and 3. Thus the first reading alone misses the factor 3 and the second alone misses the factor 2.

When do two marked periods give the same complex point?

Work with the standard unitary datum with a = m = 1. The distinguished infinite place has positive index one, and the action on ℂg has the prescribed signature for the chosen embedding. For each rational polarised form class [B] admitting the specified trivialisations away from p and over ℝ, choose a representative. Let ΓB be its rational similitudes that commute with the order action and have positive multiplier, and let 𝒟B denote its positive complex lines. Keep N prime to p. At principal level N ≥ 3 the complex points of the standard model XN are in bijection with

XN(ℂ) ≃ ⨆[B] ΓB ∖ ((Gp/Kp(N)) × (Gp/Kp) × 𝒟B).

Here Gp is the group of finite adelic points away from p, and Gp is the local group acting on the lattices at p. The disjoint union keeps different rational form classes apart. Within one class a single element of ΓB acts on all three entries. The point-set argument below works for every positive N prime to p once the family is supplied with a covering by marked period sheets. The finite sheet construction supplies the covering here at full level N ≥ 3. For the classical interpretation of a PEL level orbit, see J. S. Milne, Introduction to Shimura Varieties, Theorem 8.17.

The equivalence on periods. In a period chart the lattice Λ maps into ℂg by a period map P. By comparison on a fibre and the separation assumption for this family, two charts yield the same point exactly when there is an order-linear isomorphism w ∶ Λ₂ → Λ₁ preserving the alternating form and the markings modulo N, together with an invertible complex-linear map H satisfying

P₁(wu) = H(P₂(u))   for every u ∈ Λ₂.

To read this equivalence in the quotient, extend the two period maps to the common rational space by their placements j₁, j₂. Write Q₁ and Q₂ for the extensions, so Pᵢ = Qᵢ jᵢ. An order-linear polarised lattice isomorphism extends rationally to an identification of the two rational forms up to positive scale. They therefore have the same class [B]. In the chosen representative the identification is a similitude y with j₁w = y j₂. By the finite marking criterion the first two cosets agree after transport by y. The displayed period identity gives Q₁ = H ∘ Q₂ ∘ y−1 on V, since every rational vector has a nonzero integer multiple in j₁(Λ₁). The positive line of Q₂ ∘ y−1 is yℓ₂, and the change of complex coordinates H preserves this line. Hence ℓ₁ = yℓ₂.

Conversely, equality in the quotient gives a single y carrying the second triple to the first. The two finite cosets give the integral marked isomorphism w. Equality of the transported positive lines gives an invertible complex-linear H with Q₁ = H ∘ Q₂ ∘ y−1, by the period classification by a line. Restrict to j₁(wΛ₂) to recover the displayed equality. This proves that a class of coordinates identifies exactly one marked period point whenever it occurs.

Why does every triple occur, and what is the inverse map?

Starting with a point, choose its marked period chart. The rational classification supplies [B] and an embedding j of the lattice. Its compatible level markings away from p supply b. Its local lattice supplies bp by the realization of lattices perfect up to positive rational scale. Extend P rationally and take its positive line. The equivalence just proved makes the resulting orbit independent of the chart and the representatives.

Conversely, choose a triple and representatives of its two finite cosets. The integral realization theorem constructs the polarised lattice Λ, marking t and placement j with those readings. The positive line supplies a rational period Q, whose real extension identifies the real span of j(Λ) with ℂg. Its restriction Qj therefore has a full period lattice. Choose a period sheet representing (Λ,t), with marked isomorphism w from Λ to the sheet lattice. On that sheet the period is Qj w−1 and the placement is j w−1, so the resulting point has the prescribed orbit. The marked equivalence above identifies any two points with that orbit. The fixed-point criterion uses this bijection to compare the two legs of a correspondence.
