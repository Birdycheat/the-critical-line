Why the progression still contains enough primes

The selection argument spends a fraction of the primes in one residue class. It needs a positive lower bound for the denominator even when the modulus grows with the first chosen prime. As in both stages, 8 divides m and a is coprime to m. Put L = log U, let π(U;m,a) count all primes up to U congruent to a modulo m, and write li(u) = ∫₂ᵘ dt/log t. The progression theorem supplies a main term B. Without an exceptional zero it is li(U)/φ(m). With an exceptional pair (χ, β) it is

B = [li(U) − Re χ(a) li(Uβ)] / φ(m).

Here χ is a nontrivial quadratic character modulo m with a real zero β between 1 − 1/(8 log m) and 1. Keeping this term matters for a progression on which χ(a) = 1, because it subtracts almost the whole main term if β is close to one. The relative prime-number estimate bounds the error by B times a constant times [exp(−c₃L/log m) + exp(−√(c₃L))]. Uniformly over the admitted data this multiplier is at most ½ for sufficiently large Y. Indeed L/log m ≥ (log Y)²/4 and L ≥ Yε/6. The lower and upper admitted bounds on L also give log m ≤ 4Yε/5, which puts L in the theorem’s range C₀ log m · log(log m + 2) ≤ L. How small can B become? The separate exceptional-zero estimate, used with exponent one, supplies a fixed 0 < c ≤ 1 such that 1 − β ≥ c/m. For L ≥ 4 we have Uβ ≥ 2 and Uβ/U ≤ exp(−c/m) ≤ 1 − c/(2m). In the worst sign the remaining integral from Uβ to U is at least (U − Uβ)/L. With no exceptional pair the integral runs all the way from 2. Using φ(m) ≤ m gives the same floor in every case. For the middle inequality, the threshold gives 80 log m ≤ L and log(320/c) ≤ L/20. Thus m² ≤ exp(L/40), L ≤ 40 exp(L/40), and 320 ≤ c exp(L/20). Multiplication absorbs the denominator into the spare exponent 1/10. The last subtraction discards every integer up to √U, covering all the primes too small for P. This proves the cardinality floor before any twist is tested. The relative progression theorem and the Siegel lower bound used here are deeper analytic inputs.
