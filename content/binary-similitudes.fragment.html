Why can two spheres of different lengths have the same mass coefficient?

Fix a CM extension E/F with conjugation, and an anisotropic Hermitian plane with nonsingular matrix J. Write Q(x) = x*Jx, where * means conjugate transpose. For β ∈ F×, the rational sphere consists of x ∈ E² with Q(x) = β. Average its rational points over the adelic unitary quotient to obtain a measure Θβ. The first mass formula for this length supplies a nonnegative number κ such that, on product tests, Θβ integrates as κ times the product of the local Gram-fibre integrals. A separate choice of κ for each β would leave an unknown weight inside the sum over lengths. A rational change of variables makes the same κ work throughout that sum. Build the change of variables from two points. Suppose Q(x) = β and Q(x′) = β′, both nonzero. If x*J = (r₀,r₁), put y = (−r₁,r₀)t and take the two columns of Pₓ to be x and y. Then x*Jy = 0 and det Pₓ = r₀x₀ + r₁x₁ = β. Taking determinants in its diagonal Gram matrix gives βQ(y) = β² det J. Thus the two frames give the same diagonal form up to their respective lengths. Follow the factor at infinity and at 2 for a sphere of length two

Over ℚ(i)/ℚ take J = I₂, x = (1,0)t and x′ = (1,i)t. Their lengths are 1 and 2. The frame gives γ with rows (1,i), (i,1). Multiplication gives γ*γ = 2I₂ and detℚ(i)γ = 2. In the four rational coordinates its determinant is the norm of 2, hence 4. A pushforward measures the inverse image of a set. At infinity its factor on vectors is therefore 1/4, and on lengths it is 1/2. The fibre factor is (1/4)/(1/2) = 1/2. At 2 the absolute values are |4|₂ = 1/4 and |2|₂ = 1/2, giving pushforward factors 4 and 2 and fibre factor 2. At every odd prime the factors are one. Thus the product of the fibre factors is (1/2)·2 = 1, although the real fibre measure alone changes. This computes the transport of a mass formula without evaluating either sphere’s individual mass.

Which lengths reach the counting theorem? Start with the coefficient supplied for a fixed β ≠ 0. If the infinite fibre measure or any finite fibre measure is zero, its factorization forces Θβ = 0. The local finiteness and summability already supplied let one write the zero factorization with coefficient 1 directly. Otherwise the binary representation theorem gives a rational vector of length β from the nonzero infinite fibre measure. Now consider every β′ ≠ 0. A represented β′ receives the same κ by the preceding argument. For an unrepresented β′ the rational sum is empty, and the contrapositive of that representation theorem gives zero infinite fibre measure. Apply the individual mass formula for β′ to obtain its finiteness and summability conditions. Its zero factorization now holds with the chosen κ as well. For example, length −1 is empty over the reals in the positive definite plane above. The degree-one Gram matrix at β = 0 is singular, and anisotropy leaves only the zero rational vector there. The counting theorem uses precisely the nonzero lengths. Having a common κ on all of them permits the positive-volume comparison that gives κ = 1. Why does the infinite fibre detect a rational point in this plane?

For a proposed nonzero length t, compare J with diag(t, t det J). Their determinants differ by t², the norm of t from E to F. At an indefinite real place both planes have one positive and one negative direction. At a definite place representing the sign of t makes their signatures agree there too. With all signatures equal, Landherr’s classification gives a rational isometry of the two Hermitian forms. The image of the first basis vector then has length t. Consequently a missing rational length has the wrong sign at some definite real place. The infinite fibre is empty there and its supported measure vanishes.
