Which short rows stay independent over the number field?

The height label uses one power of two for each row. To extract those powers we need an invertible rational row matrix Δ for which both the remaining block and its inverse stay bounded. A bound on the entries alone would allow two rows to become almost parallel. The proof brings the volume spanned by the rows into the same estimate. Write D = [E ∶ ℚ], n for the number of rows and r = Dn for the real dimension in the reduction. The row reduction and its two uniform bounds · Selecting the rows and choosing powers of two

Read lengths and volume from one flag. In its real span, let L be the Euclidean lattice generated by the images of an integral basis of the row module. Its jth successive minimum λj is the smallest radius of a closed ball about zero containing j linearly independent lattice vectors. Discreteness gives vectors attaining these radii in a nested sequence of spans. Orthogonalize the resulting real basis. Scale the new orthogonal direction at step j by 1/λj, obtaining a linear map T. If the last nonzero orthogonal coordinate of a lattice vector x is its jth, then x lies outside the previous span. To allow for tied minima, let k be the first index with λk = λj. If x were shorter, it and the first k − 1 vectors would give k independent vectors shorter than λk. Thus ‖x‖ ≥ λj. Every nonzero coordinate of x is scaled by at least 1/λj. Thus ‖Tx‖ ≥ 1. Scaling along the flag of shortest independent vectors · The last nonzero coordinate and the bound on the norm

The inverse image of the open unit ball under T contains no nonzero lattice point. Its volume is vol(ball)·∏λj. By the symmetric convex body bound this is at most 2rcovol(L). To see the volume threshold, shrink the body by one half. If it had more volume than a fundamental cell of L, two of its translates into that cell would overlap. The difference of the two original points would be a nonzero lattice vector in the original body, by convexity and symmetry. The unit ball contains the open cube of side 2/√r, in orthonormal coordinates. For r ≥ 1 these observations give

λ₁ ⋯ λr ≤ rr/2 covol(L).

The covolume is taken in the real span of L. For an integral basis u with image under the row map φ, it is √det Gram(φu). Take one row from each block of D minima. Suppose we have selected i rows independent over E. Their field span has dimension Di over ℚ. The ball of radius λDi+1 contains Di + 1 real-independent lattice vectors. Lift them through φ to integral combinations of the original rows. A rational relation between the lifts would, after clearing denominators, be an integral relation between the independent images. At least one lift therefore lies outside the previous field span. Choose it as the next row wi+1. Since the minima increase, the first radius in each block, raised to the power D, is bounded by the product of the D radii in that block. Consequently

∏i=1n ‖φwi‖D ≤ ∏k=0n−1 λDk+1D ≤ λ₁ ⋯ λr.

Here φ is only required to be additive and ℤ-linear. It sends a row to its archimedean coordinates after the given change of variables. The dimension argument takes place over E before applying φ. Why are the inverses of the normalized blocks bounded?

Choose a dyadic scale ai with ‖φwi‖ ≤ ai < 2‖φwi‖. Dividing each row by its scale bounds every entry of the resulting matrix c. Take all D integral-basis multiples of each of the n selected rows. They span a full-rank sublattice of the original row lattice, of index m ≥ 1. Its squared volume is m² det Gram(φu). Each of the D real rows coming from wi carries the same scale ai. With G(c) the Gram determinant of all the rescaled rows, we get

(m covol(L))² = (∏ aiD)² G(c).

The product of the scales is bounded in the form ∏ aiD ≤ 2rrr/2 covol(L). Cancel the covolume. This bounds m² by a constant times G(c). Since m ≥ 1, it also bounds G(c) away from zero. On the bounded closed set of matrix entries this continuous determinant has a finite upper bound. Its positive lower bound keeps c in an invertible compact set, where inversion is bounded. The upper bound gives a uniform bound for the integer m. Taking the factorial of an integer upper bound makes one integer M divisible by every possible m. Multiplication by M therefore puts every original row in the module generated by the selected rows. Use the same rows at every finite place. Write Ō for the product of the local integer rings at finite places. Given the finite adelic block β, take Λ = {x ∈ En ∣ xβ ∈ Ōn}. An integer clearing the denominators of β shows that Λ contains a multiple of every coordinate row. Clearing the denominators of β−1 bounds Λ inside one fractional free module. As the ring of integers is Noetherian, Λ is finitely generated. Apply the argument at infinity to Λ and call the selected row matrix Δ. The entries of Δβ are integral by construction. For its inverse, choose positive integers d₁,d₂ clearing the denominators of β and Δ−1, respectively. Approximate a row of β−1 by a rational row x so that their difference belongs to d₁d₂Ōn. Multiplying by β shows that xβ is integral. Hence x belongs to Λ, and Mx is an integral combination of the rows of Δ. Multiplication by Δ−1 gives integral entries for MxΔ−1. The original approximation error still has integral entries after multiplication by MΔ−1. This proves that M(Δβ)−1 is integral at every finite place. Together with the compact real block this is the uniform reduction used for the height label. Why the second row can require the third minimum

Use E = ℚ(i), n = 2, and the ordinary Euclidean norm on ℂ². Let the finite block be diag(1,1/3) above 3 and the identity elsewhere. Its rational row lattice is Λ = ℤ[i]e₁ ⊕ 3ℤ[i]e₂. The first two independent real vectors can be e₁ and ie₁. They span just one line over E. Every row outside that line has length at least 3, attained by 3e₂. The four real minima are 1,1,3,3. The two chosen field rows have squared lengths 1 and 9, whose product equals the covolume 9. The dyadic scales 1 and 4 give the real block diag(1,3/4). Its inverse is diag(1,4/3). With Δ = diag(1,3), the finite block Δβ is the identity above 3 and diag(1,3) elsewhere. Both matrices and their inverses have integral entries at the places where they occur.
