What happens when a group element permutes the weight spaces?

In the rank-extraction argument, the Lie algebra gives m nonzero projectors pi on a space V over the algebraically closed coefficient field K of characteristic zero. They sum to the identity and have pairwise zero products. Their images Vi can have dimension greater than one. We seek a group element in each open part of Γ whose action on the extracted m-dimensional space has simple spectrum. Fix g ∈ Γ and a normal subgroup U of finite index. Let H be the Zariski closure of ρ(U) in the space of matrices, the common zero set of all polynomial equations satisfied by those matrices. The component argument uses the following structural input. There is an invertible u ∈ H such that x = ρ(g)u carries each Vi onto Vπ(i) for a permutation π. The diagonal operator with the lacunary weights ei on these spaces belongs to the tangent space to H at the identity. Both assertions concern the original D-dimensional representation.

Here is why passing to U preserves the tangent direction. Work first among invertible matrices, an open neighborhood of the identity. The closure of a subgroup there is a subgroup since inversion and multiplication by a fixed invertible matrix preserve polynomial zero sets in the general linear group. The closure of ρ(Γ) is the union of finitely many translates of the closure of ρ(U), one for each coset in Γ/U. The distinct translates are disjoint closed sets. Thus the translate containing the identity is also open in the full closure. The two closures agree near the identity and have the same tangent space there. In equations, for each other translate choose a polynomial vanishing on it and nonzero at the identity. Take their product Q, or 1 if there are no others. Regular denominators on the general linear group can be cleared by a power of the determinant. If f vanishes on ρ(U), then fQ vanishes on all of ρ(Γ). Differentiating in any tangent direction at the identity gives Q(I) df = 0 there, since f(I) = 0. This proves the required inclusion directly.

The correction u has to lie in H. Conjugacy of the two systems of projectors in the algebra generated by the tangent matrices first gives an invertible v in that algebra such that ρ(g)v permutes the weight spaces. The factorization theorem for its matrix units writes v = u∑idipi, with every di nonzero. The right factor rescales each weight space, so ρ(g)u gives the same permutation. The left factor is a product of elementary matrices I + N supported in off-diagonal blocks. The structural input places these N in the tangent algebra, hence in the tangent space of H by finite index. Since N² = 0, the formal exponential is simply I + sN. Every relation of H therefore vanishes on the entire line by the formal exponential calculation. Taking s = 1 puts each elementary factor, and hence u, in H. The conjugacy and factorization theorems for the tangent-generated algebra are the group-theoretic inputs here.

On a cycle O of π of length a, the return map xa preserves each weight space in that cycle. Rescale the ith space by a nonzero scalar τi and denote this diagonal operator by t. Then y = xt has return map ya = sOxa on the cycle, where sO = ∏i∈Oτi. Choose a nonzero eigenvector v of the return map of x in one of its weight spaces, with eigenvalue β. Invertibility gives β ≠ 0. For every root λ of λa = βsO, form

wλ = ∑0≤j<a λ−jyjv,   y wλ = λwλ.

Each summand lies in a different weight space. Projection onto the starting space gives v, so wλ is nonzero. Multiplication by y shifts all the terms and the return equation supplies the first term again. The a roots are distinct since βsO is nonzero and the field has characteristic zero. To count the roots across cycles too, consider a second cycle O′ of length b with return eigenvalue β′. A common λ would force sOb = (β′a/βb)sO′a. There are finitely many such equalities to avoid.

Can we avoid every collision while keeping t in H? Start with the formal scalars τi(s) = exp(eis). For any forbidden equality, its constant terms would first force β′a/βb = 1. Its linear terms would then force b∑Oei = a∑O′ei. This contradicts the unequal means of disjoint subsets. Meanwhile the formal diagonal exponential satisfies all equations of H. Indeed the invariant derivative in the given tangent direction preserves the ideal of polynomials vanishing on H, by translating the tangent direction to each group element of ρ(U). Repeated derivatives at the identity give all Taylor coefficients along the exponential, and all vanish. To obtain scalars in K, take the K-algebra generated by the formal scalars and the inverse of the product of the scalars and all the collision differences. It is a nonzero finitely generated algebra inside the fraction field of K[[s]]. A maximal ideal has residue field K by the weak Nullstellensatz. Reduction preserves all the equations of H and keeps the inverted factors nonzero. The resulting t belongs to H and the eigenvalues from the cycles are distinct. The closure H is closed under multiplication, by applying polynomial continuity in each factor successively to ρ(U). Thus M = ut lies in H and ρ(g)M has at least ∑O|O| = m distinct eigenvalues. The Stacks Project, Theorem 10.34.1.

One swap and one fixed line. Let x exchange the first two coordinate vectors and fix the third. Take t = diag(1,4,3). On the first cycle, (xt)² is 4 times the identity and the eigenvalues are 2 and −2. On the fixed line the eigenvalue is 3. Replacing the last entry of t by 2 produces a collision. This calculation illustrates just the cycle test, for which the nonzero diagonal entries are specified.

Why must an actual Galois element in every open coset have simple spectrum?

The matrix M above satisfies the polynomial equations of ρ(U). To pass from that algebraic witness to elements of gU, use the structure of the extracted module W of dimension m. It comes with an equivariant inclusion A into V, a linear left inverse B, and the property that the images of all equivariant maps T from W to V span V. Write r for the action on W, and define Φ(Z) = Bρ(g)ZA. For Z = ρ(u′) with u′ ∈ U, this gives Φ(Z) = r(gu′). Each intertwining relation ρ(g)ZT = TΦ(Z) therefore holds on ρ(U). It is a system of linear equations in the entries of Z, so it holds on H, in particular at M.

Let q be the characteristic polynomial of Φ(M). By Cayley–Hamilton, q(Φ(M)) = 0. Moving powers through each T gives q(ρ(g)M)T = 0. Since their images span V, q(ρ(g)M) = 0 on the entire space. Applying this identity to an eigenvector shows that each of the m distinct eigenvalues constructed above is a root of q. Its degree is m, so all its roots are simple. The Stacks Project, Lemma 10.16.1, Cayley–Hamilton.

Simple spectrum can be tested by a polynomial in the entries of a matrix L. For example, take the determinant of the m-by-m matrix with entries tr(Li+j), starting the indices at zero. If λ₁, …, λm are the eigenvalues with multiplicity, triangularizing L shows that this matrix equals VλtVλ, where row k of Vλ is (1,λk,…,λkm−1). Two equal roots give identical rows. With distinct roots the matrix Vλ is invertible, because a polynomial of degree less than m vanishing at all those roots is zero. Denote the determinant test by Δ. If every element in gU had repeated roots on W, the polynomial Δ(Φ(Z)) would vanish on ρ(U) and hence at M. This contradicts the simple spectrum just proved. Every open neighborhood in Γ contains a coset of the subgroup fixing some finite Galois extension. Such subgroups are normal of finite index, so the simple-spectrum locus is dense in the Krull topology. It is open as well, since the continuous function h ↦ Δ(r(h)) is nonzero there. The finite-extension neighborhoods in the Galois group.
