How do polynomials distinguish the local pieces of a weight family?

The weight-variation argument needs to select one translate of the original form at each point of a compact group. An indicator function selects it exactly. A polynomial in the matrix entries lets us make the same selection to a prescribed ℓ-adic accuracy and keep track of the weights introduced by multiplication. We first construct such a polynomial for two compact sets.

Polynomial separation lemma. Let k be a field with an ultrametric absolute value. Give ks, for finite s, the maximum norm. For disjoint compact sets Z₁ and Z₂ and any η > 0 there is a polynomial P in the s coordinates, with coefficients in k, such that |P(x) − 1| ≤ η on Z₁ and |P(x)| ≤ η on Z₂. We prove the indicator approximation directly. Kaplansky gives the classical approximation theorem for valued fields. I. Kaplansky, The Weierstrass theorem in fields with valuations, Proc. Amer. Math. Soc. 1 (1950), 356–357.

A polynomial in one coordinate. Write α = min(η, 1). Fix a distance δ > 0 and a compact set Y in k. We seek a polynomial p with three estimates. Its absolute value is at most 1 for |x| ≤ δ, it satisfies |p(x) − 1| ≤ α for |x| ≤ δα, and its absolute value is at most α for x ∈ Y with |x| ≥ δ. The first and third estimates give |p| ≤ 1 on all of Y.

The part of Y where |x| ≥ δ is compact. The closed ball of radius |c|/2 about each of its points c is a neighbourhood of c, so finitely many of these balls cover it. Order their centres by increasing absolute value. Start with p = 1. At each centre c replace p(x) by p(x)(1 − x/c)n. There are three regions to check.

  • On the new ball, |1 − x/c| ≤ 1/2 and |x| ≤ |c|. Writing p(x) = ∑j bjxj, choose n so that 2−n∑j |bj| |c|j ≤ α. The new polynomial then has absolute value at most α throughout this ball.
  • On each earlier ball we have |x| ≤ |c| by the ordering of the centres. This also holds on |x| ≤ δ. Thus the new factor has absolute value at most 1 there and preserves all previous upper bounds.
  • On |x| ≤ δα, put u = 1 − x/c. Then |u − 1| ≤ α and |u| ≤ 1. The factorization of un − 1 by u − 1 gives |un − 1| ≤ α. If the old p was within α of 1, the ultrametric triangle inequality applied to p un − 1 = p(un − 1) + (p − 1) gives the same estimate after multiplication.

Combining coordinates and centres. If either compact set is empty, a constant suffices. Otherwise their distance is positive, since the distance function attains its minimum on Z₁ × Z₂. Take δ equal to this distance and cover Z₁ by finitely many balls of radius δα centred at points t of Z₁. For the compact set Y take the union of the coordinate differences xi − ti as x ranges over Z₁ ∪ Z₂, for each coordinate and each centre. Using a single polynomial p for this finite union, set

qt(x) = ∏i p(xi − ti),   P(x) = 1 − ∏t (1 − qt(x)).

Every factor of qt is bounded by 1 on the two compact sets. For x ∈ Z₁, a covering centre t makes each of these factors within α of 1. A product of such factors stays within α of 1 by the same product identity used above. Thus one factor in 1 − P has absolute value at most α and the others are bounded by 1. For x ∈ Z₂, every centre is at least δ away in some coordinate. This makes every qt bounded by α, so the product of 1 − qt is within α of 1. The two errors are at most α ≤ η.

Left cosets of the compact level. Let Γ be a compact topological group and φ a homomorphism into GLa(k), where k is the ℓ-adic coefficient field. Assume that the entries of φ are continuous and the entries of every φ(g) and its inverse have absolute value at most 1. Fix an open subgroup Γ′ containing ker φ. Let H consist of the g such that all entries of φ(g) − I have absolute value at most η. It is an open subgroup. Indeed, closed balls of positive radius are open in an ultrametric field. Also AB − I = (A − I)B + (B − I) and A−1 − I = −A−1(A − I). The integral entries bound both matrix products entry by entry.

Put J = H ∩ Γ′. Its left cosets form a finite open cover by compactness and disjointness. Choose a representative cq for each coset. The coset and its complement are closed in Γ, so their images under φ are compact. If two elements have the same matrix, their quotient belongs to ker φ ⊆ J and they lie in the same left coset. The two compact images are therefore disjoint. Apply the separation lemma to the a² entries. It gives a polynomial Pq within η of the indicator of cqJ at every φ(g). On the selected coset we also have cq−1g ∈ Γ′ and φ(cq−1g) within η of I. In the application the matrix entries are transported from ℂ to k by the fixed field identification. Transporting the polynomial coefficients back to ℂ preserves the evaluation identity, while the error is measured in k.

For example, take Γ = ℤ₃×, φ(x) = x, Γ′ = 1 + 3ℤ₃ and η = 1/3. There are two classes, represented by 1 and −1. At x ≡ 1 modulo 3 the polynomial P+(x) = (1 + x)/2 is congruent to 1, and P−(x) = (1 − x)/2 is congruent to 0. At x ≡ −1 their roles interchange. Thus degree one suffices for a uniform 3-adic error at most 1/3 on both infinite cosets.

The degree bound for the family. For a target error ε we may choose η = min(1, ε/R), where R > 0 bounds all the values of the translated original form in the chosen evaluation coordinates. The indicator polynomials are now fixed. Write dW for a bound on the absolute values of the exponents in the matrix coefficients of the initial algebraic weight W along the circles diag(z, …, z, 1, …, 1). These coefficients are finite Laurent sums in z and there are finitely many such circles. For fixed matrices A and B, every entry of A diag(z, …, z, 1, …, 1)B is affine in z. A monomial in the entries of total degree j therefore contributes circle exponents between 0 and j. Multiplying by a coefficient of W adds at most dW to the absolute shift. Taking the maximum over the finite set of cosets gives the bound used with the target weight ν,

d = dW + maxq degtot(Pq).

The tolerance and the sets being separated enter through the chosen polynomials. The target weight ν can now vary with this same d.
