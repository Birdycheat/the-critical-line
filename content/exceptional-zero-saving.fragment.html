Why does repelling the other zeros save the exceptional factor?

Fix the modulus m, with 8 ∣ m, and put d = log m, Y = log t. In the large-modulus part of the progression estimate, the explicit formula is truncated at T₀ = m12. Its finite zero lists satisfy 1 − κ < Re ρ < 1 and |Im ρ| ≤ T₀ + 1, with a fixed positive κ ≤ 1/20 chosen within the range of the repulsion theorem. Put H = log(m(T₀ + 3)), so d ≤ H ≤ 14d. For a listed zero set v = H(1 − Re ρ). Its weight in the error after division by t is exp(−vY/H). We sum over all characters modulo this one modulus, counting multiplicities and subtracting the one exceptional occurrence if it is present in the lists. Call the sum W. When the exceptional zero occurs, its multiplicity is at least one. Removing one copy leaves every remaining weight nonnegative.

Counting a strip with multiplicities. The two finite-sum detectors prove a fixed-modulus density theorem. It bounds the number of distinct pairs (χ,ρ) with ½ ≤ σ ≤ Re ρ, |Im ρ| ≤ T, and ordinates at least one apart for each individual χ. Its bound is C(mT)24(1−σ)(log(mT))10, for m ≥ 3 and T ≥ 2. At height T = T₀ + 3 and in a strip j < v ≤ j + 1, take σ = max(½, 1 − (j + 1)/H). The bound for separated pairs becomes C exp(24(j + 1))H10.

For one χ, group these zeros by the integer part of their ordinate, and choose a representative from each occupied interval [k, k + 1). Representatives from intervals with even k are separated by at least one. The same holds for odd k. Two applications of the density theorem therefore count the occupied intervals across all χ. For a given χ the explicit formula also bounds the total multiplicity at ordinates within one unit of a listed zero by C₁H. Each occupied interval lies within that distance of its representative. Multiplication gives

total multiplicity in strip j ≤ 2C₁C exp(24(j + 1))H11.

How far to the left must a remaining zero lie?

The zero-free region has a constant cZ > 0. Any zero in the region Re ρ ≥ 1 − cZ/log(m(|Im ρ| + 2)) is the unique simple exceptional zero for the corresponding family, and its occurrence is subtracted from W. The zeros still to be bounded thus satisfy v ≥ cZ. If an exceptional pair (χ₁,β) exists, then β < 1 gives δ = 1 − β > 0 and 0 < η = min(1,δd) ≤ 1. Otherwise set η = 1.

Repulsion estimate. There are fixed positive c₀, c₁, c₂ and an exponent N = 3 such that if δH < c₁, each other zero in the indicated height range with Re ρ ≥ 1 − c₀ satisfies

v ≥ c₂ log(c₁/(δHN)).

For a sufficiently close exceptional zero, θ = δHN/c₁ is less than one, and the displayed inequality reads v ≥ c₂ log(1/θ). This is the additional distance that will pay for θ in the prime sum. In all cases one can choose 0 < θ ≤ 1 satisfying

v ≥ c₂ log(1/θ),   η₀θ ≤ ηHN,   η₀ = min(1, cZ/2, c₁/14).
When does θ = 1 suffice?

Take θ = 1 whenever ηHN ≥ η₀. When there is no exceptional pair, η = 1. If the exceptional β lies outside the stated zero-free region, δ > cZ/log(2m) implies η ≥ min(1,cZ/2). If δH ≥ c₁, use H ≤ 14d to obtain η ≥ min(1,c₁/14). If δHN ≥ c₁, then ηHN = min(HN, δdHN) ≥ min(1,c₁), since d ≥ 1. In the remaining case repulsion applies with θ = δHN/c₁. Then η₀θ ≤ 1 ≤ HN and η₀θ ≤ δdHN, giving the required comparison.

Summing the strips. Take Y ≥ Kd log(d + 2), with K sufficiently large for the fixed constants. Split exp(−vY/H) into four equal factors. Since H ≤ 14d, this threshold makes Y/(4H) at least 24 + log 2 and at least 1/c₂. It also makes cZY/(4H) ≥ 11 log H. Thus the first factor is at most exp(−24j)2−j because v > j. The second is at most θ by repulsion. The third is at most H−11 by the ordinary zero-free region. The fourth is at most exp(−cY/d) with c = cZ/56. Multiplying by the strip count cancels both exp(24j) and H11. Summing 2−j over j ≥ 0 costs at most two.

The result so far is W ≤ C′θ exp(−cY/d). The comparison for θ costs at most (14d)N/η₀ times η. Enlarging K to at least 2N/c guarantees N log d ≤ cY/(2d). Half the exponential decay absorbs dN, leaving

W ≤ C″η exp(−cY/(2d)).

For a numerical check of the repulsion step alone, take θ = 1/8, v = c₂ log 8 and c₂Y/(4H) = 2. The second factor is exp(−2 log 8) = 1/64, within the allowance 1/8. Only one quarter of the exponential was used in this calculation.

Under the two conditions Y ≥ Kd log(d + 2) and d > cA√Y, with cA > 0 fixed by the small-modulus estimate, the differenced explicit formula bounds the sum of the absolute character errors by 22tW plus a constant times t/m². For the latter term, the exceptional-zero lower bound gives η ≥ c′/m. Also exp(−cA²Y/d) ≥ 1/m in this range. Together the two errors are bounded by a constant times tη exp(−c′Y/d), with c′ = min(cZ/112, cA²) > 0. Character orthogonality divides this sum by φ(m) in the weighted progression estimate. The small-modulus range there uses its separate square-root exponential bound.
