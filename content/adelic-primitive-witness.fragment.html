How can a primitive column and its dual witness both be kept bounded?

Let E/F be the chosen CM quadratic extension and put K = 𝔸F ⊗F E. A column y with M entries in K is primitive here if there is a row z₀ with z₀·y = ∑i (z₀)iyᵢ = 1. This is ordinary row-by-column multiplication. In the isotropic-line argument the Hermitian form supplies such a row. If y = g−1e and h(f,e) = 1, then z₀ = (g−1f)∗J takes value one on y, where J is the matrix of the form. We want one compact set Ω, chosen before y, that contains both a rescaled column and a row witnessing its primitivity. Rows and columns are identified with their ordered lists of M coordinates for membership in Ω.

For every primitive y, there are a ∈ K× and z with ay ∈ Ω,   z ∈ Ω,   z·(ay) = 1.

At a complex component pick a coordinate yᵣ of largest modulus. It is nonzero because the local dot product can equal one. Take a = yᵣ−1 and take z to be the coordinate row with 1 in position r and zeros elsewhere. Every entry of ay has modulus at most one, entry r is exactly one, and z·(ay) = 1. At infinity there are finitely many complex components, and each can choose its own r. Both tuples now belong to a finite product of closed unit discs.

At a finite place where the quadratic algebra is a field, use the same largest-coordinate rule with the multiplicative size |Norm(x)| in the base local field. The base valuation ring is compact because its residue classes modulo each uniformizer power form a finite cover by balls of shrinking radius, and it is complete. To see compactness of the norm ball directly in coordinates, write x = u+wθ with θ² = Δ. The norm is u²−Δw², nonzero on the compact sphere max(|u|,|w|) = 1 because Δ is nonsquare. Its absolute value has a positive minimum there. Dividing by a coordinate of maximal absolute value and using homogeneity of degree two bounds max(|u|,|w|) wherever |Norm(x)| ≤ 1. The ball is therefore closed and bounded in the two-dimensional local field coordinates, hence compact. At a split place the algebra is a product of two fields. Choose a maximum separately on each side, giving one invertible pair of scalars and one pair of coordinate rows. The witness may select different positions in the two factors.

For example, over ℚ(i) at the split prime 5, take k ≥ 1. Write a local two-entry column as y = ((5−k,0),(0,5k)). Each single entry has a zero component. The scalar pair (5k,5−k) sends y to ((1,0),(0,1)). The row with these same two entries evaluates to (1,1), the multiplicative identity. The freedom to replace the row already matters at a complex place. For real R ≥ 1 take y = (R,0) and z₀ = (R−1,R²). After rescaling by 1/R, transporting the old row gives (1,R³). Choosing the new row (1,0) bounds both tuples for the whole sequence.

Why do the local scalings form a single idèle?

The local compact sets must fit the restricted product topology. Fix an F-basis of E and let Λv consist of tuples whose coordinates in that basis lie in the integer ring of Fᵥ. Away from a fixed finite set of places the local compact set can be chosen to be exactly Λv. To see this, first use a basis 1,θ with θ² = Δ ∈ F×. Exclude the places over 2, those where Δ is not a unit, and the finitely many denominator primes of the change of coordinates to the chosen basis. At a remaining place where Δ is nonsquare its reduction is nonsquare too. Indeed, an odd-residue-characteristic square root of a unit modulo the uniformizer lifts digit by digit because the derivative 2x at that root is a unit. Thus

|u²−Δw²|v = max(|u|v,|w|v)².

For unequal sizes the largest term gives the value. For equal nonzero sizes, cancellation would give a square root of Δ in the residue field on dividing by w². Hence the local construction produces integral coordinates in the basis 1,θ. In the split case let s² = Δ. The two components of u+wθ are u±sw. Both s and 2 are units, so taking their half-sum and their difference divided by 2s recovers integral coordinates from integral components. The fixed change of basis preserves integrality away from its denominator primes. These conditions leave only finitely many exceptional places.

Fix this finite set S independently of y. Use the compact archimedean box from the first step, the local compact sets constructed there at the finite places in S, and Λv everywhere else. Their product is a compact subset Ω of KM. Indeed it lies in one restricted-product chart with the ordinary product topology, where each local lattice is compact and the product of compact sets is compact. Andrew Sutherland, Number Theory I, Proposition 25.6 and Definition 25.7.

Now take an actual witness z₀ for y. Their components both lie in Λv at almost every finite place. Wherever this holds outside S, choose aᵥ = 1 and keep zᵥ = (z₀)v. At the finitely many other places use the local construction to choose aᵥ and zᵥ. Thus aᵥ and aᵥ−1 both equal one at almost all finite places. They assemble to mutually inverse elements of K, so a is an idèle. The rows zᵥ assemble to an adelic row because they are integral almost everywhere. In every component zᵥ·(aᵥyᵥ) = 1. Equality of adèles can be tested in all components, giving the global identity, with ay,z in the fixed Ω.

One example explains why the input row must itself be adelic. Take a rank-one column over ℚ(i) whose component at each rational prime p is p, and whose complex component is one. The column is integral at all finite places and each local entry is invertible in the local algebra. Yet the only possible dual has component 1/p at every p. It fails the restricted-product integrality condition, so this column has no adelic witness.

For the shell estimate, y is isotropic, and ay remains isotropic. The two compactly bounded inputs ay and z now feed the continuous construction of a hyperbolic partner. Its image on Ω × Ω gives the compact family of pairs to which the adelic Gram-fibre lifting argument is applied. The row normalization by itself requires only the displayed primitivity identity. If M = 0 that identity is impossible, so there is no primitive input in that rank.
