How does an away-from-p coordinate change the polarisation?

To realize a triple in the marked quotient, we must construct one integral lattice with both prescribed finite readings. Let V be the reference rational space, E₀ its alternating form, and ρ the action of the rational algebra commuting with the order. Its involution ∗ is the adjoint for E₀. A rational form class is represented by an invertible self-adjoint element β and a choice of trivialisations. Write Bβ(u,v) = E₀(ρ(β−1)u,v). This form is nonsingular since ρ(β−1) is invertible. Moving the self-adjoint operator to the other argument and swapping arguments negates the result, so the form is alternating. Its adjunction under the order follows because ρ(β−1) commutes with that action.

Extend scalars to the finite adeles away from p. For a representative b of the finite coordinate, its action is a similitude of E₀ with multiplier ν(b). The chosen trivialisation supplies an invertible x and an adelic unit τ with xβx∗ = τ. Here an adelic unit means an invertible adele. Multiplying the identity on the left by x−1 and on the right by x gives βx∗x = τ, since τ is central. Thus x∗x = τβ−1. The finite reading applies ρ(x) first and b−1 second. Calling the resulting transformation T, we obtain

E₀(Tu,Tv) = c Bβ(u,v),   c = ν(b)−1τ.

Indeed, removing b−1 from both arguments contributes its similitude factor. Moving ρ(x) across the other argument leaves ρ(x∗x), which gives τ times the twisted form.

Which rational scale satisfies both finite conditions?

Write ℤ(p) for the rationals whose reduced denominators are prime to p. The given local lattice M at p comes with a positive rational scale s. A rational w belongs to M exactly when sBβ(w,u) ∈ ℤ(p) for every u ∈ M. Away from p, the same rational form has the multiplier c just computed. We need one rational number to normalize both.

s₁ = pvp(s) ∏q ≠ p qvq(cq),   ε = s₁/c.

Here vq(q) = 1, and the product runs over rational primes. Both c and its inverse are integral at all but finitely many primes, so the product is finite. At every q ≠ p, ε and ε−1 are integral. Thus s₁Bβ equals ε times the transported reference form away from p. At p, the ratio s₁/s is a unit of ℤ(p), so the membership test for M is identical with either scale. The positive sign makes s₁ unique, because the finite prime valuations of a nonzero rational number determine its absolute value.

Why adjust the scale at the distinguished prime?

Take p = 3 and s = 9/5. Away from 3 let c have component ½ at 2, component 5 at 5 and component 1 elsewhere. The product away from 3 gives 5/2. On the rational value y = ⅓, the prescribed test gives sy = 3/5 ∈ ℤ(3), while (5/2)y = 5/6 has a factor 3 in the denominator. The common scale is s₁ = 45/2. Its ratio to s is 25/2, a unit at 3, and s₁y = 15/2 passes the test.

Why does the transported rational lattice meet every required residue class?

Let Λ̂ be the product away from p of the completions of the reference integral lattice Λ₀. For every integer n > 0 and every finite target z away from p, we need a rational vector w such that

z − T(1 ⊗ w) ∈ nΛ̂.

Write n = pkn′ with n′ prime to p. Since p is a unit at every place of Λ̂, nΛ̂ = n′Λ̂. In a ℤ-basis of Λ₀, clearing denominators of the entries of T gives one positive m prime to p with mTΛ̂ ⊆ Λ̂. Approximate T−1z by a rational vector modulo n′mΛ̂. After applying T, the error lies in n′Λ̂ as required. The factor m matters even in one dimension. Multiplication by ½ at 2 can carry an error divisible by 2 to a unit.

How is a rational representative chosen before applying the transformation?

For the target v = T−1z, choose d > 0 prime to p with dv ∈ Λ̂. At each prime power dividing dn′m, each coordinate of dv has an ordinary integral residue. The Chinese remainder theorem gives one a ∈ Λ₀ agreeing with all those residues in a basis of Λ₀. Then w = a/d differs from v by a vector in n′mΛ̂. The density statement allows denominators in w. The integral level construction will impose the condition at p.
