What can be invisible to all the nondegenerate coefficients?

Take the additive group of symmetric 2 × 2 matrices over the field of three elements. This finite example has 27 elements. Set ζ = exp(2πi/3), T = diag(1,0) and F(B) = ζB₁₁. The normalized β-coefficient is the average of ζtr((T−β)B). At β = T all 27 terms are 1. For β ≠ T, at least one of the three matrix coordinates has a nonzero coefficient in this linear form. Its sum is 1 + ζ + ζ² = 0. The off-diagonal entry appears with multiplier 2, which is invertible in this field. Hence every invertible β has coefficient zero, although F(0) = 1. The missing information sits at the rank-one frequency T.

How does the proof rule out such a discrepancy? At a real place selected using the Witt-index inequality, choose a compactly supported test function η whose Fourier transform vanishes on singular Hermitian matrices. Suppose a continuous automorphic function F has zero full-rank coefficients for all g. Integrate its unipotent translates against η. The β-coefficient of this convolution factors into the β-coefficient of F and the transform of η at the associated local frequency. For invertible β the first factor is zero. For singular β the associated local matrix is singular too, so the second factor is zero. All Fourier coefficients of the convolution now vanish, so Fourier uniqueness on the compact Hermitian quotient makes the convolution zero. Density and continuity give the same vanishing with a group translation on either side of the unipotent argument.
