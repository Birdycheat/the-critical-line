Why do the coordinates of one period generate a power of ℓ?

Fix the datum and its primitive period τ, with φ(τ) = e(E)τ. Fix an integer h ≥ 0. For the topological basis just constructed, form the ideal I in 𝔖 generated by all coordinates cλ(τh). We will show that I contains a power of ℓ. Membership in an ideal uses finite linear combinations. Thus, even if the period has infinitely many nonzero coordinates, only finitely many will be needed to recover that power of ℓ.

Saturate I by ℓ, writing P for the elements x such that ℓax ∈ I for some a ≥ 0. Its image modulo ℓ is principal in k[[u]]. Lift a generator to g ∈ P. For x ∈ P, subtract a multiple of g to get ℓy ∈ P. Saturation gives y ∈ P, so P = (g) + ℓP. Nakayama's lemma gives P = (g) since 𝔖 is Noetherian local. The coordinates separate elements and τh ≠ 0, so g ≠ 0. If g = ℓy, saturation would give y ∈ (g). Cancelling g would make ℓ a unit. Thus ℓ ∤ g. Stacks Project, Lemma 10.20.1.

How Frobenius restricts the generator. Write φ for Frobenius on the two rings. It takes u to uℓ and satisfies φ(τ) = e(E)τ. Every coordinate of τh is divisible by g. Apply φ to its finite basis expansion modulo Jm. Each coordinate of the resulting finite sum is divisible by φ(g), and the error still belongs to JmA. Indeed φ fixes ℓ and φ(E) ≡ Eℓ modulo ℓ, so φ(J) ⊆ J. Each coordinate of the error belongs to Jm by 𝔖-linearity. Passing to the separated quotient 𝔖/(φ(g)) gives φ(g) ∣ Ehci(τh) for every i. This holds for every element of I in place of the coordinate. Choose r with ℓrg ∈ I. Since φ(g) reduces to the nonzero series ḡℓ, and ℓ is prime in 𝔖, we may cancel each ℓ as follows. If ℓx = φ(g)z, reduction modulo ℓ in the domain k[[u]] gives ℓ ∣ z. Division by ℓ yields x ∈ (φ(g)). Repeating gives φ(g) ∣ Ehg. The root calculation below makes g a unit. Multiplying ℓrg by g−1 gives the required ideal membership. Stacks Project, Krull's intersection theorem.

Why the Frobenius divisibility forces a unit

The chosen Eisenstein polynomial E has a nonzero term of degree i with ℓ ∤ i. Equivalently, E ∉ W(k)[uℓ]. To see where this hypothesis enters, write g as a unit times a monic distinguished polynomial D by Weierstrass preparation. Write σ for Witt Frobenius on W(k) and Dσ for its action on the coefficients. Since Dσ(uℓ) is distinguished, its divisibility into EhD holds already for polynomials. If u divided D with order a > 0, the orders of the divisor and dividend at zero would be ℓa and a, because E(0) ≠ 0. Thus D(0) ≠ 0. Sharifi, Iwasawa Theory, Theorem 2.2.3 and Corollary 2.2.7.

If D has positive degree, choose a root x of largest absolute value ρ. Distinguishedness gives 0 < ρ < 1. Extend σ to the algebraic closure of the unramified coefficient field and let z be the image of x. Every conjugate z′ of z has absolute value ρ, by uniqueness of the extended absolute value. For yℓ = z′, we have Dσ(yℓ) = 0 and |y| = ρ1/ℓ > ρ. Hence D(y) ≠ 0 and E(y)h = 0. For h = 0 this is already impossible. For h > 0 the polynomial E vanishes on every root of m(uℓ), where m is the minimal polynomial of z. These roots are distinct in characteristic zero because z ≠ 0. Thus m(uℓ) divides E. Eisenstein irreducibility and monicity give equality, contradicting the prescribed coefficient. Consequently D = 1 and g is a unit. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, Theorem 7.38 and Corollary 7.40.

For example, over ℤ₃[[u]] take E = u³ − 3 and g = u − 3. This E is Eisenstein, but all its nonzero terms have degree divisible by 3. Here φ(g) = E divides Eg, although g is a nonunit and 3 ∤ g. This tests the extra hypothesis of the criterion.

Why does one power of ℓ suffice at every precision?

After choosing the integral basis, the integral comparison gives an approximate right inverse over A = Ainf. Its product with the comparison matrix is a power of the period τ times the identity. To get a statement over 𝔖 = W(k)[[u]], we need one 𝔖-linear map from A to 𝔖 that sends this scalar to a power of ℓ. Write e for evaluation from 𝔖 to A.

Uniform descent of a right inverse. Fix ℓ, the Breuil–Kisin datum over K/ℚℓ, its primitive period τ and an integer h ≥ 0. There exists r ≥ 0 with the following property. Take any dimensions d, d′ and precision n, a d × d′ matrix R over 𝔖 and a d′ × d matrix Z over A. If the first congruence below holds in A, there exists a d′ × d matrix G over 𝔖 satisfying the second in 𝔖.

e(R)Z ≡ τhId (mod ℓn),   RG ≡ ℓrId (mod ℓn).

Apply the coordinate-ideal result to τh. Choose a finite set of indices and coefficients aλ ∈ 𝔖 with ∑aλcλ(τh) = ℓr. This produces a single 𝔖-linear functional f = ∑aλcλ on the entire period ring. For any integral column, apply f entry by entry. Multiplication on the left by e(R) commutes with this operation because each entry of R belongs to 𝔖. An error in ℓnA maps into ℓn𝔖. The second congruence therefore follows from the first with G = f(Z). This argument chooses r and f before either matrix dimension, the precision, or the later layer.

For a two-coordinate calculation take 𝔖 = ℤ₃[[u]] and the free 𝔖-algebra 𝔖[w]/(w² + 3), with basis 1, w. The element α = 3 + uw has coordinate ideal (3, u). The constant-coordinate functional f takes α to 3. No 𝔖-linear functional can take α to 1, since (3, u) is proper. With the row R = (3, u) and column Z = (1, w)t we get RZ = α; applying f gives the column (1, 0)t and product 3. This finite free algebra illustrates the coordinate step in the descent. The period identity and Frobenius supplied the coordinate-ideal result in the arithmetic case.

For a tower of height H the exponent is h = 2H. At precision n a deeper layer m ≥ n supplies Rn and an integral product Z = QY in the displayed order. Applying the same f gives Gn. If n ≤ r the second congruence also admits G = 0. The argument supplies an exponent valid at all levels without computing its least possible value.
