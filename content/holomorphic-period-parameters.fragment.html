Coordinates for a polarized period, up to change of frame

Fix the marked lattice L, its alternating form E and the representation ρ of the order. Let P₀ be a period of this lattice. Its values span ℂg over ℝ. Choose the positive Hermitian form H₀(z,w) = ztM₀w whose imaginary part on the lattice is E. Complex conjugation is written with a bar, the adjoint of a matrix as *, and the involution of the order as a ↦ a*. We seek coordinates which assign the same point to periods differing by a complex change of frame.

Extend a second period P from the basis of P₀(L) to a real-linear map F of ℂg. Write F(z) = Az + Bz. The columns of A and B are, respectively, the half-difference and half-sum of F(eⱼ) and iF(ieⱼ). They depend holomorphically on the finitely many period values on a lattice basis. Near P₀ the matrix A is invertible since it equals the identity at the centre. Put C = A−1B. Left multiplication of both matrices by the same change of frame cancels in C. Conversely equal C gives an explicit change of frame between the two periods.

S = CtM₀,   St = S,   Sρ(a*) = ρ(a)*S   for every a ∈ 𝒪.

These two linear equations cut out a complex vector space of dimension d′. A neighbourhood of zero in that space parametrizes admissible periods up to change of frame. Indeed, recover C = (SM₀−1)t and use the representative Q(S)(x) = P₀(x) + CP₀(x). This is an invertible real deformation of a lattice for small S. Choosing a complex basis of the solution space gives coordinates Z(P) ∈ ℂd′. Both the representative and the coordinate map vary holomorphically.

How the polarization and the order give the two equations

After complexifying the underlying real space, write (z,u) with u the independent coordinate replacing the conjugate of z. The complexified alternating form pairs (z,u) with (w,v) as (ztM₀v − wtM₀u)/(2i). The antiholomorphic subspace for the new coordinate z + Cu consists of (−Cu,u). Vanishing of the alternating form on two such vectors is exactly symmetry of CtM₀. Conjugation gives vanishing on two holomorphic vectors as well. Thus the transported real form is of type (1,1), which means it is invariant under multiplication of both entries by i. It is the imaginary part of the Hermitian form E(iz,w) + iE(z,w) in these coordinates. Positivity persists near zero by continuity on the compact unit sphere.

Order equivariance is Cρ(a) = ρ(a)C. Transposing and using the polarization identity ρ(a)tM₀ = M₀ρ(a*) gives the second equation. Each step is reversible because M₀ is invertible.

Why the period domain has the dimension of the base

Let X be a smooth quasi-compact complex algebraic base of pure dimension d, with the polarized family, order action and sections killed by N > 0 described above. Assume fineness in the following sense. Every point has an admissible marked period description, every admissible period occurs, and equivalent marked periods determine the same point. Fix an admissible marked lattice admitting a period P₀. Then d equals the dimension d′ of its period coordinates.

Choose a chart c ↦ y₁(c) near a point realizing P₀. The local lifting theorem gives periods P₁(c) on that fixed lattice. Shrink the chart until Φ(c) = Z(P₁(c)) is defined. This holomorphic map is injective. Equal coordinates give equivalent marked periods, hence equal points, and the chart is injective. If d exceeded d′, adjoining zero coordinates would give an injective holomorphic map from an open subset of ℂd into ℂd with singular derivative everywhere. This contradicts the inverse theorem for injective holomorphic maps. Lebl, Theorem 1.6.6.

For the reverse inequality, a neighbourhood of zero in period coordinates is covered by countably many holomorphic images of open sets in ℂd. If d < d′, each image has zero volume in ℝ2d′. On a compact cube inside the domain the derivative is bounded. Subdivide into cubes of side δ. Their images lie in at most a constant times δ−2d balls of radius a constant times δ, with total volume tending to zero. Countably many cubes cover the domain. The countable union of images therefore cannot contain an open ball.

Why a countable set of frame changes covers a whole open set

The complex points of X have a countable basis for their topology. A finite affine cover embeds chart by chart into finite-dimensional complex coordinate spaces. Thus countably many lifted charts suffice to cover X(ℂ). Let their period families be Pᵢ(c) on lattices Lᵢ. The integral isometries w from L to Lᵢ form a countable set since they are determined by finitely many integer vectors. The invertible complex matrices commuting with the order have a countable dense subset in their matrix topology. For each such matrix H, take the holomorphic map c ↦ Z(HPᵢ(c)w) on the open set where its period values are close enough to P₀ for the coordinate chart.

For every sufficiently small S, its representative Q(S) occurs over some chart point. The fibre comparison supplies a marked isometry and a commuting frame giving precisely this representative. Choose a frame from the countable dense set close enough to preserve the finitely many strict inequalities defining the chart. The coordinate of the resulting period is still exactly S, since coordinates are invariant under change of frame. This gives actual coverage by the countable family.

Recovering the holomorphic point and its exponential from the periods

Keep the hypotheses of the dimension theorem and suppose the marking is full of level N ≥ 3. Let P(b) be a holomorphic family of admissible periods on the fixed marked lattice over an open set of ℂm. For each b choose a realizing point y(b) and an exponential eb. Then y is holomorphic in b and eb(z) is jointly holomorphic in (b,z).

Fix b₀ and lift a base chart through its chosen point with central period P(b₀). Denote its periods and joint exponential by P₁(c) and e₁(c,z). As above Φ(c) = Z(P₁(c)) is injective. Its source and target have the same dimension, so it has a local holomorphic inverse. Set c(b) = Φ−1(Z(P(b))). Equality of the two period coordinates gives the unique complex frame K(b) satisfying P₁(c(b)) = K(b)P(b). The holomorphic inverse theorem used here.

Choose g central period columns forming a complex basis and write U(b) for those same columns of P(b). They remain a basis nearby. If U₁(b) denotes the corresponding columns of P₁(c(b)), then K = U₁U−1 is holomorphic. Separation identifies the two base points. On that fibre, composing the chart exponential with K realizes the original period and sends its marked division points to the prescribed sections. Full-level uniqueness identifies this composite with the chosen exponential. Thus

y(b) = y₁(c(b)),   eb(z) = e₁(c(b), K(b)z).

Both right-hand sides are holomorphic near each b₀, which proves the assertion for the original pointwise choices.
