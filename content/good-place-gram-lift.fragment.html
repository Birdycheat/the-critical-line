Which denominator is a unit at a finite place?

Let E/F be a CM quadratic extension, with conjugation z ↦ z̄. Let X be a rational frame of n columns in an M-dimensional nonsingular Hermitian space. Assume its n-by-n Gram matrix has nonzero determinant. The columns are then a basis of their span, with n ≤ M. Suppose an adelic frame Y has the same Gram matrix. We want an isometry carrying X to Y that preserves the coordinate lattice at almost every finite place. Fix a common rational change of columns that makes the Gram matrix diagonal, with nonzero entries β₁,…,βₙ in F. Consider one pair of columns of equal squared length β. The real-place construction divides by δ = β − λ̄t, where t = h(y,x) and λλ̄ = 1. To keep this division integral, we need δ to be a unit.

Here are four possible values of λ, fixed once for the extension. Choose a nonzero θ with θ̄ = −θ by subtracting the conjugate of any element of E outside F. Then d = θ² lies in F× and 1, θ is an F-basis of E. Take s ∈ F× with s² ≠ d. Put P = (s²+d)/(s²−d) and Q = 2s/(s²−d). Expanding the squares gives P²−dQ² = 1, so α = P+Qθ has norm one and Q ≠ 0. Our choices are 1, −1, α and −α.

At a finite place v, write 𝒪v for the integers of Fᵥ and Rᵥ for the ring of integers of E ⊗F Fᵥ, a product of two valuation rings at a split place. For the moment suppose 2, d, Q and β are units of 𝒪v and P is integral. Then θ is an integral unit. Each t ∈ Rᵥ can be written a+bθ with a = (t+t̄)/2 and b = (t−t̄)/(2θ) in 𝒪v. Both quotients are integral and fixed by conjugation. For λ = p+qθ, multiply δ by its conjugate to obtain a scalar in the base ring,

Nλ = δδ̄ = (β−pa+qbd)² − d(qa−pb)².

Suppose all four norms vanish in the residue field. Taking differences gives N−1−N1 = 4βa and N−α−Nα = 4β(Pa−Qbd). The first forces a ≡ 0, and the second then forces b ≡ 0 because Qd is a unit. But now N1 ≡ β² ≠ 0. Thus at least one norm is a unit in 𝒪v. For its phase, δ−1 = δ̄/Nλ belongs to Rᵥ.

Why two signs can fail. At an inert place the residue algebra is a field. If β−t and β+t both vanished there, their sum 2β would vanish. At a split place each sign can fail in a different component. For example, 5 splits in ℚ(i). In the residue algebra 𝔽₅ × 𝔽₅, conjugation swaps the factors and i = (2,−2). Take β = 1 and t = (1,−1). The choices λ = 1 and −1 give δ = (0,2) and (2,0). With θ = i and s = 1, we have α = i. For λ = i both components of δ equal 3 modulo 5, so its norm equals 4 modulo 5 and division is integral.

An integral isometry at almost every finite place

We can now specify a finite set S outside which every step works. Include all places above 2 and all ramified places. Include the places where any diagonal entry βᵢ, d or Q fails to be a unit, or P fails to be integral. Also include any place where the form matrix J, its inverse, the rational frame, or the common change of columns and its inverse have a nonintegral entry. Each fixed number-field entry has finitely many denominator primes, and each nonzero diagonal entry, as well as d and Q, is a unit away from finitely many primes. Finally include the finitely many places where the given adelic frame has a nonintegral entry. For a compact family of frames this last set can be chosen uniformly. The open sets of tuples integral outside a given finite set cover the family, so finitely many suffice, and we take the union of their exceptional sets. Sutherland, The ring of adeles, strong approximation, §25.3, Definition 25.7.

Fix v outside S. Write h(z,w) = z*Jw, where * is conjugate transpose. For one pair of integral columns x, y with common squared length β, choose λ by the four-norm test and put u = x−λy. For any column w and scalar c, let Tw,c(z) = z−wc h(w,z). The two maps we need are

A = Tu, 1/δ,   B = Ty, (1−λ̄)/β,   x ⟼ Ax = λy ⟼ BAx = y.

Indeed h(u,x) = δ, and B multiplies y by λ̄. To see that the maps preserve the lattice in both directions, write Lz = w h(w,z). This operator is self-adjoint for h and L² = h(w,w)L. Multiplying (1−c̄L)(1−cL) therefore gives the identity whenever c+c̄ = cc̄ h(w,w). This equality holds for A because h(u,u) = δ+δ̄, and for B because (1−λ̄)(1−λ) = 2−λ−λ̄. Thus each map is unitary with inverse Tw,c̄. Every coefficient of the maps and their inverses is integral since w, J and c are integral.

Start with the identity. If g has matched some columns, use x = gXᵢ and y = Yᵢ for the next pair of diagonalized columns. Every earlier matched column is perpendicular to both x and y, so A and B fix it. Replacing g by BAg matches one more column and preserves the lattice. After the last column, undo the common column change to get gᵥX = Yᵥ for the original frames. At each v outside S, gᵥ belongs to the unitary stabilizer of RᵥM, where M is the ambient dimension. The places above 2 enter S because the norm differences divide by 4. The adelic orbit argument supplies the transformations at the remaining places and assembles the local maps.
