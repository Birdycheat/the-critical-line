Why do the lattice inequalities force positive definiteness?

In the count of frames on a common face, the only initial constraint on the Hermitian forms is a lower bound on lattice vectors. Let L be the totally complex number field, let N ≥ 1, and put d = [L∶ℚ]N. Choose one embedding σw in each complex conjugate pair. For a family of Hermitian matrices hw indexed by these places, set qh(y) = ∑w σw(y)*hwσw(y), where * denotes conjugate transpose. Every summand is real. Suppose qh(y) ≥ 1 for all nonzero y ∈ 𝒪LN. An integral basis of 𝒪L in each coordinate identifies these vectors with ℤd. Denote by G the real symmetric matrix of their quadratic form in this basis. We claim

G is positive definite,   1 ≤ (4d)d det G.

First, nonnegativity on all integer points implies nonnegativity on rational points by clearing denominators and using homogeneity of degree two. Density and continuity give nonnegativity on ℝd. Strict positivity on rational points alone could leave an irrational null direction. The uniform lower bound of one rules this out, as the following volume calculation shows.

For ε > 0, put Gε = G+εI. It is positive definite and still takes value at least one on each nonzero integral vector. Write Gε = BtB using its positive square root. The lattice Bℤd has covolume |det B|. Set t = |det B|1/d. The symmetric cube [−2t,2t]d has volume 4d|det B|, strictly greater than the Minkowski threshold 2d|det B|. It therefore contains some Bc with 0 ≠ c ∈ ℤd. Its squared Euclidean length is at most 4dt² and at least one. Raising to the power d gives 1 ≤ (4d)d det Gε. The determinant is a polynomial in ε, so the same inequality holds at ε = 0. The nonnegative eigenvalues of G have positive product and hence are all positive. Sutherland, Theorem 14.13, Minkowski’s lattice point theorem.

This also gives positivity at each individual place. The real coordinates in the integral basis parameterize the entire product ∏w ℂN as a real vector space. Indeed the matrix of all embeddings of the basis is invertible by its nonzero discriminant. Solving for a tuple whose entries at conjugate embeddings are conjugate gives real coefficients, by uniqueness of the solution. In particular any nonzero z at one chosen place, with zeros elsewhere, comes from a nonzero real coordinate vector. Its value for G is z*hwz > 0. Every hw is therefore positive definite. Sutherland, Proposition 12.6, the embedding matrix and the discriminant.

Where does the discriminant enter the frame bound?

Write D = |disc L|, m = [L∶ℚ] and P = ∏w det hw. The factors of P are positive by the real Gram estimate. To compute their product from G, take an integral basis b₁,…,bₘ and form T = (σ(bⱼ))σ,j using all embeddings. The matrix of traces TrL/ℚ(bᵢbⱼ) is TtT, so its determinant gives (det T)² = disc L, and |det T|² = D.

Apply T separately to each of the N coordinates, and group the output by embedding. Call the resulting square matrix U. Reordering the coordinates leaves |det U|² = DN. In this output space form a block diagonal matrix H with hw at the chosen embedding and its conjugate, equivalently its transpose, at the conjugate embedding. An entry of U*HU is a sum over all embeddings. The two summands for a conjugate pair add to twice the real part of the chosen summand, precisely that pair’s contribution to 2G. Thus U*HU = 2G. The two blocks in each conjugate pair have the same real determinant, so det H = P². Taking determinants now gives

2d det G = DN P²,   1 ≤ DN dd P.

For the inequality, substitute the identity in 1 ≤ (4d)ddet G. The right side becomes (2d)dDNP². Because d ≥ 2 and D ≥ 1, this is at most (ddDNP)². Taking the positive square root proves the assertion. Now let the columns of an integral matrix A be independent common minimal vectors. Their values for qh are one. Hence each diagonal entry of each pulled-back form σw(A)*hwσw(A) is at most one. Hadamard’s determinant inequality bounds its determinant by their product, hence by one. Multiplying over the places gives |NormL/ℚ(det A)|P ≤ 1. The preceding lower bound for P therefore bounds the index |NormL/ℚ(det A)| by DNdd in the count of common frames. Writing J = DNdd, the same calculation gives a lower bound at each place. The product of the pulled-back determinants is at least P ≥ 1/J, since the nonzero integral determinant has absolute norm at least one. Every factor is at most one, so each individual determinant is at least 1/J. This is the bound used in the coordinate estimate. Hadamard’s inequality here follows by writing the positive matrix as a Gram matrix and orthogonalizing the vectors that it represents. Each projected length is at most the original vector length.

For a check with a nonreal matrix entry, take L = ℚ(i), N = 2 and h with diagonal entries 1 and upper-right entry i/2. Its eigenvalues are ½ and 3/2. On a Gaussian integer vector (a+bi,c+ei) it takes the integer value a²+b²+c²+e²−ae+bc, so every nonzero vector satisfies the bound of one. With the integral basis (1,i), the real Gram matrix separates into the (a,e) and (b,c) blocks, both of determinant ¾. Thus det G = 9/16. Here d = 4, D = 4 and P = ¾, giving 2⁴ · 9/16 = 9 = 4² · (¾)².
