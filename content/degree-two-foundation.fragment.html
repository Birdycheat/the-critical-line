How an integral boundary matrix bounds torsion

The character count above uses the orders of the torsion subgroups of two integral homology groups. A torsion class is killed by a nonzero integer. An infinite free summand in homology can therefore coexist with the finite quantity we need to bound. Start with a free lattice E = ℤr, with r ≤ A, and any set of integral relation vectors. Write Λ for their integer span. Suppose each relation has sum of absolute coordinates at most b, where b ≥ 1. The bound used in the proof is

|tors(E/Λ)| ≤ bA.

If we have r independent relation vectors, put them in a square matrix V. The sublattice they generate has index |det V|. Expanding the determinant as a signed sum over permutations and taking absolute values gives a sum of products. Allowing every choice of one column for each row only increases this sum, and the enlarged sum factors as ∏i ∑j |Vij|. This proves the bound br. Suppose instead that the relation vectors span only a proper rational subspace. Choose a maximal independent subset and complete it to a rational basis using standard unit vectors. Let K be the integer span of that completed list. Its determinant still has absolute value at most br, since every added vector has length one. To compare torsion, choose a lift in E for each torsion class in E/Λ and reduce its lift modulo K. If two images coincide, the difference x of their lifts lies in K. Their difference class is torsion, so some nonzero multiple of x belongs to Λ. Consequently x lies in the rational span of the original relations. In the completed basis every coefficient of an added vector must then vanish. Because x belongs to K, the remaining coefficients are integers. Thus x is an integer combination of selected relations and the two classes were equal. This injects the set of torsion classes into the finite set E/K. For example, impose the three relations (2,0,0), (0,2,0), (1,1,0) in ℤ³. The third coordinate stays free. In the first two coordinates a vector is a relation exactly when the coordinates have the same parity. Hence the torsion subgroup has order two. Choosing the first two relations and the third unit vector gives det V = 4. The general bound gives 2³ = 8. The three numbers measure respectively the actual torsion, the index for this completed list, and the estimate using the common row bound.

The extra cost in positive homological degree

Let a group G permute the simplex basis in each degree and commute with each face map. Suppose the augmented complex is exact through degree m. An orbit identifies simplices related by the action. A stabilizer fixes a specified simplex. Suppose there are at most A orbits per degree up to m and every corresponding stabilizer has order at most s ≥ 1. A boundary in degree m + 1 is an alternating sum of m + 2 faces. After identifying generators in the same orbit, coordinates add and the sum of their absolute values can only decrease. In degree zero the coefficient module at the end of the induction is generally a kernel. Identifying g x with x presents the coinvariants of that module. It gives the determinant factor (m + 2)A. For positive degree, take the short exact sequence given by the first permutation module, its augmentation and its kernel. The connecting map in the long exact sequence compares the desired torsion with the torsion one degree lower for that kernel and the finite homology of the permutation module. For a single orbit the latter is the homology of the stabilizer by Shapiro’s lemma. A group of order h has hj bar generators in degree j > 0, and the two equivariant coefficient maps show that multiplication by h kills its positive-degree homology. The cycles form a subgroup of a free abelian group with that many generators, hence the homology has order at most hhʲ. Combining the orbits costs at most sA sʲ. Repeating the exact-sequence comparison for j = m, …, 1 gives

|tors Hm(G,ℤ)| ≤ (m + 2)A sm A sᵐ.

The last exponent uses ∑j=1m sj ≤ m sm. This part needs the orbit and stabilizer estimates, exactness of the complex, the long exact sequence, Shapiro’s lemma and annihilation of finite-group homology. Each supplies a distinct input.
