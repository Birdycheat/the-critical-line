How many common witnesses can two flags have?

Two flags on the same row may share a witness of smaller dimension than a hyperplane. First fix its codimension s in the definite complement of dimension k. For each row we want to count the possible witnesses, with the row weights from the genus averaging identity. The saturation argument reduces this question to primitive lattices at each exact determinant. A primitive lattice here has the form P = L ∩ U for an E-subspace U. Write Ns,d(L) for the number of these subspaces with dimension j = k − s and |P∨/P| = d².

Theorem (primitive sublattices in a row genus). Fix the integral complement lattice of a row. Its Hermitian form is totally definite of rank k over a CM field E of degree 2f, unramified over its real subfield at the finite places. Its dual quotient is isomorphic over 𝒪E to 𝒪E/(T), with T ≥ 3 a rational prime splitting completely in E. Put t = Tf, and let Mr be the standard Hermitian lattice mass in rank r. The frame has a scale b ≥ 1, a rank parameter m ≥ 2, and a positive constant cl for which log rd(E) ≤ (log b)/cl. Fix any 0 < c₀ ≤ cl. The bound holds for every real C ≥ max(16, 1/c₀), every H ≥ bCm²f, every integer d ≥ 1, and every 2 ≤ s ≤ k. If mgen = ∑[L] 1/|Aut(L)| is the mass of the row genus, then

(1/mgen) ∑[L] Ns,d(L)/|Aut(L)| ≤ (d/t)k (dt)Cs + C log f Ξs.

When j ≥ 2, the remaining factor is Ξs = MsMj/Mk. When j is 0 or 1, it is (Ck)kf(Ht)Ck. The distinction comes from how we count the subspaces.

Proof for two factors of rank at least two. Mark a subspace in each lattice. A mark with orbit of size q contributes q/|Aut(L)|, the reciprocal of the order of its stabilizer. We may therefore fix the subspace first and count the compatible lattices with these weights. Put n = d², X = nt² and λ = log₂(2f). Three estimates give the count.

  1. Rational subspaces. The subspace orbit bound covers every rank-j subspace admitting an integral lattice of discriminant order n by at most n1+λ rational isometry classes.
  2. Intersections. For a fixed subspace, the fixed-discriminant mass bound in rank r is Mra(r+λ)/2 at discriminant order a ≥ 1. The intersection in U has order n. In its perpendicular space the order lies between 1 and X, since the ambient discriminant module is cyclic over 𝒪E of order t². Thus the two masses are bounded respectively by Mjn(j+λ)/2 and MsX·X(s+λ)/2. The extra X allows every possible order of the second intersection.
  3. Gluing. A lattice can strictly contain the direct sum of its two intersections. For fixed intersections of orders n and a, the gluing bound permits at most (na)2s+1 ambient lattices here. The discriminant modules on the two sides can be generated by at most s+1 and s elements, respectively. An ambient lattice gives an intermediate submodule between the sum of the intersections and the sum of their duals. Here is the finite-module count. If a finite module Q over the ring of integers has g generators, each of its submodules also has at most g. At each prime supporting Q, lift the submodule to the free module of rank g over the discrete valuation ring. Its preimage is free of rank at most g, so its images generate the submodule locally. Pad each list with zeros and combine corresponding entries across the primary components by the Chinese remainder theorem. Hence every submodule is generated by some ordered g-tuple in Q, and there are at most |Q|g of them. Multiply the three estimates. Keep nj/2, replace the remaining factors of n by X, and use (nX)2s+1 ≤ X4s+2. The exponent of X is (1+λ) + λ/2 + 1 + (s+λ)/2 + 4s+2. Consequently the unnormalized marked mass is at most

Count the first two determinants in a four-dimensional Gaussian lattice

Use the identity Hermitian form on ℤ[i]⁴. A saturated plane has a Gaussian basis u, v. Put pab = uavb − ubva. Its Gram determinant d is the sum of the six squared absolute values of these minors, by Cauchy–Binet. The dual quotient has order d². A single nonzero minor forces the field plane to be the corresponding coordinate plane, whose saturated lattice has determinant 1. There are six such planes. Andrew Putman, The Cauchy–Binet formula, Theorem 0.1

At determinant 2, precisely two minors must therefore be units. Their index pairs share an index, since expanding the definitions gives p₁₂p₃₄ − p₁₃p₂₄ + p₁₄p₂₃ = 0. Disjoint pairs would leave a single nonzero product. With common index a, the plane is spanned by ea and eb + εec with b < c and ε one of the four Gaussian units. The two coefficients of value one along a and b show that these vectors already generate the whole intersection. There are 4 choices of a, 3 unordered pairs {b,c}, and 4 phases, or 48 planes. They form a single orbit under the 4!·4⁴ = 6,144 coordinate isometries, so their contribution for this lattice is 48/6,144 = 1/128. This calculation illustrates the marked mass for one lattice. The genus theorem uses the split-prime hypotheses stated above.

From one primitive count to a pair of flags. Fix two relevant Gram labels and let H be the product of their heights. Fix the cutoff exponent α and take 1 ≤ Δ ≤ Hα. Let K be the uniform bound for one flag over a given witness. An ordered pair costs at most K² over that same witness. By saturation, a primitive determinant d contributes with multiplicity at most (Δ/d)k+λ+1. The comparison of row weights with genus weights applies to each exact determinant, so we may average the primitive counts first. The primitive mean has a fixed constant C₁. Once the pair constant satisfies C ≥ C₁, the first label height is at least bC₁m²f and the second is at least one. Their product H meets the same height condition for every d in the sum. If A(d) denotes the right-hand side of the genus theorem, the resulting mean number of pairs is bounded by

K² ∑1≤d≤⌊Δ⌋ (Δ/d)k+λ+1A(d).

In every summand, (Δ/d)k(d/t)k equals (Δ/t)k. For instance, in the scalar identity take k = 4, Δ = 4 and t = 5. At d = 1 the product is 4⁴(1/5)⁴. At d = 2 it is 2⁴(2/5)⁴. Both are 256/625. The costs of gluing and of choosing the two flags still have to be paid. Only this fourth power is common to all the terms of the sum. Write e = C₁(s + log f) for the exponent from the primitive mean. The remaining powers satisfy (Δ/d)λ+1 ≤ Δλ+1 and (dt)e ≤ (Δt)e. At most ⌊Δ⌋ terms occur, so Δλ+2 suffices for these multiplicities. The pair estimate allows Δλ+4, then absorbs it and the fixed-witness cost using the two label heights. Here the primitive-mean constant C₁ is fixed first. The final pair estimate uses a sufficiently large constant C ≥ C₁. Taking two independent genus means would lose the condition that both flags have the same witness.
