How many independent directions do the period maps detect?

Start with one word u. Its normalized period map Au = cu−1/2Pu takes du columns into the common row space. Its Gram matrix is Kuu = Au∗Au. Choose an orthonormal basis of the kernel and extend it to the source. For each unit vector x in the kernel basis, Aux = 0 gives (Kuu − I)x = −x. It contributes exactly 1 to the squared Hilbert–Schmidt norm Q(Kuu − I). The remaining basis vectors contribute nonnegative terms. By the dimension formula for a linear map,

dim ker Au ≤ Q(Kuu − I),   rank Au ≥ (1 − e0)du   if   Q(Kuu − I) ≤ e0du.

The weighted count of minor lists bounds the contribution at each intersection defect. Squaring the norm pairs two terms, so the two sums over defects give the square of a single sum. In the resulting estimate, the factor du bounds the row mass multiplied by the local representation dimension and its Gram normalization. It counts the source columns, whose number is known before applying the period map. The scalar bound on that square gives Q(Buu) ≤ ξdu, where ξ = exp(−kf log k).

Write D∗ for the positive reference mass of the whole row space and Γ for the family scale with log Γ = CΓm⁵fL. The complementary estimate gives Q(Kuu − Buu − I) ≤ Γdu²/D∗. The chosen word sizes satisfy duΓ²/D∗ ≤ C0. Multiplying by du/Γ gives a complementary error of at most (C0/Γ)du. Applying ‖X + Y‖² ≤ 2‖X‖² + 2‖Y‖² gives e0 = 2ξ + 2C0/Γ. For example, the coordinate map from ℂ⁸ to ℂ⁷ that drops the last coordinate has squared error exactly 1 and rank 7. Appending zero rows leaves its Gram matrix unchanged. An upper bound for the number of available rows therefore needs additional information.

What happens when all the words share the same rows?

Write D = Dall for the dimension of the entire common row space, including the rows later discarded when the lattice map takes its top block. Let R = ∑u du count all the columns. The automorphic projection trace gives D ≤ (1 + η₁)D∗. Independently, for u ≠ v the period correlation estimate gives Q(Kuv) ≤ (1 + η₀)dudv/D∗, with Kuv = Au∗Av. To measure how many rows the columns span together, form the positive operator F = ∑u AuAu∗. Its kernel is the orthogonal complement of their span, since ⟨Fy, y⟩ = ∑u ‖Au∗y‖².

For example, take two one-column maps from ℂ to ℂ², both sending z to (z, 0). Both diagonal Gram matrices equal 1, yet the columns span just one line. Their mixed Gram entry is 1, and F has diagonal entries 2 and 0. Move the second column to (0, 1). The two diagonal Gram matrices still equal 1, but the mixed Gram entry vanishes, F becomes the identity, and the span has dimension two. In both cases tr F = 2. The second trace moment tr(F²) falls from 4 to 2 as the repeated direction is replaced by an independent one.

Cycling the factors under the trace gives tr F = ∑u tr Kuu and tr(F²) = ∑u,v Q(Kuv). Put a = √e0. On the diagonal, Cauchy–Schwarz gives |tr(Kuu − I)| ≤ √du ‖Kuu − I‖ ≤ a du, with the Hilbert–Schmidt norm throughout. The triangle inequality gives ‖Kuu‖ ≤ (1 + a)√du. For distinct words, the count of pairs of flags sharing a subspace supplies one contribution to the mixed Gram estimate. Adding the distinct-word terms gives

tr F ≥ (1 − a)R,   tr(F²) ≤ (1 + η₀)R²/D∗ + (1 + a)²R.

Dimension bound. Suppose 0 < σ ≤ 1/4, D∗ > 0, R ≥ D∗/σ, −1 ≤ η₀, and η₀, η₁, a ≤ σ/2. Then the period columns span at least D/(1 + 5σ) dimensions. To see this, the lower bound for R absorbs the last summand of tr(F²) into a multiple of R²/D∗. The independent row count then gives

D tr(F²)/(tr F)² ≤ (1 + η₁)[1 + η₀ + σ(1 + a)²]/(1 − a)² ≤ 1 + 5σ.

For the last inequality, replace η₀, η₁ and a by σ/2 and multiply by (1 − σ/2)². Expanding, the right side exceeds the left by σ(16 − 52σ + 4σ² − σ³)/8, which is positive for 0 < σ ≤ 1/4. Finally let h be the rank of F. Its h positive eigenvalues sum to tr F and their squares sum to tr(F²). Cauchy–Schwarz gives (tr F)² ≤ h tr(F²), proving the claim.

The family meets the lower bound on R by choosing ⌈C₀Γ²/σ⌉ words. Each contributes at least D∗/(C₀Γ²) columns. Multiplication gives R ≥ (C₀Γ²/σ) · D∗/(C₀Γ²) = D∗/σ.

To see where this hypothesis enters, consider a smaller family. Take one word consisting of a single unit column in ℂ100 and set D = D∗ = 100. Its Gram matrix is (1), so its diagonal error is zero. There are no distinct-word pairs and the row count is exact. Yet F is the projection onto that one column, with both traces and rank equal to 1. At σ = 1/100 the theorem requires at least 10,000 columns in total. Controlling the minor lists gives the error estimate. The independent supply of columns allows the trace ratio to force a large span.

How much survives the ramification conditions?

For each word retain an ru-dimensional subspace, included by an isometry Vu. Suppose ∑u(du − ru) ≤ ρR, with 0 ≤ ρ ≤ 1/4. The arithmetic column count supplies this loss. Set F′ = ∑u (AuVu)(AuVu)∗. Compression replaces each Gram block by Vu∗KuvVv. In orthonormal bases adapted to the retained subspaces, this keeps a submatrix, so tr((F′)²) ≤ tr(F²).

The first trace also decreases. In a word with mu = du − ru discarded columns, the trace of the identity on those columns is mu. The absolute trace of the error there is at most a√(mudu) by Cauchy–Schwarz. Summing and using ∑u√(mudu) ≤ √ρ R gives tr F′ ≥ (1 − a − ρ − a√ρ)R. For σ ≤ 1/20 the factor is positive. The compressed trace estimate then yields

rank F′ ≥ D/(1 + 5σ + 3√ρ).

To check the loss in this bound, put t = √ρ ≤ 1/2. The lower bound for the first trace has changed from (1−a)R to (1−a)R(1−x), where x = t(t+a)/(1−a). Since a ≤ σ/2 ≤ 1/40, we have x ≤ 7t/13. The upper bound for the second trace moment still applies. Thus the previous upper bound 1+5σ for the trace ratio increases by a factor of at most (1−7t/13)−2. This function has positive second derivative, so it lies below the chord joining its values at 0 and 1/2. Those values are 1 and (26/19)² < 2. The chord is therefore at most 1+2t. Finally 1+5σ ≤ 5/4 gives (1+5σ)(1+2t) ≤ 1+5σ+3t. Applying the same eigenvalue inequality to F′ gives the displayed rank.

For example, suppose the trace hypotheses hold with D = 100, σ = 1/100 and ρ = 1/10,000. The full span has dimension at least 100/1.05, hence at least 96. For the retained columns the denominator rises to 1.08. Their images still span at least 93 of the same 100 directions.

Where does the extra 3√ρ come from?

Put t = √ρ and L = 1 + 5σ + 3t. For the numerator, take the bound for tr(F²) before division by the first trace. Multiplying it by D, absorbing the term linear in R and using the same numerical inequality as above gives D tr((F′)²) ≤ (1 + 5σ)(1 − a)²R². For the denominator the discarded-column estimate gives tr F′ ≥ [1 − a − t(t + a)]R. Because t ≤ 1/2 and a ≤ σ/2 ≤ 1/40, one has 2L(t + a) ≤ 2(11/4)(21/40) ≤ 3(1 − a). Multiply this by t(1 − a) and expand the square to obtain (1 + 5σ)(1 − a)² ≤ L[1 − a − t(t + a)]². Applying the eigenvalue inequality to F′ gives the stated rank.

Restricting the columns changes the domain of the period map. Both ranks above are still measured in the space of all D rows. The map to the dual arithmetic lattice keeps only its Dar arithmetic rows. This projection has kernel of dimension D − Dar, so it can remove at most that many independent directions from any span. For example, let π project ℂ³ onto its first two coordinates. The planes spanned by (e₁, e₃) and by (e₁, e₂) both have dimension two before projection. Their images have dimensions one and two, respectively. In either case the exact loss for a plane W is dim(W ∩ ker π), by the dimension formula applied to π restricted to W.

Thus the number of missing directions among the arithmetic rows is at most the number missing among all rows. If the latter number is at most ωD and Dar ≥ (1 − δ)D with δ < 1, the missing fraction among the arithmetic rows is at most ω/(1 − δ). This is the denominator in the slope argument, where one makes a further selection of columns by slope.
