Which Hermitian forms can a positive functional see?

The torsion estimate uses a complex of ordered vertices. Its boundary deletes one vertex at a time. To use the complex we must prove that every integral cycle in it bounds. We start by finding the geometric object that records which tuples are allowed.

Let F be totally complex and take one complex embedding from each conjugate pair. A point h in our real vector space is a tuple of Hermitian matrices of size N ≥ 1. Each nonzero vector y in 𝒪FN gives a real linear functional on that space, namely the sum qy(h) of its Hermitian values over the chosen embeddings. Write P for the closed convex set on which all these functionals are at least one. A form is a vertex when the equalities qy(h) = 1 determine it uniquely. Equivalently, the only variation annihilated by every active functional is zero.

The embedded integer vectors form a full real lattice. A sufficiently large multiple of the identity form therefore belongs to P. The real Gram bound makes every member positive definite at each place. In a neighborhood of any such form the least eigenvalues stay above a common positive constant c. An equality qy = 1 there forces the squared Euclidean norm of the embedded vector to be at most 1/c. Only finitely many lattice vectors occur in that ball. All other inequalities stay strict throughout the neighborhood.

Let T be the closed cone of tuples of positive semidefinite matrices and let X consist of all linear functionals strictly positive on its nonzero elements. The sum of the traces belongs to X since a nonzero positive semidefinite matrix has a positive eigenvalue. For a vertex v the normal cone collects the functionals minimized at that vertex.

Aᵥ = {ℓ : ℓ(v) ≤ ℓ(h) for every h ∈ P}.

Each cone is closed and convex because it is an intersection of closed half-spaces. To see that the cones cover X, fix ℓ ∈ X. On the unit vectors of T, compactness gives ℓ a strictly positive minimum. Thus bounded upper levels of ℓ on P are compact. The minimum on P is attained, and its minimizers form a compact convex set. Choose a minimizer of maximal squared Euclidean norm in that set. Strict convexity of the squared norm prevents this point from being the midpoint of two distinct minimizers. If some nonzero direction killed all its active functionals, small displacements in both directions would be feasible. Indeed only the finite list above can become active nearby. The two displaced values of ℓ average to the minimum and so both attain it. This contradicts the midpoint property. The chosen minimizer is a vertex.

Only finitely many cones can meet the convex hull of a finite set of functionals ℓ₁,…,ℓs in X. Fix any h₀ ∈ P. If a convex combination of the ℓᵢ is minimized at v, at least one ℓᵢ satisfies ℓᵢ(v) ≤ ℓᵢ(h₀). Those finitely many compact sublevels contain only finitely many vertices. A finite neighborhood cover gives a finite pool of possible active inequalities, and their active subset determines each vertex uniquely.

For a two-dimensional example, impose h₁ ≥ 1, h₂ ≥ 1 and h₁+h₂ ≥ 3. A positive functional with equal coefficients is minimized along the segment from (1,2) to (2,1). Both endpoints belong to its minimizing face. A slight increase in the coefficient of h₁ selects (1,2) alone. The shared supporting functional lets the ordered pair of endpoints appear in the complex.

How does cutting a convex set help fill a cycle?

Given closed convex sets Aᵢ covering a nonempty convex set X, keep an ordered tuple of labels whenever the corresponding sets share a point of X. This collection is the nerve of the cover. Deleting a label keeps the same witness. A chain of degree p is a finite integer sum of such tuples of length p+1. Labels can repeat. The boundary of a tuple is the alternating sum over deletions. Deleting two different positions in the two possible orders produces opposite signs, so the boundary applied twice is zero. In degree zero, summing the coefficients defines the augmentation to ℤ.

Consider first a nonempty compact convex portion K that meets finitely many cover sets. Suppose that all the sets meeting K contain one common point in K. Choose one of their labels a. Prepending a to each tuple defines an integral operator s. Deleting its newly prepended entry returns the original tuple, and every other deletion has the opposite sign from the corresponding term in s∂. Hence ∂s+s∂ is the identity, including the augmentation if s sends the empty tuple to [a]. A cycle z is filled by sz.

In the general compact case, count the unordered subsets of active labels that have a common witness in K, including the empty subset. This finite count controls an induction even though tuples may have repeated entries and arbitrarily large length. Choose a realized subset Σ of largest cardinality. Unless all active labels have a common witness, some active label i is outside Σ. There is no point witnessing Σ together with i. The witnesses of Σ form a nonempty compact convex set, disjoint from the nonempty compact convex set K ∩ Aᵢ. Separate them by a hyperplane, with i strictly on the left and Σ strictly on the right. In Euclidean coordinates the midpoint hyperplane perpendicular to a shortest segment between the two compact sets works. Varying either endpoint toward another point of its set cannot shorten the segment, which puts the entire set on its own side. Boyd and Vandenberghe, §2.5.1, pp. 46–47.

Each closed half of K and the slice on the hyperplane has fewer realized subsets. The left half loses Σ, the right half loses {i}, and the slice loses both. Each is nonempty since K contains the segment joining the opposite witnesses. The nerve on K is the union of the two half nerves. Their intersection is the nerve of the slice. Indeed a tuple witnessed on both sides is witnessed on the joining segment, at the point where it crosses the hyperplane, since every set named by the tuple is convex.

To join the fillings, split an integral cycle as z = u+v with chains in the two halves. The boundary b = ∂u = −∂v belongs to their intersection. Exactness there gives a chain e with ∂e = b. Now u−e and v+e are cycles in the two halves, so the induction fills them. Their fillings add to a filling of z. For zero-dimensional cycles b is an integer and multiplying a slice vertex by b gives a chain of augmentation b. This proves

∂(U+V) = (u−e) + (v+e) = z.

Why does the arithmetic face complex give an exact sequence?

On [0,4] take the closed intervals A = [0,1], B = [1,3], C = [3,4]. There is no common point of all three, and no edge [A,C]. Cut at 2. Only B meets the slice. The cycle [C]−[A] splits into −[A] on the left and [C] on the right. The two augmentations are −1 and 1. Use −[B] on the slice to correct them. The corrected cycles [B]−[A] and [C]−[B] are the boundaries of [A,B] and [B,C]. Their sum cancels the artificial vertex in the boundary. At the contacts 1 and 3 the cut still gives the union and intersection identities. One half then keeps all the faces. Every cut strictly between 1 and 3 separates the chosen witness sets, reducing the face count on both sides.

Return to the normal cones of the Hermitian vertices. The parameter space X of positive functionals can be unbounded. A cycle has finite support, so choose one witness in X for each tuple that occurs and include one fixed point of X. Their convex hull K lies in X and is compact, being the image of a finite-dimensional closed simplex under the convex-combination map. By the finite-cone property only finitely many normal cones meet K. They cover it. Every tuple in the cycle already occurs in its nerve, and the compact filling argument supplies a finite integral chain with that boundary. Its witnesses in K are also witnesses in X.

⋯ → ℤ[ordered triples] → ℤ[ordered pairs] → ℤ[vertices] → ℤ → 0.

Here ℤ[ ] means finite integer combinations of the allowed tuples. The augmentation from ℤ[vertices] to ℤ sends each vertex to 1 and is onto because the cover contains a vertex. In every preceding degree the alternating deletion rule puts every boundary in the kernel. The filling puts every kernel element in the image. An integral change of lattice basis permutes the vertices and carries a common supporting functional to its pullback. This commutes with deletion at each position. We have therefore constructed the exact complex of permutation modules needed in the group homology bound. The face orbits and stabilizers are counted separately. Exactness here is asserted before identifying equivalent faces under the group action.
