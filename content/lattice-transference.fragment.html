How does a dual minimum put a lattice point near every target?

The dual test needs a lattice point near a prescribed vector that can have arbitrary real coordinates. A lower bound on the length of every nonzero integral covector supplies a covering radius for the original lattice. We derive the bound first in integer coordinates, then identify which dual lattice appears in the arithmetic statement. All costs below are squared distances. Find one short direction. For a positive definite real symmetric matrix G of size d ≥ 1 write QG(x) = xᵀGx. Suppose QG⁻¹(c) ≥ δ > 0 for all nonzero integer columns c. To control QG, factor G = BᵀB. The inverse image under B of the box with |yⱼ| ≤ 2|det B|1/d has volume 4d. It is symmetric and convex and its volume exceeds 2d, the Minkowski threshold for ℤd. It therefore contains a nonzero integer v. Summing the coordinate squares of Bv gives QG(v) ≤ 4d(det G)1/d. In particular a positive lower bound ℓ on every nonzero integer vector forces ℓd ≤ (4d)d det G. The volume of the pulled-back box · Its nonzero integer point · From the box to the determinant.

Apply that determinant inequality to G⁻¹ with ℓ = δ and to G under the supposition that all its nonzero integer vectors have squared length greater than 2(4d)²/δ. Multiplication cancels the two determinants and gives 2d ≤ 1. Consequently there is a nonzero integer vector with QG(v) ≤ 2(4d)²/δ. Write this vector as an integer multiple of a primitive one, whose coordinates have greatest common divisor one. The primitive vector is at least as short and can be completed to an integral basis by Smith normal form. In that basis the first diagonal entry of the Gram matrix is its squared length, say a. An integral basis change permutes the lattice vectors and its inverse transpose permutes integral covectors, so both the dual lower bound and the covering problem survive. Round one coordinate after the others. Write the new matrix in blocks [[a,bᵀ],[b,D]] and put S = D − bbᵀ/a. For an error vector (u,y), completing the square gives

QG(u,y) = a(u + bᵀy/a)² + QS(y).

Minimizing in u shows that S is positive definite. For an integral covector c′ on the remaining coordinates, solving Gx = (0,c′) gives x′ = S⁻¹c′. Hence QS⁻¹(c′) = QG⁻¹(0,c′), and the same δ works in dimension d−1. For a target (p₀,p′), use induction to choose an integer c′ with small remaining error y = p′−c′. Round p₀ + bᵀy/a to its nearest integer for the first coordinate. The square on the right now costs at most a/4. Starting with C₀ = 1, the resulting constant satisfies

Cd = Cd−1 + 8d² = 1 + 8 ∑j=1d j²,   QG(p−c) ≤ Cd/δ.

The added cost is at most 2(4d)²/(4δ) by the short-direction bound. In dimension zero the only error is zero, so the initial constant 1 works. Transfer from integral coordinates to Hermitian forms. Let L be totally complex with discriminant Δ, and take a positive definite Hermitian form h at each complex place in rank N ≥ 1. Choose an integral basis b₁,…,be of 𝒪L, where e = [L:ℚ]. The archimedean quadratic form qh acquires a real Gram matrix G in d = Ne coordinates. Every target tuple has real coordinates in this basis, and integer coordinates give the original arithmetic lattice. The integer theorem requires a lower bound for the inverse real Gram matrix. The input λ∨(h) measures integral covectors using (hw⁻¹)ᵀ at each place w.

For a nonzero integer covector c, let T have entries Tr(bᵢbⱼ), hence det T = Δ. In each block multiply the row of coefficients of c by the adjugate of T, then use those integers as basis coefficients of f ∈ 𝒪LN. Since adj(T)T = ΔI, this gives Tr(fₖbⱼ) = Δckj and f ≠ 0. If z is the tuple represented by real coordinates x, linearity and conjugate pairing of the embeddings give Δcᵀx = 2 Re ∑w fwᵀzw. The factor 2 counts the two embeddings at each complex place. Now choose x representing zw = hw⁻¹ fw. Set s = qh∨(f). Then QG(x) = s and Δcᵀx = 2s. Expanding the nonnegative square QG(tx−G⁻¹c) gives QG⁻¹(c) ≥ 2t cᵀx−t²s. Substitute t = 2/Δ. Every integral c ≠ 0 now satisfies QG⁻¹(c) ≥ 4s/Δ² ≥ 4λ∨(h)/Δ². Applying the integer result with this δ gives, for each target z, a lattice point v satisfying

qh(z−v) ≤ CdΔ²/(4λ∨(h)).

Thus the constant CT in the dual probe bound depends on the field and rank, and the dependence on the input form is carried by its dual minimum. Check the rounding shift and the discriminant on a Gaussian lattice

Take L = ℚ(i), N = 1, h = 1 and the integral basis (1,1+i). Its Gram matrix is [[1,1],[1,2]]. Coordinates (2/5,2/5) give the complex target 4/5 + 2i/5. Rounding both coordinates separately gives the lattice point 0 and squared error 4/5. The Schur complement equals 1. After rounding the second coordinate to 0, the shifted first coordinate is 2/5 + 2/5 = 4/5. Rounding it to 1 gives squared error (−1/5)² + (2/5)² = 1/5.

In this basis the trace matrix is [[2,2],[2,0]], with determinant −4. The integer covector (0,1) times its adjugate is (−2,2), giving f = −2 + 2(1+i) = 2i. Since |f|² = 4, the converted lower bound for this covector is 4·4/(−4)² = 1. Direct multiplication by G⁻¹ = [[2,−1],[−1,1]] gives the same value. This example uses the squared length convention in both calculations.
