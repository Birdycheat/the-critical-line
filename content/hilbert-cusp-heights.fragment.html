Where do the finitely many cusp matrices come from?

Let F be totally real of degree d, with ring of integers 𝒪. A point of the Hilbert upper half-space has one coordinate τw = xw + iyw, with yw > 0, at each real embedding w. For a matrix in SL₂(F) with bottom row (c,b), direct multiplication by the conjugate denominator gives Im(gτ)w = yw/|w(c)τw+w(b)|². We need one row whose denominator is small at every embedding.

Embed 𝒪² in ℝ2d using all its real embeddings. At each place, apply to a pair (a,b) of real coordinates the determinant-one map (a,b) ↦ (√y · a, (xa+b)/√y). The product map preserves the covolume of the lattice for every τ. Choose a cube [−R,R]2d of volume greater than 22d times this covolume. Minkowski’s convex body theorem supplies a nonzero lattice point in it. Adding the squares of the two coordinates at each place gives a pair (c,b) ∈ 𝒪², independent of the place, with

|w(c)τw + w(b)|² ≤ 2R²yw   at every w.

Complete that row. Its two entries generate a nonzero integral ideal I. The class group is finite, so fix an integral ideal J in each class. For the class of I there is a scalar λ with λI = J. Hence |N(λ)| = N(J)/N(I) ≤ N(J), uniformly bounded by a constant B over the fixed finite list. Put (p,q) = λ(c,b). From pJ−1 + qJ−1 = 𝒪 choose a,b′ ∈ J−1 with aq−b′p = 1. This gives a determinant-one matrix g with top row in J−1 and bottom row in J. Choose one such matrix r for each class that occurs. Every entry of r−1g is a difference of two products in J J−1 = 𝒪. Thus g = rk with k ∈ SL₂(𝒪) and r in a fixed finite set. Multiplying (p,q) by a unit preserves the ideal, so the same construction works for every unit multiple. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, Theorem 4.19 (Minkowski) · Theorems 4.3–4.4 (ideal classes)

Why does each imaginary part have a positive lower bound?

Write y′w for the heights obtained with bottom row λ(c,b). Put A ≥ max(1,2R²) and enlarge B to at least 1. The small-denominator inequality gives y′w ≥ 1/(A|w(λ)|²). Multiplying over the embeddings yields ∏wy′w ≥ P = 1/(AdB²). A product lower bound alone still permits one height to tend to zero.

To balance them, take the numbers ½ log y′w and subtract their mean. Dirichlet’s unit theorem says that the vectors (log |w(u)|)w of units u form a full lattice in the hyperplane of coordinate sum zero. Add a lattice vector to move the centered numbers into a fixed bounded parallelepiped. If each resulting coordinate has absolute value at most L, the differences of the logarithms of the new heights have absolute value at most 4L. The required new bottom row is u−1λ(c,b). Its height zw = |w(u)|²y′w gains the square of the unit, and its product is unchanged since |N(u)| = 1. Set κ = e−4L. The largest zw is at least P1/d, and the smallest is at least κ times the largest. In particular the following constants work for every τ. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, Theorem 5.9

t = κ min(1,P) > 0,   t ≤ Im(rkτ)v,   κ Im(rkτ)w ≤ Im(rkτ)v   for all v,w.

The rightmost matrix k acts first. These inequalities suffice for the theta and Eisenstein estimates at unbounded heights. The finite list is the list of matrices r. The integral matrix k can vary with τ.

Balance two real heights by one unit

In ℚ(√5) take ε = (1+√5)/2, whose conjugate is −ε−1. For any T > 0 take heights (Tε−4, Tε4). With u = ε² the two squared absolute values are ε4 and ε−4. Multiplying the heights by them gives (T,T). The product remains T² even as the common height grows without bound.
