How does the square-root exponential arise for one character?

Write d = log m, s = √(log y), and ψχ(y) = ∑n≤y Λ(n)χ(n). In the small-modulus range, d is at most a fixed small multiple of s. Take cZ to be the smaller of 1/20 and a positive constant provided by the ordinary zero-free-region theorem. Its region is Re z ≥ 1 − cZ/log(m(|Im z| + 2)), with 0 < cZ ≤ 1/20. Let Bχ consist of the zeros of L(·,χ) in this region. Across the characters of this modulus there is at most one such zero. If present, it is simple, real, and belongs to a nonprincipal quadratic character. The product over all characters is what makes uniqueness simultaneous. See Koukoulopoulos, The Distribution of Prime Numbers, Theorem 12.3. There are constants c, C > 0 such that for y ≥ 2 and d ≤ cs,

|ψχ(y) − 𝟙χ principaly + ∑β∈Bχ yβ/β| ≤ Cy exp(−cs).

For a nonprincipal χ, smooth by weighting Λ(n)χ(n) with y/n − 1 for n ≤ y. Denote this sum by S(y). The contour formula for it has a linear term D(1)y, where D = −L′/L, and a term −yβ/[β(β − 1)] for each β in the region. To see the rate of decay of the remainder, take height T = exp(cZs/2) and put J = log(m(T + 2)). For d ≤ cZs/4 we have J ≤ 3cZs/4 + 2. The shifted line can be chosen with real part σ ≤ 1 − cZ/(4J). Truncation and the shifted line together cost at most a constant times (s + 2)³(y/T + Tyσ). For sufficiently large s, J ≤ s/4, so Tyσ ≤ y exp(−cZs/2). Absorbing the cubic factor and increasing the constant on bounded s gives a remainder of order y exp(−cZs/4).

To undo the smoothing take u = y(1 + h). The expression [yS(u) − uS(y)]/(u − y) gives weight one to n ≤ y, and a weight between zero and one to the at most yh + 1 integers in (y,u]. This costs at most (yh + 1)log u. The linear term cancels. The difference of the β term is −yβ/β with error at most 2yh, uniformly up to β = 1 from below. Indeed the difference quotient for S(x)/x averages xβ−2 over [y,u], with a factor −yu/β. The relative factor multiplying −yβ/β lies between (1 + h)β−1 and 1 + h, hence within h of 1 since ½ ≤ β < 1. The two contour remainders, after differencing, are of order y exp(−cZs/4)/h. These competing errors explain why the width of the averaging interval matters.

For the principal character, the unrestricted smoothed zeta sum has main term x log x − (1 + γ)x, where γ is Euler’s constant, and error of order x exp(−cζ√(log x)). This zeta estimate also follows from the ordinary prime number theorem. If ψ(t) = t + E(t), partial summation gives S(x) = x ∫1x ψ(t)dt/t². The bound |E(t)| ≪ t exp(−b√(log t)) makes ∫x∞ |E(t)|dt/t² exponentially small in √(log x), after decreasing b. Its constant ∫1∞ E(t)dt/t² equals −1 − γ. Indeed −ζ′(w)/[wζ(w)] − 1/(w − 1) is the integral of E(t)t−w−1 for real w > 1. Taking w down to 1 in the integral and the Laurent expansion gives the constant. See Koukoulopoulos, proof of Theorem 8.1 and DLMF (25.2.4).

The difference of the principal main term gives y(1 + h)log(1 + h)/h, between y and y(1 + h). Remove the powers of primes dividing m. At each exponent their weights sum to at most log m, and there are at most log y/log 2 exponents. Now put c₀ = min(1, cζ, cZ/4) and h = exp(−c₀s/2). Both h and exp(−c₀s)/h equal exp(−c₀s/2). The logarithms in the short interval and the deleted prime powers fit within Cy exp(−c₀s/4) when d ≤ c₀s/4. This proves the displayed estimate with c = c₀/4. The large-modulus formula below uses the same elementary weights with a different contour and a longer list of zeros.

Where does the exceptional factor come from at small modulus?

Suppose 8 divides m. The progression theorem chooses one pair (χ₁,β) for the entire modulus when there is a nonprincipal quadratic character with a real zero 1 − 1/(8d) < β < 1. It chooses the pair in the ordinary zero-free region first if one exists. This rule makes the main term the same in both modulus ranges. For a single character let Mχ(y) be y if χ is principal and zero otherwise, with yβ/β subtracted if χ = χ₁. Put η = min(1,(1 − β)d) if a pair was chosen and η = 1 otherwise.

Set cA = min(c/4,cZ) and assume d ≤ cAs. If a selected β lies in the region, the character bound already contains its contribution. If the selected β lies outside, the regional list for χ₁ is empty. The additional term in Mχ₁ has modulus at most 2y exp(−s/2). Indeed β ≥ ½, and 1 − β > cZ/log(2m) ≥ cZ/(2d) ≥ 1/(2s). Since log y = s², this is precisely the loss in the exponent of yβ. If no pair exists every regional list is empty.

Summing the absolute errors over the φ(m) ≤ m characters costs one factor of m. To retain η, use the exceptional-zero bound η ≥ b/m for a fixed b > 0. This is the exponent-one case of Siegel’s lower bound. For a real primitive character of conductor f and a real zero β near 1 one has 1 − β ≥ bεf−ε. A character modulo m is induced from conductor f ≤ m, and its additional Euler factors have no zeros of positive real part. The factor d in η is at least log 8. Thus introducing η costs at most one further factor m/b. But m² ≤ exp(2cAs), with 2cA ≤ c/2 and 2cA ≤ 1/10. Both exponentials survive. See Kedlaya, Notes on analytic number theory, Theorem 10.9.

∑χ mod m |ψχ(y) − Mχ(y)| ≤ [(C + 2)/b] yη exp(−c′s),   c′ = min(c/2, 2/5).

For a reduced class a, multiply each error by the conjugate of χ(a), whose modulus is one, and divide the sum by φ(m). Character orthogonality gives the small-modulus contribution to the weighted progression estimate. The error still falls with η in a class where the exceptional term subtracts from the main term. See DLMF §27.8, Dirichlet characters and orthogonality.

Check the loss from primes dividing the modulus

For m = 8 and y = 16, the unrestricted count includes 2, 4, 8 and 16. Each contributes log 2, so passing to the principal character removes exactly 4 log 2. The general bound counts four possible exponents, with allowance log 8 at each. This checks the correction to the principal sum independently of any exceptional zero.
