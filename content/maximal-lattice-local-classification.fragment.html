Where can a maximal integral lattice fail to be self-dual?

Fix a nondegenerate Hermitian form h on an E-vector space V, with conjugation in the first variable. The quadratic CM extension E/F is unramified at every finite place. A lattice M is integral if all its pairings belong to 𝒪E. Its dual M∨ consists of the vectors pairing integrally with M. In the marked enlargement count, we need to compare two maximal integral lattices in this same space at one finite place v at a time. Write q for the size of the residue field of F there.

Maximality can be tested locally. An integral enlargement gives a submodule of the finite module M∨/M on which the pairing modulo 𝒪E vanishes. Conversely the inverse image of such a submodule is integral. The primary components over different places of F pair to zero, since their annihilators are coprime and conjugation preserves each fibre over F. Thus an enlargement found at one place gives a global enlargement by taking zero in the other components.

At a split place the two factors of the completed coefficient ring are exchanged by conjugation. Write the completed lattice as A ⊕ B. The form pairs the two summands perfectly over the fraction fields and vanishes on each summand separately. Integrality gives B ⊆ A∨, where the dual here lives in the other summand. Replacing B by A∨ preserves integrality. Maximality forces equality, so the whole lattice is self-dual.

At an inert place write R = 𝒪E,𝔭, take a uniformizer π from the real field, and write v(π) = 1. In an orthogonal R-basis the norms are πbⱼuⱼ with bⱼ ≥ 0 and uⱼ fixed units. Here is why diagonalization also works above 2. Divide the Gram matrix by the smallest power of π occurring in its entries. The reduction is a nonzero Hermitian form over 𝔽q². If all diagonal values vanished, evaluating on x + t y for all residue scalars t would give Tr(t h(x,y)) = 0 for every t. The trace pairing of the separable quadratic residue extension is nondegenerate. Thus some vector has unit norm in the scaled form. Lift it, subtract its orthogonal projection from the remaining vectors and continue on its complement. The Stacks Project, lemma 9.20.7 on the trace pairing.

If bⱼ ≥ 2, adjoining the basis vector divided by π preserves integrality. If two exponents equal 1, write their basis vectors as x, y with norms πu, πu′ respectively. Choose a unit c with residue norm −u/u′. Indeed the residue norm sends a to aq+1. Its kernel has at most q + 1 elements by the polynomial root bound. Its image in 𝔽q× therefore has at least (q² − 1)/(q + 1) = q − 1 elements, hence fills that group. Then (x + c y)/π pairs integrally with the lattice and with itself, giving a strict enlargement. Thus a maximal lattice has either all unit norms in this basis or exactly one norm of valuation 1. In the latter case that basis vector x, with a = h(x,x), supplies the entire defect.

M∨/M = R·(x/a + M) ≃ R/πR,   |M∨/M| = q²  locally.

The bad inert places are the ones where the valuation of a Gram determinant is odd. Indeed a change of basis multiplies the determinant by the norm of the determinant of the change, whose valuation is even. In the orthogonal basis the valuation is the sum of the exponents just computed. This identifies the bad places for both maximal lattices before choosing a comparison map. The local classification also appears in W. T. Gan, J. P. Hanke and J.-K. Yu, On an exact mass formula of Shimura, §3, pp. 105–107.

How can one extra line absorb the defect?

At a bad inert place let M now denote its localization. Choose the same fixed-field uniformizer π for both lattices. Give a new vector e norm π, orthogonal to V. The direct sum M ⊕ R e has two defects of length one. We glue them by adjoining a single vector. With x and a as above, choose c ∈ R× such that c̄c ≡ −π/a modulo π and put

w = (c x + e)/π,   L = M + R w.

We have e = πw − c x and h(w,e) = 1. The pairings of w with M are integral because h(M,x) ⊆ πR. Its norm is (c̄c a + π)/π². The norm congruence makes the numerator divisible by π². The change from a basis of M together with e to the same basis together with w has determinant 1/π. The old Gram determinant had valuation two, so the new one has valuation zero. An integral Gram matrix with unit determinant has an integral inverse, proving that L is self-dual. Its intersection with V is exactly M, since the coefficient of e in m + r w is r/π. We can therefore recover M once the distinguished vector e is identified.

A dyadic calculation. Take the unramified quadratic extension ℚ₂(ζ₃)/ℚ₂ and its ring of integers R, with residue field 𝔽₄. On the line R x put h(x,x) = 2. Its dual is ½R x. A vector in the dual outside R x has norm of valuation −1, so the lattice is maximal. Take π = 2 and c = 1, whose residue norm equals −1 in 𝔽₂. With w = (x + e)/2 the Gram matrix on (x,w) has rows (2,1) and (1,1). Its determinant is 1 and e = 2w − x pairs to 1 with w. This calculation uses division by the uniformizer only for the new vector in the extended space.

How does the isometry return to the original space?

At every place where both lattices are self-dual, apply the unimodular isometry theorem directly. In the rational formulation it gives an E-linear isometry identifying the two localizations at that place. At a split place the localization allows denominators from the real integers away from v, so both conjugate primes are kept. At a bad inert place apply this theorem to the two self-dual extensions L, L′ just constructed in V ⊕ E e. The constructions can be made in the localized rational space. The coefficients lift from the residue field, and clearing denominators away from the place gives a full global lattice with the prescribed localization. The isometry g taking L to L′ may move e. The vectors p = ge and y = e both have norm π in L′ and each pairs to 1 with a vector of L′.

Correcting the distinguished vector. For u ∈ L′ and a unit b with b + b̄ = h(u,u), the map

S(z) = z − b−1h(u,z)u

is an integral isometry. Expanding the two pairings gives cancellation by the trace identity for b, and replacing b by b̄ gives the inverse. Put d = π − h(y,p). When d is a unit, take u = p − y and b = d. Then h(u,p) = d and S p = y.

Otherwise choose u with both h(u,p) and h(u,y) units. Use the two witnesses pairing to 1. If neither works on the other vector, their sum works on both. Choose θ ∈ R whose residue differs from its conjugate and put δ = θ − θ̄, a unit. The scalar b = (θ/δ)h(u,u) has the required trace. If it reduces to zero, add δ, whose trace is zero. Now the new difference π − h(y,S p) equals d + b−1h(u,p)h(y,u). Its second summand is a unit, so the previous step sends S p to y. This proves the required correction even in residue characteristic two.

Checking the second case above 2. In the example take p = −e, y = e and u = w. Then d = 4, h(u,p) = −1 and h(y,u) = 1. With θ = ζ₃ and θ² + θ + 1 = 0 we have δ = 1 + 2θ and b = θ/δ = (2 + θ)/3. Thus b−1 = 1 − θ. The new difference is 4 − (1 − θ) = 3 + θ, which reduces to the nonzero element 1 + θ in 𝔽₄. The second isometry now has a unit denominator.

Composing the correction with g fixes e. It preserves e⊥ = V because h(e,v + t e) = πt. Restriction therefore takes L ∩ V to L′ ∩ V, the two original localized lattices. Repeating this argument at each finite place proves that the two maximal lattices belong to one genus. The isometry may depend on the place. This is the genus used in the weighted count of marked pairs.
