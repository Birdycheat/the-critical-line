Why averaging a differential form preserves its periods here

How can a group cocycle that detects a cycle be replaced by a form invariant under every archimedean matrix? Let m < r₂ be the positive homological degree over the totally complex field L′. Given any starting size, the comparison chooses a size N at least that large. At this one size it applies to every real homogeneous m-cocycle c of Γ = GLN(𝒪L′). The space of integration consists of one positive definite Hermitian matrix per complex place. A bar chain [g₁|…|gm] gives the affine simplex with vertices I, g₁·I, …, (g₁⋯gm)·I, where g·h = (g⁻¹)*hg⁻¹ at each place. Integrating a form ω over these simplices and adding the chain coefficients gives its period ⟨ω,z⟩ on a cycle z. A partition of unity and Stokes’ formula first give a Γ-invariant form with the periods of c. Averaging over central scalars preserves these periods and gives a form ω′ invariant also under those scalars. To compare its values at different points, pull its value at g·I back to the tangent space at I by g. These coefficients are square-integrable on a finite-volume fundamental domain for SLN(𝒪L′) in ∏wSLN(ℂ). Indeed the partition gives a bound by a constant times heightam, and N was chosen to make the moment of order 2am finite. Their normalized Haar mean gives a form ω̄ invariant under all of ∏wGLN(ℂ). To prove that ω̄ represents c, it remains to show that every period of the mean-zero remainder ω₀ = ω′ − ω̄ vanishes. Where the lower factor 4/5 comes from

Let In and IN be the numbers of cosets in the respective Hecke averages and put α = In/IN. The cosets inherited from size n contribute α times a smaller-size Hecke period vector yj. For the other cosets, transfer from their stabilizers gives the original periods after summing. Thus xj+1 = (1 − α)xj + αyj. The smaller-size operator acts linearly on a finite-dimensional space of period vectors, so ‖yj‖ ≤ K‖xj‖ with K independent of N and j. Since IN ≥ N, we can arrange α(1 + K) ≤ 1/5. The reverse triangle inequality now gives ‖xj+1‖ ≥ (1 − α(1 + K))‖xj‖ ≥ (4/5)‖xj‖. For example, if ‖x₀‖ were 1 and the upper constant were 2, three iterations would require 64/125 ≤ ‖x₃‖ ≤ 54/125. The limit argument works for every finite constant.
