Why does lowering one weight produce the terms of the column moment?

In the mixed coefficient moment the factor (g−1)l,z comes from the dual of the defining representation of G = U(p,q). Let m = p + q = n + 1. A smooth tensor is a column x of m smooth vectors of π. Its group action has coordinate (A(g)x)c = ∑l(g−1)l,cπ(g)xl. We write elv for the column with only one nonzero entry.

Tensor translation theorem used here. Suppose 1 ≤ q ≤ p, π is a discrete series with regular Harish-Chandra parameter λ, and opposite-sign coordinates of λ differ by at least 200m³. For each s for which λ − es remains a regular dominant parameter, there is a discrete series πs of that parameter. On smooth tensors there is one summand for each such s. In the branching application consecutive coordinates differ by at least two, so each lowering preserves strict order. The input from translation theory supplies maps to the shifted models that jointly separate smooth tensors. Tensor adjunction identifies a map in the reverse direction by raising the same coordinate back. Each shifted model occurs once by the multiplicity-one argument for this tensor, and distinct shifts have distinct infinitesimal characters. Here is why these maps split the tensor. Choose a nonzero map from each model into the tensor. Composing with a coordinate map to a different model gives zero by Schur’s lemma. Composition with its own coordinate map must be a nonzero scalar, since the coordinate maps jointly detect vectors. Rescale the inclusion to make that composition the identity. Subtracting the sum of the included coordinates from a tensor leaves a vector with every coordinate zero, hence zero. The translation and multiplicity-one results are inputs here. For the classical construction see G. Zuckerman, Annals of Mathematics 106 (1977), 295–308.

Write ps for the map that keeps a summand inside the tensor, and rs for its coordinate in the Hilbert space of πs. The first reconstructs x by summation. The second intertwines the group actions. Give the tensor its invariant form [y,x] = ∑lεl⟨yl,xl⟩ with εl equal to +1 or −1 according to the signature. Invariance and Schur's lemma give a scalar cs for the form on one summand. For smooth x,y the identities used below are

∑s psx = x,   rsA(g)x = πs(g)rsx,   [y,psx] = cs⟨rsy,rsx⟩.

The decomposition can be read one coordinate at a time. The differential operator Ω sends x to the column with entries ∑ldπ(Ecl)xl, where Ecl is the elementary matrix. On the s-summand it has eigenvalue λs + (m − 1)/2. The eigenvalues are distinct. The product over other summands of (Ω − κt)/​(κs − κt) therefore keeps precisely the s-coordinate. This argument takes place on the smooth direct sum, where the indicated derivatives exist.

What does one lowering cost in formal degree?

Fix the same Haar measure on the same group for both degrees. The formal-degree formula for a regular discrete series gives dπ = C·∏a<b(|λa − λb|/(b − a)). The positive constant C depends on the signature and Haar normalization. The quantitative version used in this moment applies when both signs occur and the opposite-sign gap is at least 120m³. Lowering one entry by one reduces any gap by at most one, so both parameters qualify under the preceding 200m³ hypothesis. This degree formula is the representation-theoretic input. The comparison below only uses the product. M. F. Atiyah and W. Schmid, §5, p. 25, formal-degree formula following (5.15).

In the ratio dπ/ds, the constant and the denominators cancel. A pair avoiding s contributes 1. For a pair touching s, let h be its gap after the shift. The coordinates lie on the same translate of the integers and the new parameter is regular, so h ≥ 1. Its old gap is at most h + 1 ≤ 2h. There are m − 1 such pairs. With X = 8mB ≥ 4 the reciprocal degree satisfies

1/ds ≤ 2m−1/dπ ≤ 22m/dπ ≤ Xm/dπ.

The middle bound is the one used in the moment. It counts a factor 2 whenever s is the first or second coordinate of an ordered pair. As a check of the ratio alone, lower the second coordinate of (13/2, 7/2, 1/2, −5/2). Its three affected gaps change from (3, 3, 6) to (4, 2, 5). Thus the ratio of the old product to the new one is (3/4)(3/2)(6/5) = 27/20. This finite product example checks the direction of the inequality independently of the large-gap hypothesis.

How much squared mass can one shifted summand carry?

Return to the pair (G,H) and the unit vectors of the mixed moment. Put ξ = iw₀, P = i∗ and κ = 1/dσ for the chosen Haar measure ν on H. The z-coordinate corresponds to the positive line fixed by H. Two estimates for ps enter with different powers of X.

First consider its entries on elv₀. The quantum cofactor identity for the differential matrix writes each entry of the projector as a signed minor of size n = m − 1, divided by a product of n nonzero parameter differences. To see the denominator, put tj = λj − (m − 1)/2. On the s-summand the cofactor matrix at u acts by ∏j≠s(u + tj). Evaluating at −ts kills the other summands and leaves the factor ∏j≠s(λj − λs), of absolute value at least one. A zero projector contributes zero throughout. For a nonzero summand the matching index is determined by its eigenvalue λs + (m − 1)/2, since the λs are distinct. The differential word estimate controls the factors in each term of this cofactor. A word of length j ≤ n in elementary matrix derivatives applied to the unit vector v₀ has norm at most Mj, with M = 8√m(B + n). There are n! permutations in the minor. Expanding its factors, whose scalar parts have size at most 4B, bounds its norm by n!(4B + M)n. The parameter spread gives n ≤ 2B, hence M ≤ 3X, 4B + M ≤ X², and n! ≤ Xn. Every entry thus has norm at most X3n ≤ X4m. Taking x = y = elv₀ in the signed form and applying Cauchy–Schwarz gives |cs|‖rs(elv₀)‖² ≤ X4m.

Second, on a smooth vector η the distinguished entry satisfies P((ps(ezη))z) = γsPη, where P is applied after taking the coordinate. Removing the z row and column leaves the quantum determinant of the smaller group. Its action on σ is scalar. The numerator is the product of the n factors μj − λs + 1/2. Each has size at most 2B + 1/2 ≤ X. The same denominator has size at least one, so |γs| ≤ Xn ≤ Xm.

It remains to explain why these two bounds multiply. The coefficient projection on H supplies a smooth test vector η with ⟨η,ξ⟩ ≠ 0. Define b = rs(ezη). Average the coefficient ⟨b,πs(h)a⟩ against φ(h) = conj(⟨w₀,σ(h)w₀⟩). This integrable weight defines a bounded functional of a, hence a vector b′ by Hilbert-space representation. Inversion symmetry and φ ∗ φ = κφ give ‖b′‖² = κ⟨b′,b⟩ by integrating twice. On the fixed positive coordinate, averaging the signed form identity gives κ⟨η,ξ⟩⟨ξ,(psx)z⟩ = cs⟨b′,rsx⟩. At x = ezη this yields |cs|‖b′‖² = κ²|⟨η,ξ⟩|²|γs|.

Now let al,s(g) = ⟨ξ,(psA(g)elv₀)z⟩. Applying the averaged identity at that translated input expresses al,s as a scalar times a coefficient of πs. Schur orthogonality gives its squared integral. The factor κ²|⟨η,ξ⟩|² cancels by the preceding identity, leaving

∫G |al,s|² dμ = |γs| · |cs|‖rs(elv₀)‖² / ds ≤ X6m/dπ.

This equality also covers a vanishing coefficient. Only the positive number κ and the nonzero test pairing were divided out. At m = 4 the scalar, the squared input and the reciprocal degree cost at most 4, 16 and 4 powers of X. The finite sums in the column moment are taken after this estimate. M. Duflo and C. C. Moore, orthogonality relations for square-integrable representations.
