How can the filtration jumps be separated while the Hecke evaluations stay close?

Write w0 for the auxiliary place where we will impose a local type, and v for the place where we change the weight. Both are split places of the CM field E above ℓ, with distinct primes below them in the real subfield. Choose an embedding τ0 of E into ℂ inducing v under the chosen ℓ-adic identification. The level K away from w0 contains the integral local group at v. For an open subgroup Kv of GLa(Ev), the initial form is fixed by the elements of K whose component at v lies in Kv. This subgroup can be smaller than K.

Weight-variation theorem. Take an automorphic representation of weight λ at this initial level, with joint Hecke eigenvalues θ. Use operators integral for the local evaluation bounds at weight λ, acting by the prescribed scalars on vectors fixed at the initial level. Each translation in their supports is trivial above both underlying auxiliary primes. Then for every ε > 0 there are finitely many systems θb and coefficients cb such that

|P(θ) − ∑b cbP(θb)|ℓ ≤ ε   for every integral polynomial P.

Each system on this list has a representative with a nonzero K-fixed vector. Its weight μ agrees with λ away from embeddings above the prime of the real subfield below v. At τ0, the jumps jk = k − μτ₀,k satisfy jk+1 − j0 > a(jk − j0) for 0 ≤ k < a − 1. The ℓ-adic approximation concerns Hecke evaluations. The estimate below on the entries of μ uses ordinary absolute values of integers.

A family that tolerates a large weight. Normalize the initial form f at a detecting coordinate of its weight-λ frame. At precision ε the family construction supplies an integer d and a matrix M(g) for each group point g. Both are chosen before the target dominant weight ν. For every ν the construction gives a family of functions Bν(y1, y2), each linear in a weight-λ test vector. To compare with f at g, substitute y1 = y2 = M(g). The difference is bounded by ε in every frame coordinate. For fixed matrix arguments the functions in the family are K-fixed.

How the local pieces recover the original form. Let C be the compact open level used to construct the family. Write c(g) ∈ C for an equivariant label of the adelic point and φ for the matrix representation of C at the varying place. Put A(g) = φ(c(g))M(g)−1. The construction uses finitely many pieces with representatives cq, a translated form Fq for each piece and a common bound R > 0 on their frame evaluations. At each point the translated form for the piece containing the label agrees with f. Take η = min(1, ε/R). The polynomial separation lemma gives polynomials Pq approximating these indicators to within η. Using the same matrix A = A(g) in every summand, the family has the explicit value

Bν(y₁, y₂)(v, g) = ∑q Pq(Ay₂) Δν(φ(cq)−1Ay₁) Fq(v, g).

Substitute y₁ = y₂ = M(g). Then AM(g) = φ(c(g)). On the selected piece the argument of the weight coefficient Δν is within η of the identity entry by entry. This coefficient is an integral polynomial in the entries times an integer power of the determinant, normalized by Δν(I) = 1. Integral polynomials take integral values on integral inputs, and their values change by at most the largest change in an entry. A determinant unit within η of one remains within η of one after any integer power, by factoring differences of powers and using inversion for negative powers. Thus on the selected piece both multipliers are within η of one. On every other piece the polynomial has absolute value at most η and the weight coefficient at most one. By the ultrametric inequality the full error is at most Rη ≤ ε. This bound holds for every dominant ν with the same polynomials.

The size of the weight error. The family lies in a finite-dimensional span of automorphic functions. Project it into finitely many irreducible automorphic representations, retaining the components on which the family is nonzero. For each such component, let sk(μ) denote the sum of the first k weight entries at τ0. The weight estimate for the family gives |sk(μ) − sk(ν)| ≤ d. These partial sums are the exponents of the highest-weight character when the first k diagonal coordinates are scaled together. Taking differences of consecutive partial sums gives |μτ₀,k − νk| ≤ 2d. At all other archimedean places the family has the original weight. Only τ0 and its complex conjugate lie over the chosen archimedean place, so this gives the weight agreement required in the theorem.

Choosing the gaps. Take A = a + 1, N = (a + 1)(4d + a) + 1 and νk = −NAk for 0 ≤ k < a. This list is dominant. Write the actual jumps as jk = k + NAk + ek with |ek| ≤ 2d. In the difference (jk+1 − j0) − a(jk − j0), the term from the geometric progression is N(Ak + a − 1), the index term is 1 − (a − 1)k, and the error has absolute value at most 4ad. The sum is positive because k < a and N > a² + 4ad. Dominance puts the jumps in increasing order. This proves the claimed separation for every retained component at once. From gaps to unequal means of disjoint sets of jumps.

Transport of Hecke coordinates. For a finite adelic translation h with trivial component at v, the construction gives M(gh) = M(g). Thus inside each term of a Hecke sum at g the two matrix arguments can be kept fixed. The operator acts by the scalar of that projected representation, since each function with fixed matrix arguments belongs to its K-fixed subspace and thus also satisfies the initial level condition. Adding the components gives the ε approximation in the original frame. The uniform polynomial estimate now applies to the specialized functions and produces one finite list for all P. Polynomial induction on the Hecke operators.

To impose the type at w0 in the next step, keep the resulting weight μ. The placewise integrality bound for a Hecke translation only counts weights over primes where it has a nonidentity component. All positions where the weight could have changed are above the prime under v, where the translation is trivial. The original scalings of the Hecke operators still meet the bound. The common level and the translated forms give the uniform bound used in the sum. Its finite decomposition, the two bounds on partial sums, and the weights at the other places are proved below.
