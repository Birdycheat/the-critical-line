How to choose the prime in the inverse ideal class

The Jacobi sum uses a prime ideal P whose residue field has a prime number of elements. To apply that calculation to an arbitrary nonzero integral ideal 𝔞, the same P must make 𝔞P principal. For a prime of a number field K above a rational prime p, its absolute norm NP is the residue field size pf. Thus absolute residue degree one means NP = p. We can require this degree, prescribe the ideal class [P] = c and exclude any finite set of prime ideals of K. The degree over the rational residue field · The prime selected in an ideal class

Let H be the Hilbert class field of K. Its Artin isomorphism sends [P] to the arithmetic Frobenius at a prime above P, acting on residues by x ↦ xNP. Apply the degree-one prime theorem to H/K with target automorphism Art(c). The Frobenius supplied by the theorem restricts to this precise automorphism. Two Frobenius lifts at the same place agree modulo inertia, and inertia in H/K is trivial. Applying Art−1 gives [P] = c. In the cyclotomic application take c = [𝔞]−1 and exclude the primes dividing N𝒪K, where N is the cyclotomic level. This set is finite because N ≠ 0. Then p ∤ N, reduction preserves the order of ζN, and N divides p − 1 as the character construction requires. The inverse on [𝔞] comes from [𝔞][P] = 1. Write L/K for the finite Galois extension and g for the requested automorphism. The proof chooses an auxiliary prime ℓ with [L : K] dividing ℓ − 1 and Φℓ irreducible over L. In L(ζℓ) choose an automorphism h restricting to g on L and raising ζℓ to a generator τ of (ℤ/ℓℤ)×. Irreducibility permits this choice by adjusting a lift of g with an automorphism fixing L. Above the finite exclusions and the ramified primes of L/K there are only finitely many primes of Z. Away from these, seek a prime w with Nw ≡ τ modulo ℓ. A prime-distribution result gives divergence of the sum of reciprocal prime-ideal norms in this residue class. See the inverse class in a quadratic field

In ℚ(√−23) let ω = (1 + √−23)/2, so ω² − ω + 6 = 0. Exclude the primes above 2 and start with 𝔞 = (2,ω). Taking P = (3,ω) gives residue field 𝔽₃. The two ideals are coprime and both divide (ω). Their norms 2 and 3 multiply to N(ω) = 6, hence 𝔞P = (ω). Here the inverse class is genuinely different. The norm form a² + ab + 6b² never takes the value 2, so 𝔞 is nonprincipal. The element ω − 2 has norm 8 and is nonzero modulo the other prime (2,ω − 1) above 2, giving (ω − 2) = 𝔞³. Thus [𝔞] has order three and [P] = [𝔞]².
