Why do the derivatives of the period columns give a nilpotent matrix?

Fix a prime ℓ, a finite extension K/ℚℓ, and a stable lattice of rank d in a semistable representation ρ of its absolute Galois group GK. In the chosen period rings write Bst = Bcris[X]. The invariant period columns form an invertible matrix Q satisfying ρ(g)g(Q) = Q for every g. The choice of logarithm fixes the action on its variable. Differentiating a column gives another vector in the same basis. Its coordinates will control how far the column moves when the logarithm is translated.

m = Q−1∂Q,   f = Q−1φ(Q),   ∂ = d/dX.

Constant coordinates. Both matrices have entries in K₀, the maximal unramified subextension of K/ℚℓ, and f is invertible over K₀. In the invariant basis the monodromy operator N = −∂ has matrix −m.

Proof. Write t for the cyclotomic period. The group acts on the variable by g(X) = X + κℓ(g)t, where κℓ is the Kummer cocycle for the chosen parameter. Since the shift is independent of X, differentiation commutes with this action. The entries of ρ(g) are ℓ-adic scalars. Hence ρ(g)g(∂Q) = ∂Q. Also g(Q−1) = Q−1ρ(g). Multiplying these identities gives g(m) = m. Frobenius φ commutes with the Galois action and fixes ℚℓ, so the same calculation with φ(Q) gives g(f) = f. Its inverse is φ(Q−1)Q, which is fixed as well.

The arithmetic input is the Kummer non-coboundary theorem. There is no z in Bcris such that g(z) − z = κℓ(g)t for every g in GK. The crystalline fixed-field theorem gives BcrisGK = K₀. For the classical period rings the non-coboundary assertion also follows from the semistable fixed-field theorem, since such a z would make X − z a fixed polynomial of degree one. Brinon and Conrad, §9.1, Theorem 9.1.5 and its fixed-field consequence · §9.2, Theorem 9.2.10.

To see how the Kummer obstruction enters the matrix calculation, take a fixed polynomial P and induct on its degree. A constant fixed polynomial belongs to K₀ by the crystalline theorem. In positive degree, −∂P is fixed and has lower degree. By induction it is a constant a in K₀. Comparing coefficients in characteristic zero gives P = z − aX with z in Bcris. If a ≠ 0, the identity g(P) = P gives g(z/a) − z/a = κℓ(g)t for every g, a contradiction. Thus every fixed polynomial is constant. Applying this entry by entry gives the asserted coefficient field.

∂jQ = Qmj   (j ≥ 0),   md = 0.

Indeed, ∂Q = Qm and ∂m = 0, so the product rule proves the derivative formula by induction. Choose an integer D larger than every entry degree of Q. Then ∂DQ = 0 and multiplication by Q−1 gives mD = 0. The embedding of K₀ into the period ring is injective, so this equality holds over the field K₀. Over an algebraic closure every eigenvalue of m therefore has Dth power zero. Its characteristic polynomial is Td, and Cayley–Hamilton gives md = 0. This bound depends only on the number of columns. The Stacks Project, Lemma 10.16.1.

How do Galois and Frobenius act at logarithm zero?

Keep the same period rings, stable lattice, and constant matrices m and f. Suppose the chosen logarithm is L = X + b, with b in Bcris, and satisfies g(L) = L + sg and φ(L) = ℓL, where sg = c(g)t with c(g) in ℤℓ. Write evx for evaluation at X = x. At L = 0 the period matrix becomes U = Q(−b) over Bcris. Its inverse is Q−1(−b), since evaluation preserves products.

ev−b−sg ∘ g = g ∘ ev−b,   ev−b ∘ φ = φ ∘ ev−b.

Both identities agree on the coefficient ring. To check them on X, subtract the Galois formulas for L and X. They give g(b) − b = (c(g) − κℓ(g))t. Hence evaluating g(X) at −b − sg gives −g(b). For Frobenius, φ(X) = ℓX and the normalization of L gives φ(b) = ℓb. Evaluating φ(X) at −b thus gives −φ(b). A polynomial homomorphism is determined by its action on the coefficients and on X.

Evaluate ρ(g)g(Q) = Q at −b − sg. The left side is ρ(g)g(U). On the right, expand Q(−b − sg) about −b. For a polynomial, the coefficient of the jth power of the increment at a point is the jth derivative there divided by j!, as one sees by expanding each monomial. Here that coefficient is Umj/j!, and it vanishes for j ≥ d. Consequently ρ(g)g(U) = U exp(−sgm), with the exponential a finite sum. Evaluating φ(Q) = Qf at −b gives φ(U) = Uf.

Integral coefficients. Let W = W(kK) be the Witt ring of the residue field of K. The unramified field K₀ is W[1/ℓ] with the chosen coefficient embedding. For each of the three matrices f, f−1 and m, take the maximum of the finitely many nonnegative exponents needed to clear its entries. Denote the respective exponents by a, a′ and k. This gives F = ℓaf, F′ = ℓa′f−1 and M = −ℓkm over W. In particular Md = 0 in the Witt ring. The negative sign identifies ℓ−kM with the matrix of monodromy.

ρ(g)g(U) = U ∑0≤j<d sgjMj / (j! ℓkj).
ℓc₁φ(U) = UF,   FF′ = ℓc₃I,   c₁ = a,   c₃ = a + a′.

The first Frobenius exponent clears the forward matrix. The second also pays for its inverse because ff−1 = I. The exponential acts on the right of U because successive derivatives of Q multiply its columns by m on the right. The logarithm-zero calculation tests the sign and the order of multiplication.
