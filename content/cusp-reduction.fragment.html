How does a shortest row control the cusp diagonal?

Take a number field L of degree d over ℚ and an adelic invertible matrix g of size N ≥ 1. For a nonzero row x over L, form xg at every place. Its height Hg(x) is the product of the Euclidean lengths at the infinite places, squared at complex places, and the maximum entry norms at the finite places. The product formula makes this height invariant under scaling x by L×. Below any fixed height there are only finitely many rational lines. Place a minimizing row last in a rational basis and factor the resulting matrix as an upper triangular matrix times a maximal compact element. The right compact factor preserves all the row lengths. For N ≥ 2 the only new adjacent pair is the bottom block [[α, u], [0, β]]. Write A = |α|𝔸, B = |β|𝔸 for the products of their normalized local absolute values. The last row has height B. Two-dimensional approximation produces a nonzero rational row whose image under this block has height at most C√(AB), with C depending only on L. Putting the two coordinates in the last two slots gives a competitor for the minimum, since the triangular matrix has zeros to their left. Hence

Where the square root comes from

If A ≥ B, the row (0, 1) already works. Otherwise put s = (A/B)1/(2d). Divide the block by β. After multiplying its first row by a scalar in L×, the upper left entry is a positive idèle of archimedean size s² times an element of a fixed compact set. Call the adjusted upper right entry a. In an integral basis, (H + 1)d candidate multiples of a fall into Hd boxes after rational translation to clear finite denominators and reduction modulo the integer lattice at infinity. Two lie in one box. Their difference gives a nonzero integer ℓ of L of archimedean size O(H + 1) and c ∈ L such that ℓa + c is integral at all finite places and O(1/H) at infinity. For H = ⌈1/s⌉ both coordinates of the resulting row have size O(s) at infinity. Its finite norm product is bounded by compactness. To obtain the real cusp parameters, take rᵢ = |bᵢᵢ|𝔸1/d. The idèle with value rᵢ at every infinite place and 1 at every finite place has norm rᵢd. Dividing it out leaves a norm-one idèle. Rational diagonal scalings bring these remainders into compact representatives of the norm-one idèle classes. Rational upper triangular row additions bring the entries of the upper unitriangular factor into compact additive representatives. The resulting factorization has the fixed compact triangular set, positive diagonal and maximal compact subgroup used in the cusp cover, with t ri+1 ≤ rᵢ for t = C−2/d. At finite places the positive diagonal is the identity, so for g = (x, 1) the rational change of basis has finite component in a fixed compact set. Check the complex-place exponent over ℚ(i)

Take g∞ = [[1, 2], [0, 3]] and identity at every finite place. Every rational line has a primitive Gaussian integer representative (u, v), whose finite height factors are 1. Its height is |u|² + |2u + 3v|². Height 1 would require a unit u with 3 dividing 2u, since u = 0 gives height 9 for a primitive row. The row (1, −1) attains height 2. Use it as the last row of γ = [[0, −1], [1, −1]]. Multiplying γg∞ on the right by the unitary matrix [[1, 1], [1, −1]]/√2 gives [[−3/√2, 3/√2], [0, √2]]. At finite places γ is integral and invertible, so the triangular factor there can be the identity. The last diagonal idèle has norm 2, and its real parameter is √2. The first has norm 9/2 and real parameter 3/√2.
