Which changes of sign can be realized by a finite adelic norm?

The marked-pair comparison requires one scalar with two properties. It is positive on the chosen eigenline at the distinguished place and negative on its companions. At the finite places away from p we need a change of variables giving this same scalar as a norm.

Proposition. Suppose that M is a number field with a nonidentity endomorphism σ inducing complex conjugation under every embedding φ into ℂ. Let K be its fixed field and S a set of embeddings of M stable under conjugation. A scalar a ∈ K× can have the prescribed signs and be a norm at every finite place precisely when 4 divides |S|. Here the prescribed sign is negative at each φ ∈ S and positive at all other embeddings. At a finite place v the norm is taken from the quadratic algebra Kv ⊗K M to Kv. When this holds, at each rational prime p one obtains a unit u in 𝔸fp ⊗ℚ M, where 𝔸fp denotes the finite rational adeles away from p, satisfying

u (1 ⊗ σ)(u) = 1 ⊗ a.

Proof. Applying any embedding gives σ² = 1 by injectivity. Pick s moved by σ and put t = s − σs. Then t ≠ 0 and σt = −t. Every x in M has the unique expression b + d t with coefficients in K, where b = (x + σx)/2 and d = (x − σx)/(2t). Every embedding of K extends to M and has real image because σ fixes K. Since [M : K] = 2, each real embedding has two extensions. They send t to opposite, purely imaginary values and are complex conjugates. Consequently S lies over a set T of exactly |S|/2 real places.

At a real place, the norm on ℂ× is z ↦ |z|², with image the positive real numbers. Form the idèle y of K taking the value −1 at T and 1 everywhere else. The local character of M/K takes the value +1 on norms and −1 otherwise. Writing ηv for these characters, we obtain

∏v ηv(yv) = (−1)|T|,   |S| = 2|T|.

The global Artin reciprocity theorem identifies the kernel of this product with the principal idèles times the norm idèles. Thus for even |T| we can choose a ∈ K× so that y/a is a norm idèle. At each real place yv/a is positive. At every finite place 1/a is a norm, so its inverse a is a norm there as well. Conversely the product formula applied to any such a gives (−1)|T| = 1. Equivalently, one may handle two real places at a time and multiply the scalars and finite witnesses. The disjoint pairs retain exactly the prescribed negative signs. Kedlaya, Notes on class field theory, Definition 7.5.1 and Proposition 7.5.6.

In coordinates, write δ = t² ∈ K×. The norm equation becomes x² − δy² = a. At almost every finite place 2, δ and a are units. In its residue field of odd order q, the sets of squares and of elements a + δy² each have (q + 1)/2 elements. They intersect, giving a solution with a nonzero coordinate because a is a unit. The partial derivative in that coordinate is a unit, so Hensel’s lemma lifts this solution to the local integers. At each remaining place take a solution supplied by reciprocity. The coordinates now form finite adeles X, Y of K. The finite adele U = X + Y t of M has norm a and inverse σ(U)/a. To view its restriction as a tensor, choose an integral basis of the ring of integers of M. At a rational prime q, this basis identifies the product of the local integer rings above q with the ℤq-span of the basis, and their fraction algebras with ℚq ⊗ℚ M. Therefore the coordinates of a finite adele in that basis are integral almost everywhere. Omitting the coordinates above p gives exactly the unit in the norm identity. Stacks Project, Lemma 10.153.3, simple-root form of Hensel’s lemma.

The marked root. In the regular irreducible case let M = F[γ] over the CM field F. Assume the minimal polynomial of γ is separable of odd degree n, and |φ(γ)|² = c at every embedding for the same nonzero rational similitude multiplier c. In any one realization of M in ℂ, conjugation preserves M because it preserves F and sends γ to c/γ. Transport this map back to M. The resulting σ induces conjugation in every realization since it agrees on F and the formula on γ holds at every embedding. It acts nontrivially on F. Choose the embedding τ representing the distinguished place of positive index one, and a root μ above τ. Of the n extensions of τ exactly one sends γ to μ. Take the other n − 1 and their conjugates for S. The lists are disjoint because τ differs from its conjugate on F. Thus |S| = 2(n − 1) is divisible by four. For n = 3 the two unmarked embeddings above τ yield two negative real places, while the marked embedding stays positive. The power basis supplies a polynomial A ∈ F[X] with A(γ) = a. This is the arithmetic input for the change of Hermitian form in the comparison of pairs.

Example. Put K = ℚ(√5), M = K(i) and a = −1. At every odd finite place the residue-field count and lift above solve x² + y² = −1. The integer (1 + √5)/2 satisfies X² − X − 1 = 0. Its reduction gives the irreducible polynomial X² + X + 1 over 𝔽₂, so the quadratic completion above 2 has residue field 𝔽₄. A simple residue root of X² + X + 1 lifts to a primitive cube root ζ in the completion. Taking x = ζ and y = ζ² gives both coordinates explicitly, since ζ² + (ζ²)² = ζ² + ζ = −1. This verifies every finite place. Both real embeddings take a to −1, whereas a global norm from M× must be positive there.

What changes if the prime 2 is omitted?

The necessity of parity in the proposition uses every finite place. For ℚ(i)/ℚ the same count and lift give x² + y² = −1 at every odd prime. Hence −1 is a finite adelic norm away from 2 despite its single negative real place. The obstruction at 2 is visible modulo 4. Integral squares sum to 0, 1 or 2 modulo 4, whereas −1 is 3. If a denominator were needed, clearing a minimal common power of two would give a sum divisible by 4 with at least one odd numerator, also impossible.
