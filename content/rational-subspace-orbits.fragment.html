Which ideal records the isometry class of a rational subspace?

The row count first chooses a subspace, then counts its possible intersection lattices. At this first stage two subspaces count as one choice if an E-linear isometry of the ambient Hermitian space carries one onto the other. Fix their dimension j and a positive integer n. We want to cover all subspaces U that admit an integral lattice L of discriminant order |L∨/L| = n. The ambient form is positive definite at every infinite place. The CM field E is quadratic over its real subfield F and unramified at all finite places of F.

Enlarge L to a maximal integral lattice M inside U. A prime ideal 𝔓 of 𝒪E is bad for M if its dual quotient is nonzero there. By the local structure of maximal lattices, this happens only above inert places. The quotient at such a prime is a line over the residue field. Multiplying these orders and applying the index-square identity gives

𝔞 = ∏𝔓 bad 𝔓,   N(𝔞) = |M∨/M|,   n = N(𝔞)[M ∶ L]².

Here N is the absolute ideal norm and the index counts elements of the additive quotient. The empty product gives the unit ideal. The ideal retains the identity of every bad prime by unique factorization of ideals. Its integer norm bounds the size of the label. Two different prime ideals can have the same norm.

Why does this information determine the rational space? Choose an E-basis of U, let β be its Gram determinant, and consider a second space of the same dimension with Gram determinant β′. Both determinants belong to F and are positive at every real embedding. At an inert prime a basis change multiplies the determinant by a norm, whose valuation is even. In a basis of a maximal lattice at that place the valuation is zero for a unimodular lattice and one for a lattice with a defect line. Thus equal bad ideals say exactly that β and β′ have the same valuation parity at each inert place.

The following consequence of Landherr’s theorem is used here. A totally positive definite Hermitian space over this E that admits a unimodular lattice contains an orthonormal E-basis. Indeed, at every inert place the Gram determinant in a local integral basis of a unimodular lattice is a unit, and units are local norms in an unramified quadratic extension. Passing to any rational basis multiplies this determinant by another norm. At a split place the local space is determined by its rank. At every real place the signature agrees with that of the identity matrix. Landherr’s local-to-global classification therefore identifies the space with the standard space. The integral span of the resulting orthonormal basis can be a different lattice from the one we started with. B. H. Gross, Incoherent definite spaces and Shimura varieties, §3, Theorem 3.1.

Add the same Hermitian line of squared length β to each space. Their determinants become β² and ββ′. Both have even valuation at every inert place. Take a maximal integral lattice in each enlarged space. Such a lattice exists by clearing denominators in a basis and enlarging an integral lattice. The determinant test just proved rules out defects at every inert place. The split places are already unimodular. A finite dual quotient vanishing at every prime is zero, so both enlarged lattices are unimodular. The preceding consequence of Landherr gives an orthonormal basis for each enlarged space. Matching those bases gives an isometry between them. It remains to remove the common line.

How does an isometry of two subspaces move them inside the ambient space?

Witt extension in the definite case. In a finite-dimensional positive definite Hermitian space over E, any E-linear isometry between two subspaces extends to an isometry of the entire space. We give the construction because the argument uses it twice. Write h conjugate-linear in its first entry. If x and y have the same squared length and x ≠ y, put w = x − y and α = h(w,x). Equal lengths give h(w,w) = α + ᾱ. Positivity implies α ≠ 0. Define

R(z) = z − w · h(w,z)/α.

Substituting x gives y, and substituting a vector orthogonal to w fixes it. Expand h(Rz,Rt) − h(z,t). The coefficient of h(z,w)h(w,t) is (−ᾱ − α + h(w,w))/(ᾱα) = 0. Thus R preserves the form, so it is injective, hence bijective in finite dimension. To extend a specified subspace isometry, match the images of its basis vectors one at a time. The difference between the current and prescribed image of the next vector is orthogonal to all already matched images, since all their pairings agree. The displayed map makes the correction and fixes the earlier images. Equal vectors require no correction.

Apply this first in the enlarged target space. The isometry constructed from the two orthonormal bases can send the distinguished line generator somewhere else. Its image and the prescribed generator both have squared length β, so the correction sends one to the other. The resulting isometry carries the orthogonal complement of the source line onto the orthogonal complement of the target line. These complements are the two original spaces. Apply Witt extension a second time, now in the original ambient space V. It extends the resulting isometry between the two subspaces of V. This proves that all subspaces with the same bad ideal and rank lie in a single ambient rational orbit.

One orbit can meet the ambient lattice in different ways. In the standard plane over any such field, the unit vectors x = (1,0) and y = (3/5,4/5) each span a unimodular lattice on their own line. They give n = 1 and the unit ideal. The map above has matrix (1/5) times the matrix with rows (3,4) and (4,−3). It moves the first line onto the second. In the ambient lattice 𝒪E², however, the second line meets that lattice in 𝒪E(3,4). Indeed, if 3c and 4c are integral then c = 4c − 3c is integral. In the coordinate with unit vector y, the intersection is 5𝒪Ey. On the slice with rational coordinates its integer points are the multiples of (3,4), since the rational algebraic integers are precisely ℤ. Over 𝒪E it is the intersection module that the row argument counts after choosing a rational orbit.

How many rational subspace orbits can one discriminant order require?

Orbit bound. Write D = [E ∶ ℚ]. For fixed dimension j and n ≥ 1, at most n1+log₂D rational isometry orbits suffice to cover all subspaces admitting an integral lattice of discriminant order n. If j = 0 the only candidate is the zero space, whose lattice has order one. For positive rank, select one representative for each bad ideal that occurs at that rank with norm at most n. Every admissible subspace has such a label by the index-square identity. The two isometry extensions show that the representative for its label covers it. This covering argument only uses ideals that occur on some subspace of the chosen dimension.

It suffices to count all nonzero integral ideals of norm at most n. To count ideals of a single norm k > 1, fix a rational prime p dividing k. Factorization of ideals shows that each ideal of norm k has a prime divisor 𝔓 above p. If its residue degree is f𝔓, removing this factor leaves norm k/pf𝔓. There are at most D choices for 𝔓, because ∑𝔓∣p e𝔓f𝔓 = D, with all summands positive. J. S. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, §3, ideal factorization and Theorem 3.34.

Let Ω(k) count the rational prime factors of k with multiplicity. Each removal decreases Ω by at least one. The unit ideal is the only possibility at norm one. Induction gives at most DΩ(k) ideals of norm k, allowing repeated counts if several prime divisors work. Since 2Ω(k) ≤ k, we obtain

# orbits ≤ ∑k=1n DΩ(k) ≤ ∑k=1n klog₂D ≤ n1+log₂D.

For n = 4, the index-square identity alone permits ideal norms 1 and 4, with indices 2 and 1. There is a further check. Each bad prime lies above an inert place and has norm q² ≥ 4. A bad ideal of norm 4 would therefore contain exactly one such prime. At that place the determinant has odd valuation. Everywhere else it is a local norm, including all real places by positivity. Landherr’s product condition requires the product of these local signs to be +1, excluding this single negative sign. Only the unit ideal can occur in this example. The general bound deliberately counts all ideals of norm at most n and needs no test of which ones are realized. Gross, §3, Theorem 3.1, product condition.

In the row count the order is n = d² and D = 2f. The factor n1+log₂(2f) counts the rational choices once. For each choice the two intersection masses and the gluing count supply the remaining factors of the mean.
