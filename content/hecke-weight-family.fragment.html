Why does the sum over local pieces define a finite family of automorphic forms?

Fix the original weight space W and the finite level K of the weight-variation theorem. The construction of the translates gives a compact open level C containing K, a label c(g) ∈ C and forms Fq of weight W fixed by C. Choose the finitely many indicator polynomials Pq at the required precision. For every dominant integral weight ν, the sum defining Bν then has the following property. As the two matrices y₁, y₂ range over GLa(ℂ) and the vector u ranges over W, its values are automorphic forms in one finite-dimensional complex space. This space may depend on ν. The integer d in the two exponent bounds below can be chosen before ν.

Comparing the two places. Let τ be the chosen complex embedding of the CM field E, let v be the place over ℓ it induces under the field identification ι from the algebraic closure of ℚℓ to ℂ, and let α(g) be the matrix of the infinite component of g at τ. The embedding ι−1τ extends to an embedding of the completion Ev into the algebraic closure of ℚℓ. Apply it entry by entry to the matrix at v, then apply ι, to obtain κ(g). Both α and κ respect multiplication. For a rational point γ they give the same matrix, since both apply τ to its entries. On C the infinite matrix is the identity, and the restriction of κ to C is the homomorphism φ used to construct the polynomials. On the archimedean group the finite matrix is the identity. A. V. Sutherland, Number Theory I, Lecture 12, Corollary 12.24 and its proof.

M(g) = α(g)−1κ(g),   A(g) = φ(c(g))M(g)−1.

Left multiplication by γ leaves M and the label unchanged. For t ∈ C we have M(gt) = M(g)φ(t) and c(gt) = c(g)t. The two copies of φ(t) thus cancel in A(gt). For an archimedean element x, the label stays fixed and M(gx) = α(x)−1M(g), giving A(gx) = A(g)α(x). Finally, if h is finite adelic and its component at v is the identity, both α(h) and κ(h) are the identity. Hence M(gh) = M(g). This last identity lets every term of a Hecke sum use the same pair of evaluation matrices.

How translation acts on the family. Write RxF(g) = F(gx). At finite level both A and Fq are fixed by C, so their product in each summand is fixed by K. At infinity, A acquires the right factor α(x), and Fq(u, gx) = Fq(W(x)u, g). Substitution gives the identity of functions

RxBν(y₁, y₂)(u) = Bν(α(x)y₁, α(x)y₂)(W(x)u).

The first matrix argument also records a character. The coefficient Δν(h) evaluates the image of a highest-weight vector under h against a fixed linear functional. For an invertible upper triangular matrix b, its upper unitriangular part fixes this vector and its diagonal multiplies it by χν(b) = ∏i biiνi. Thus Δν(hb) = χν(b)Δν(h). Replacing y₁ by y₁b multiplies the whole family value by this scalar, since y₁ occurs only in Δν.

One finite span at fixed ν. Write Xq = φ(cq)−1. For a matrix variable n, every function n ↦ Pq(ny₂) lies in the span of the monomials of degree at most deg Pq in its a² entries. Indeed, multiplying by y₂ substitutes linear forms for those entries. The translates n ↦ Δν(Xqny₁) also span a finite-dimensional space. If Δν(h) = L(ρν(h)w), the only factor varying with y₁ is the vector ρν(y₁)w in the fixed finite-dimensional representation. Substitution n = A(g) maps both function spaces linearly into functions of g. The functions Fq(u, ·) are themselves a linear image of the finite-dimensional space W. Products of one basis element from each of the three spaces span every summand at q. Taking the sum over the finitely many q proves the common finite-span assertion.

Each family value is left invariant under rational points and locally constant in the finite variables by its open level. Along the archimedean group, the covariance formula expresses its translates through continuous matrix coefficients and polynomials in the entries of α(x). This gives continuity there, and hence on the product with the finite adeles. All these archimedean translates lie in the common finite span just proved. These are the defining conditions for an automorphic form on the definite unitary group used here.

Two tests on a circle. For 0 ≤ k ≤ a, put T = diag(z, …, z, 1, …, 1) with k copies of z on the unit circle. Let x(z) be its archimedean realization at τ, with the other infinite places fixed, and put uz = W(x(z))u. Write sk(ν) for the sum of the first k entries of ν. All statements about an exponent m refer to a term zm with nonzero coefficient in the indicated Laurent polynomial. For every g, u, y₁ and y₂ the bounds are

Bν(Ty₁, Ty₂)(uz, g)   has exponents   m ≤ sk(ν) + d,
Bν(I, Ty₂)(uz, g)   has exponents   m ≥ −d.

To see this, bound the exponents in the original weight coefficients by −dW and dW. Such a bound exists because each matrix entry of the algebraic representation restricted to a diagonal circle is a Laurent polynomial, and there are finitely many entries and choices of k. At the conjugate embedding the circle parameter is inverted, which preserves this symmetric bound. The factor Pq(ATy₂) has exponents between 0 and its total degree because every entry of ATy₂ is affine in z. When both arguments move, Δν(XqATy₁) is a coefficient of ρν(T) with fixed vector and functional. Its exponents are at most sk(ν) by the highest-weight circle estimate. When the first argument is fixed at I, this same factor is constant in z. Exponents add in products, so d = dW + maxq deg Pq works in both tests and survives summation.

In rank one the two tests can give different exponents even for a single summand. Take W trivial, P(t) = t, ν = −4 and a nonzero constant form. Here Δν(t) = t−4 and we may take d = 1. At k = 1, moving both scalar arguments gives a multiple of z−3, attaining the upper bound −4 + 1. Holding the first at 1 gives a multiple of z, whose exponent is at least −1. To bound the partial sums of a weight actually occurring in an automorphic representation, we will project the family and apply the partial-sum estimate to each nonzero component.
