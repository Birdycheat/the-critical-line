How can frames of different ranks be compared in one coordinate system?

Until now the precision measured the accuracy of a witness on one representation. We now vary the representation itself. At level n the lattice has rank dn, its period matrix is Tn, and a matrix Πn projects it onto a fixed target of rank d modulo ℓn. The transition fn from level n + 1 to level n intertwines the actions and satisfies Πnfn ≡ Πn+1 modulo ℓn. Here Πn intertwines the level action with the target action modulo ℓn and has a right inverse modulo that power. All the frames use the same local field, Breuil–Kisin datum and primitive period τ. They have the same height bound H.

Write 𝔖 = W(k)[[u]] for the coefficient ring. The maps e and eW evaluate its series in the integral period ring Ainf = W(𝒪ℂℓ♭) and in the larger Witt ring W = W(ℂℓ♭), respectively. Set v = eW(u). Its first Witt coefficient is a nonzero element of positive valuation, so v is a unit in W. We use an intermediate ring 𝒪ℰ inside W. An element x belongs to it when, at every precision n, some power vb makes vbx congruent to an evaluated series modulo ℓn. This permits a denominator in u whose exponent may depend on the precision. We will keep track of when the coefficients return to 𝔖.

At one positive level the finite-level comparison theorem gives a square invertible matrix Bn over W and rectangular matrices Cn, Dn over 𝒪ℰ. Modulo ℓn they satisfy ΠnTn ≡ BnCn and ΠnTnDn ≡ Bn. Cancelling Bn gives CnDn ≡ Id. The integral comparison for an equivariant map gives a matrix Yn over 𝔖 with Tn eW(Yn) = fnTn+1. Thus the rectangular factors combine into a square change of coordinates:

Un = Cn eW(Yn) Dn+1,  BnUn ≡ Bn+1 (mod ℓn).

Indeed the intervening product is ΠnfnTn+1Dn+1. For n ≥ 1 the congruence makes det Un a unit modulo ℓ in W. We use the factorization theorem for 𝒪ℰ: every nonzero element is ℓa times a unit of that ring. The determinant therefore has a = 0, and the adjugate gives Un−1 over 𝒪ℰ.

Start with H₁ = I and put Hn+1 = Un−1Hn. Multiplying the displayed congruence on the right by Hn+1 makes consecutive matrices BnHn agree modulo ℓn. Completeness of the Witt ring gives a limit T∗. Its reduction is the invertible reduction of B₁, so T∗ is invertible. The theorem on the Witt ring of a perfect field supplies both completeness and the residue-field test for units. Brinon–Conrad, §4.2.

Set Rn = Hn−1Cn and R′n = DnHn. The same target coordinates now work at every level:

ΠnTn ≡ T∗Rn,  RnR′n ≡ Id (mod ℓn).

At n = 0 both congruences hold automatically. The entries of the comparison matrices lie in 𝒪ℰ. In the next step the vectors on which they act have coefficients in the smaller ring 𝔖.

Which part of a layer can every deeper layer still represent?

Fix the precision n. For m ≥ n let In,m be the image of the map s ↦ Rm eW(s) modulo ℓn, with s a column of dm series in 𝔖. Substituting the transition equation in the common coordinates and cancelling T∗ gives Rm eW(Ym) ≡ Rm+1 modulo ℓm. Each deeper image is therefore contained in the preceding one. We also need an inclusion going in the other direction after multiplication by one power of u:

For each n there exists c ≥ 0 such that vcIn,n ⊆ In,m for every m ≥ n.

First absorb the cost of two height bounds. Let τ be the primitive period and put a = τ2H. Its first Witt coefficient a₀ is nonzero. The first coefficient of e(u) has positive valuation, so a sufficiently large power is divisible by a₀ in the valuation ring of the tilt. Lifting the quotient gives h in Ainf with ha = z + ℓd for z = e(u)b. Multiplying by the finite sum zn−1 + zn−2(−ℓd) + ⋯ + (−ℓd)n−1 shows that e(u)bn ≡ yτ2H modulo ℓn for some y in Ainf. Take c = bn. Precision zero is automatic. The choice uses H and n before a deeper layer or a column is chosen.

For each level j, the height bound supplies integral period matrices Pj and P′j with Pj = τHTj after inclusion in W, and PjP′j = τ2HI. Since τ is a unit in W, TjP′j = τHI there. For a chosen m ≥ n, let Sm be a right inverse of the projection Πm modulo ℓm, lifted to a matrix of ℓ-adic integers. Given s at level n, form the column

am = y P′mSmΠnPn e(s).

Its entries lie in Ainf. Multiply by T∗Rm modulo ℓn. The two period identities contribute τH each, and ΠmSm contributes the identity. What remains is yτ2HΠnTn eW(s), which agrees with T∗Rn eW(ucs). Cancelling T∗ gives the desired equality with an integral period column on the deeper layer.

To recover a column over 𝔖 use the coefficient retraction theorem. It supplies a 𝔖-linear map p from Ainf to 𝔖 with p(1) = 1, hence p(e(s)) = s. At precision n multiply both the matrix Rm and the target by the same power of v so that all their entries agree with evaluated series. The resulting congruence between integral elements already holds in Ainf: divisibility by ℓn in W is detected by the first n Witt coordinates, and inclusion of the corresponding tilt rings is injective. Apply p entrywise. Its linearity over 𝔖 preserves the congruence and replaces the trial column by one over 𝔖. In W the common power of v cancels. This proves the inclusion with the same c at every deeper layer.

Why do the images stop shrinking at each fixed precision?

Fix n and take the exponent c from the common multiplier. Each In,m is a subgroup of In,n and contains vcIn,n. Represent an element of the ambient image In,n by a tuple s of dn series at level n. Whether its image survives at level m can be decided from finitely many coefficients of s.

Indeed changing s by ℓna + ucb changes its image by an element of every deeper image: the first term vanishes modulo ℓn, and the second belongs by the common multiplier. Thus a label for the membership test is a tuple modulo (ℓn, uc). Each series is represented uniquely by a polynomial of degree less than c with coefficients in W(k)/ℓn. For q = |k|, the first n Witt coordinates give qn possible coefficients. The number of available labels is therefore

| (𝔖/(ℓn, uc))dn | = qn c dn.

For each m keep just the labels whose level-n images belong to In,m. These finite sets decrease. Each strict decrease discards at least one label, so there is a last change if there are any changes at all. Equal sets of surviving labels give equal images because every element to be tested already lies in In,n. Hence there exists a level m₀(n) after which the image is constant. This is the Mittag-Leffler condition. Stacks Project, Definition 10.86.1 and Example 10.86.2.

For a small model take the ideals 𝔽₂[[u]], u𝔽₂[[u]] and u²𝔽₂[[u]]. The four labels modulo u² are 0, 1, u, 1 + u. The second ideal retains 0 and u. The last retains only 0, although the ideal itself still contains infinitely many series. Repeating the middle ideal for as many levels as desired postpones the last change. Counting labels bounds how often the image can change. The stabilizing level may still be arbitrarily large.

Can a vector that survives at one precision be lifted through every precision?

Denote the stable image at precision n by Jn. Choose x in Jn. At a single level m ≥ max(m₀(n), m₀(n + 1)) we can represent x by a column of series because the image has already stabilized at precision n. Evaluate that very column at precision n + 1. Its image belongs to Jn+1 because this higher precision has also stabilized at m, and its reduction is x. This proves surjectivity of the reduction between successive stable images. It requires no surjectivity of the individual maps between the approximating lattices. Stacks Project, Example 10.86.2.

Start with any column s at the level m = m₀(n). Apply the preceding lifting step to its image, and then to each successive lift. We obtain compatible residues at all precisions above n. At lower precisions reduce the original column: nestedness puts its image in the image from the corresponding lower level. Since W is separated and complete for powers of ℓ, the chosen compatible residues determine a unique column x in Wd. Different choices of lifts can give different columns. Stacks Project, Lemma 10.86.3. Brinon–Conrad, §4.2.

x ≡ Rm eW(s) (mod ℓn),  and for every j there is sj with x ≡ Rj eW(sj) (mod ℓj).

The level m works for every starting column at the chosen precision. The limit column may depend on that starting column. This order of choices lets us lift several prescribed columns at the same level in the next argument.

Why can the surviving columns be chosen to form a basis?

First locate any limit column x. Its entries are approximated to arbitrary ℓ-adic precision by entries in 𝒪ℰ. By the approximation definition, first approximating an entry and then clearing its denominator at the same precision shows that x also belongs to 𝒪ℰd. Moreover, for every j the vector τHT∗x is congruent to the integral period vector ΠjPj e(sj) modulo ℓj. Its first j Witt coordinates are therefore integral for every j. Every coordinate belongs to the tilt valuation ring, so τHT∗x lies in Ainfd.

To impose independence, start at precision one. Choose a level m where every starting column has a lift. The right inverse at this level has entries in 𝒪ℰ. There are finitely many entries, so the maximum of their denominator exponents gives one power vb and a rectangular matrix D over 𝔖 with eW(D) ≡ vbR′m modulo ℓ. Multiplying by Rm gives vbId modulo ℓ. Lift each of the d columns of D by the preceding result and put the resulting columns into a square matrix V. Then

V ≡ vbId (mod ℓ),  τHT∗V ∈ Matd(Ainf).

The residue of v is nonzero, so det V has nonzero residue. The unit factorization in 𝒪ℰ and the adjugate identity put V−1 in that ring. The columns form a basis over 𝒪ℰ, with integral period columns after multiplication by τHT∗. To see why a further lattice argument is needed, observe that v is a unit in 𝒪ℰ (its inverse has denominator u), whereas u is a nonunit in 𝔖. The integral basis theorem gives a basis of the full intersection Ainfd ∩ τHT∗𝒪ℰd over 𝔖. The coordinate-ideal argument then controls the right inverses uniformly.
