How can we read a trace from vectors that reconstruct the identity?

Suppose a vector space W has dimension a over a field k. Let γ ∶ k → S be a unital map into a commutative ring, and ψ ∶ W → Sa an additive map with ψ(cy) = γ(c)ψ(y). For each coordinate i take finitely many vectors xji. Suppose weights bj in S give ∑j bj ψ(xji) equal to the ith coordinate vector. Then for every k-linear endomorphism T of W, the same weights read its trace.

γ(tr T) = ∑i,j bj ψ(T xji)i.

Choose a basis of W. Put its images under ψ in the columns of a square matrix X. Form the ith column of C by taking the coordinates of each xji in this basis, applying γ to the entries, and summing with the weights bj. The hypothesis reads XC = I. Since det(X) det(C) = 1, det(X) is a unit. The adjugate formula then gives C = X−1. Applying T to each vector before taking the weighted sum gives the columns of Xγ([T])C, where [T] is its matrix in our chosen basis. For square matrices over S, tr(AB) = ∑i,j AijBji = tr(BA). Hence the transformed diagonal sums to tr(γ([T])CX) = γ(tr T).

For instance, take X with rows (1, 1) and (0, 1), and [T] with rows (2, 1) and (3, −1). In the new coordinates the rows are (5, −5) and (3, −4). The diagonal entries have both changed, while their sum is still 1. In the application, the reconstruction of I comes from a relation in the matrix lattice itself.

Which trace does the integral operator actually measure?

Fix σ and let a ≥ 1 be the dimension of the local representation. Let p be the trace of its local Weil action transported back to Ω by ι−1. Let e be the vector of d integral coordinates representing the a by a identity matrix, so that ∑k ekBk = Ia. Write tk = tr Bk. We will recover p from the d coordinates of Ve and these d ordinary matrix traces. Its matrix recovers every coordinate column of the representation. The two-by-two example below computes what this measures.

Choose the coefficient field. A crystalline representation in this construction comes with a model over a finite extension of ℚℓ. Adjoin all entries of the finitely many Bk to that extension. Each entry is algebraic, so the resulting field L is finite over ℚℓ. The column identities and the expression for Ia hold over L since its inclusion into Ω is injective. Crystalline representations are potentially semistable. The dimension calculation for a single coefficient embedding gives dimℂ W = a, the hypothesis needed in the coordinate trace formula. Brinon and Conrad, §9.3, on crystalline and semistable representations.

Why is the polynomial preserved? Let L′ extend L and choose a basis b₁, …, bh of L′ over L. A vector over L′ decomposes into h vectors over L by applying the coordinate projections πj entrywise. Every representation matrix has entries in L, so each πj commutes with its action. The same decomposition works for tensors with Bst. If a period is fixed after a finite extension, each of its projections is fixed there. Conversely, for a finite list of periods take the compositum of their fields of invariance. This proves that the unramified dimension of the potentially semistable periods and the rational dimension of the representation both multiply by h. Potential semistability is therefore preserved.

In the complex quotient every enlarged period class is the sum of the included classes of πj(m), with coefficients ι(bj). The inclusion on Weil spaces is thus surjective. It commutes with the Weil action because the coefficient inclusion commutes with both the Galois action and Frobenius. Both spaces have dimension a, so the map is an isomorphism. The two operators therefore have the same characteristic polynomial. For two models, apply this comparison to their common coefficient field, where their matrices agree. The coefficient of degree a − 1 in the original model is then −ι(p), the negative of the specified Weil trace.

Solve once for the weights. Since U is invertible over Bcris, applying the ring map φ₂ to its inverse gives an inverse for φ₂(U). Solve for w in φ₂(U)w = e. Then put v = φ₁(U)w. The operator equation implies Ve = s v. Let β(y) = 1 ⊗ c(y) in S = Ω ⊗F Bst. For each physical column i take xji = [φ₂(mji)] and use the weights β(wj). In coordinate q, distributivity and the formula for ψ give

∑j β(wj)ψ(xji)q = ∑k (Bk)qi ⊗ c(ek) = δqi.

In the last equality each ek belongs to ℤℓ and passes across the tensor sign. The entries sum to the (q, i) entry of the identity. After applying Tσ, the change of exponent replaces φ₂(U)w by v. Sum the resulting entries with q = i. For each k, the diagonal sum in the first tensor factor is tk. The coordinate trace formula now gives the first equality below. For any F-linear λ ∶ Ω → F, write Lλ for λ followed by the inclusion of F into Bcris.

∑k tk ⊗ c(vk) = p ⊗ 1,   ∑k (Ve)k Lλ(tk) = sLλ(p).

To obtain the second equality, apply λ ⊗ 1 to the first. It sends t ⊗ c(v) to λ(t)c(v). Both sides are constant polynomials in Bst, so their constant coefficients agree in Bcris. Multiply by s and use sv = Ve. The target and each auxiliary component satisfy the calculation with their own e and basis traces. Their common multiplier permits the subtraction in the finite-layer trace comparison.

A calculation with two different ranks. To see what the last formula measures, take the three-dimensional space of upper triangular two-by-two matrices over ℚ. Use its basis (E₁₁, E₁₂, E₂₂), where Eij has a single 1 in position (i, j). The identity has coordinate vector e = (1, 0, 1), and the three basis traces form the same list. On the physical plane let T have rows (2, 1) and (0, 3). Choose s = 2 and let V send a basis matrix B to 2TB. Then Ve represents 2T, whose coordinates are (4, 2, 6). Testing these against the basis traces gives 4 + 6 = 10. Division by 2 recovers tr T = 5. The diagonal of the three-dimensional operator V is (4, 4, 6), so taking its own trace would give 14. The period argument above constructs exactly the test that reads the trace on the physical plane.
