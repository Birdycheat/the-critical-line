How does strong base-change descent pass from cuspidal forms on general linear groups to definite unitary groups?

To construct the odd-rank Galois companion of a regular algebraic conjugate self-dual cuspidal automorphic representation π of GLn(𝔸E), we must first descend π from the general linear group to a definite unitary group U(n) over the CM field E so that the weight-one geometric realizations and Hecke approximation theorems apply. The trace-formula comparison initially delivers only a weak descent matching Satake parameters at almost all split places, and its base change back to GLn can a priori involve several cuspidal factors and nontrivial Speh segments. Two rigidity mechanisms collapse both ambiguities completely: the open width of the Jacquet–Shalika strip forces every Speh segment length to equal one, and split rigidity together with cuspidal multiplicity one forces a single factor equal to π itself.

Theorem (Weak unitary descent, Speh-length collapse, and strong base-change descent off nonsplit sets). Let E be a CM number field unramified over its maximal totally real subfield E+ at all finite places, let n ≥ 1, and let π be a regular algebraic conjugate self-dual cuspidal automorphic representation of GLn(𝔸E) that is unramified at every nonsplit finite place.

  1. Weak unitary descent. Combining the trace-formula run theorem with partial-norm unitarity and split rigidity rules out every nontrivial isobaric partition, producing a definite unitary automorphic representation σ of U(n) unramified at all nonsplit finite places whose local base change matches the Satake multiset of π at all but finitely many split places.
  2. Jacquet–Shalika collapse of Speh lengths. Let q > 1 and d ≥ 1 be integers and let z ∈ ℂ. If every shifted Speh parameter lies strictly inside the Jacquet–Shalika strip,
    q−1/2 < |z · q(d−1)/2 − k| < q1/2   for every 0 ≤ k < d,
    then d = 1. Applying this criterion to the Speh base change of σ collapses every Speh segment length di to 1.
  3. Strong base-change matching and descent off nonsplit sets. Once every di = 1, split rigidity and cuspidal multiplicity one force a single cuspidal factor (c = 1, a0 = n, P0 = π). Consequently, σ matches π strongly at every split finite place w and every archimedean embedding τ′:
    σw ≃ πw   (every split w),   χinf(σ, τ′) = χinf(π, τ′)   (every τ′).
    In particular, for every polarized regular algebraic cuspidal automorphic representation π, strong base-change descent holds off any finite nonsplit set T.
Proof

Step 1: Excluding isobaric partitions to obtain weak descent. Apply the trace-formula dichotomy to π over E. Either there exists a definite unitary automorphic representation σ unramified at all nonsplit finite places and matching the Satake multiset of π at all but finitely many split places, or there is a nontrivial isobaric multi-partition D matching π at almost all split places. In the second case, partial-norm unitarity ensures that every cuspidal constituent of D is unitary, and conjugate self-duality ensures that π itself is unitary. Split rigidity then contradicts the nontriviality of D, leaving only the weak unitary descent σ.

Step 2: Proof of Speh-length collapse. Suppose for contradiction that d ≥ 2 in the Jacquet–Shalika strip inequality. Both k = 0 and k = 1 belong to {0, …, d − 1}. Evaluating the upper bound at k = 0 gives |z| · q(d−1)/2 < q1/2, whereas evaluating the lower bound at k = 1 gives q−1/2 < |z| · q(d−1)/2 − 1 = (|z| · q(d−1)/2)/q. Multiplying the second inequality by q > 0 produces q1/2 = q−1/2 · q < |z| · q(d−1)/2 < q1/2, a contradiction. Hence d = 1.

Step 3: Upgrading weak descent to strong matching. Apply Speh base change to the weak unitary descent U = σ. This produces c conjugate self-dual cuspidal representations P1, …, Pc of positive ranks a1, …, ac and positive Speh lengths d1, …, dc with ∑i di ai = n, whose split local components are induced from Speh quotients. At all but finitely many split places w, uniqueness of split local components equates the Satake multiset Bw of π with the disjoint union of the shifted Satake multisets Si · qw(di−1)/2 − k (0 ≤ k < di). Because infinitely many split places exist outside any finite set, choose one such split place w of norm qw > 1; for any index i, pick z ∈ Si (nonempty since ai ≥ 1). Every shift z · qw(di−1)/2 − k belongs to Bw, so the local Jacquet–Shalika bound for unitary cuspidal π forces the strict strip inequality, and Step 2 gives di = 1 for every i.

Step 4: Collapsing to a single factor and descending off nonsplit sets. With every di = 1, the relation ∑i Si = Bw holds at all but finitely many split places, and each conjugate self-dual cuspidal Pi is unitary. Split rigidity yields an index j with aj = n and Pj isomorphic to π. If another index i ≠ j existed, we would have ai + aj ≤ ∑k ak = n = aj, contradicting ai ≥ 1. Thus c = 1, a0 = n, and cuspidal multiplicity one forces P0 = π. Single-factor equivalence and local component uniqueness then identify the local split components at every split place w, and the one-term archimedean sum equates infinitesimal characters at every embedding τ′, giving strong base-change matching. Finally, for any polarized regular algebraic cuspidal π, split places are automatically unramified in E/E+, nonsplit places are unramified by hypothesis, and the quadratic polarization character squares to one so π is conjugate self-dual; strong descent therefore passes directly to descent off any finite nonsplit set T.

Worked check (Speh strip width at norm five). Fix a split place of norm q = 5. The Jacquet–Shalika open interval (5−1/2, 51/2) has multiplicative width 51/2 / 5−1/2 = 5. For a Speh segment of length d = 2 and central parameter z, the shifts (d − 1)/2 − k for k = 0, 1 are +1/2 and −1/2, so the two moduli |z| · 51/2 and |z| · 5−1/2 have ratio exactly 5: if |z| · 51/2 < 51/2 then |z| < 1, forcing |z| · 5−1/2 < 5−1/2 outside the open strip. For d = 3, the shifts are +1, 0, −1 and the first two entries |z| · 5 and |z| still differ by a factor of 5 (with total ratio 25). Only d = 1 (ratio 1 < 5) can fit strictly inside (5−1/2, 51/2).

How do adic Frobenius sections, Galois-invariant crystalline periods, and quadratic CM patching close local Weil moments and assemble the odd-rank companion?

In the local Weil-polynomial limit, the weighted trace congruences must simultaneously control global traces and arbitrarily many power sums of the crystalline Frobenius on local period modules, and the resulting companions must then be transferred from definite unitary groups back to general linear cuspidal representations across quadratic CM extensions. The analytic engine behind the crystalline comparison is an ℓ-adic matrix iteration inside the integral crystalline period ring Acris: starting from a Breuil–Kisin Frobenius matrix and its constant-term specialization, iterating Pn+1 = α φ(Pn) β′ converges to an invertible Frobenius-intertwining section Q whose Galois defect vanishes identically, producing an invertible Galois-invariant period matrix in Bcris. Combined with uniform jump bounds on layer frames, strong descent from the preceding subsection, and patching across infinitely many quadratic CM extensions, this closes joint local Weil moments and completes the global odd-rank attachment.

Theorem (Adic Frobenius sections, crystalline period invariance, joint Weil closure, and odd-rank assembly). Fix a prime ℓ, a finite extension K/ℚℓ with Breuil–Kisin prism datum, and a CM field E.

  1. Power sums as traces of powers. For every Weil–Deligne representation x and local Weil element σ, the roots of the characteristic polynomial at σn are the n-th powers of the roots at σ; because the monic characteristic polynomial splits over ℚ̄ℓ, the n-th eigenvalue power sum at σ equals the ordinary trace at σn.
  2. Adic Frobenius sections over Acris. Let R be an ℓ-adically complete ring in which ℓ is a nonzerodivisor, equipped with an endomorphism φ fixing ℓ and an element u ∈ R with φ(u) = uq (q > 0). Suppose matrices α, α′, β, β′, Γ ∈ Md(R) satisfy α α′ = β β′ = ℓ2N Id, ℓ(α − β) = uq Γ, and ℓ(2N+1)(n+1)+1 | uqn+1−1 for all n ≥ n0. Then the rescaled limits of Pn+1 = α φ(Pn) β′ and P′n+1 = β φ(P′n) α′ yield matrices Q, Q′ ∈ Md(R) with k = n0 + 2N n0 satisfying
    Q Q′ = ℓ2k Id,   Q ≡ Q′ ≡ ℓk Id (mod u),   Q β = α φ(Q).
    Applying this in R = Acris with u = ψ(X) and φ(u) = uℓ produces the Frobenius section of every Breuil–Kisin frame.
  3. Galois invariance of the section and invertible Bcris period matrix. For every crystalline Breuil–Kisin frame F with Frobenius section (Q, Q′), the Galois defect D = Q′ ε′ σ(Q) − ℓ2k Id is entrywise divisible by u and satisfies the twisted Frobenius equation, hence D = 0 and ε′ σ(Q) = Q. Consequently, mapping the integral frame matrix Y (with Y Y′ = τ2N Id) into Bcris via f = aB ∘ φA produces an invertible Galois-invariant period matrix
    U = f(τ)−N f(Y) Q ∈ GLd(Bcris),   ρ(g) σB(U) = U   (every g ∈ GK).
  4. Uniform layer frames, joint local Weil closure, and global odd-rank assembly. Bounding the absolute values of all prescribed Hodge–Tate jumps by a single finite integer N builds crystalline Breuil–Kisin frames on every approximating matrix-algebra layer and transfers them to a linked limit frame, feeding local model transport into single-trace closure and yielding joint local Weil moment closure. Specializing the four-property trace limit to odd rank, transferring all four local comparisons (unramified, de Rham, crystalline, and Weil–Deligne polynomial) along strong descent from the preceding subsection to GLm cuspidal forms, and patching across an infinite family of quadratic CM extensions assembles the complete global odd-rank Galois attachment.
Proof

Step 1: Traces of powers and adic Frobenius iteration. For any Weil–Deligne representation x and element σ, raising σ to the n-th power replaces each root of its characteristic polynomial by its n-th power. Over ℚ̄ℓ the monic characteristic polynomial splits completely, so its next-to-leading coefficient is the negative sum of its roots, equating the n-th eigenvalue power sum at σ with the trace at σn. Now let R, φ, u, α, α′, β, β′, Γ satisfy the adic iteration hypotheses. Taking determinants in β β′ = ℓ2N Id gives det(β) det(β′) = ℓ2N d, so det(β) is a nonzerodivisor; multiplying β′ β by det(β) = adj(β) β and cancelling det(β) yields β′ β = ℓ2N Id. Define P0 = P′0 = Id, Pn+1 = α φ(Pn) β′, and P′n+1 = β φ(P′n) α′. Induction on n using φ(ℓ) = ℓ and φ(u) = uq yields ℓ(Pn+1 − ℓ2N Pn) = uqn+1 Mn for explicit matrices Mn, and symmetrically for P′n. Telescoping gives ℓn(Pn − ℓ2N n Id) ∈ u Md(R) for all n. For n ≥ n0, factoring out u from uqn+1 and cancelling the nonzerodivisor ℓ from the divisibility hypothesis gives Pn+1 − ℓ2N Pn = ℓ(2N+1)(n+1) u Cn. Writing Pn0+m = ℓ2N m Sm makes the sequence Sm = Pn0 + u ∑j<m ℓj ℓn0+2N n0+1 Cn0+j an ℓ-adic Cauchy series converging in Md(R) to S (and symmetrically S′ from P′n). Induction shows Pn P′n = ℓ4N n Id and Sm+1 β = α φ(Sm); Hausdorffness of ℓ-adic completion passes these to S S′ = ℓ4N n0 Id and S β = α φ(S). Setting Q = ℓn0 S and Q′ = ℓn0 S′ establishes all three Frobenius section equations.

Step 2: Instantiation in the crystalline period ring, defect vanishing, and period invariance. Inside Acris, let ψ : S → Acris be the Breuil–Kisin evaluation and u = ψ(X), so φ(u) = uℓ. Every power series s differs from its constant term by a multiple of X, so φ(ψ(s)) − φ(ψ(s(0))) is divisible by uℓ. Since φ(ψ(E)) = ℓ v and φ(ψ(E(0))) = ℓ w0 for units v, w0 ∈ Acris×, rescaling the frame matrices by v−N and w0−N supplies α, α′, β, β′, Γ satisfying Step 1 and constructs the Frobenius section (Q, Q′) satisfying φ(E)N(Q A0) = φ(E(0))N(A φ(Q)). Now let F be a crystalline frame with cocycle εF(g) and set D = Q′ ε′ σ(Q) − ℓ2k Id, where ε′ = φ(εF(g)). Applying the twisted cocycle defect equation to the Frobenius–Galois relation of the next subsection yields ℓ2N D = A0 φ(D) (w0−2N A′0). Modulo the ideal generated by u, we have Q ≡ Q′ ≡ σ(Q) ≡ ℓk Id (since u | σ(u)) and ε′ ≡ Id (by the crystalline congruence on εF(g) − Id and X | φ(X)). Hence u divides every entry of D, so the Acris vanishing lemma forces D = 0. Left-multiplying by Q and cancelling the nonzerodivisor ℓ2k proves ε′ σ(Q) = Q. Finally, mapping into Bcris via f = aB ∘ φA sends the period element τ = η([ε] − 1) to a unit f(τ) ∈ Bcris×. Setting U = f(τ)−N f(Y) Q gives an invertible matrix (with explicit inverse f(τ)−N ℓ−2k Q′ f(Y′)), and combining the Ainf Galois equation for Y with ε′ σ(Q) = Q proves exact Galois invariance of U.

Step 3: Uniform layer frames, joint closure, and global odd-rank assembly. Taking N = supτ′ suph ∈ J(τ′) |h| bounds every jump uniformly across all embeddings and all approximating matrix-algebra layers. Semistability of each layer produces a de Rham Breuil–Kisin frame Fm of uniform height N, the limit-tower theorem links them to a limit frame F0, and crystallinity of all layers propagates to every Fm and F0. Inside single-trace closure, transporting global representations to local models over finite extensions L/K and instantiating a Breuil–Kisin datum and period element from the next subsection feeds these crystalline frames into the signed Weil-operator comparison, establishing joint local Weil moment closure. Specializing the four-property trace limit to odd rank then yields the odd-rank unitary limit; composing with strong base-change descent from the preceding subsection transfers all four local spherical properties (unramifiedness, de Rham jumps, crystallinity, and Weil–Deligne polynomials) back to GLm cuspidal representations. Finally, for any infinite family of quadratic CM extensions serving every target place w, patching produces a single global companion ρ over E agreeing on all but finitely many extensions in the family; for each target place w, selecting a serving quadratic CM extension outside that finite exceptional set identifies the restricted companion via degree-one Frobenius density and descends all four local spherical properties to E, completing the odd-rank attachment.

Worked checks (power traces and one-dimensional adic Frobenius iteration). First, for a two-dimensional Weil–Deligne matrix with eigenvalues 2 and 3, the characteristic polynomial at σ is T2 − 5T + 6 (trace 5), while σ3 has eigenvalues 8 and 27 with characteristic polynomial T2 − 35T + 216 and trace 8 + 27 = 35, matching 23 + 33. Second, illustrate the adic Frobenius iteration in dimension d = 1 with N = 0, q = 2, ℓ = 3, β = β′ = 1, α = 1 + u2, Γ = 3, and φ(u) = u2 (so 3(α − β) = u2 · 3). Each step satisfies Pn+1 − Pn = Pn u2n+1 and Pn ≡ 1 (mod u); in the limit S = ∏j=1∞ (1 + u2j), applying φ shifts indices by one to give α φ(S) = (1 + u2) ∏j=2∞ (1 + u2j) = S = S β with S ≡ 1 (mod u).

How are integral spanning matrix lattices, Breuil–Kisin prism data, integral period equations, and Hodge–Tate power-sum expansions constructed?

The fixed-weight period descent and joint local Weil closure rely on four concrete local-algebraic foundations: realizing continuous compact-group representations inside finite ℓ-adic extensions with Galois-stable spanning matrix lattices, constructing Breuil–Kisin Eisenstein prism data and concrete period-ring cores over every finite extension K/ℚℓ, deriving the three Ainf Frobenius–Galois equations and de Rham descent from a Breuil–Kisin frame, and diagonalizing the Hodge–Tate operator in an adapted Hodge basis to expand its powers linearly in lattice coordinates. Each ingredient follows directly from elementary ring-theoretic, Eisenstein, and linear-algebraic mechanisms.

Theorem (Spanning lattices, Breuil–Kisin data, integral frame equations, and Hodge–Tate operator expansion). Fix a prime ℓ, a compact topological group Γ, and a finite extension K/ℚℓ.

  1. Finite-extension entries, left-stable spanning lattices, and precision-carrying tuples. Every continuous representation r : Γ → GLa(ℚ̄ℓ) has all matrix entries inside a single finite extension L/ℚℓ and admits a finite set t ⊆ Ma(L) spanning Ma(L) over ℚℓ whose ℤℓ-span contains Ia and is preserved by left multiplication by every r(g). On finite product tuples of auxiliary representations, the lattice spanned by group tuples and ℓM-scaled basis matrices carries linear relations to r modulo ℓN with a uniform trace-norm bound ‖tr(∑i wi bi)‖ ≤ ℓK for all wi ∈ ℤℓ.
  2. Breuil–Kisin prism data and period-ring core fitting. Every finite extension K/ℚℓ admits an Eisenstein Breuil–Kisin prism datum over Witt vectors W(kK) of its residue field whose evaluation sends the Eisenstein polynomial E(X) to a generator of ker θ in Ainf. Moreover, every abstract local period-ring triple fits a concrete period-ring core matching algebraic sections into BdR+, Teichmüller roots of unity, the unique Fontaine logarithm t = log([ε]), Frobenius on the maximal unramified extension, and the cyclotomic character.
  3. Integral period equations and de Rham descent for Breuil–Kisin frames. For any Breuil–Kisin frame F with Frobenius matrix A and integral period matrix Y ∈ Md(Ainf) whose image in W(ℂℓ♭) equals τN T and Y Y′ = τ2N Id, writing A0 and E0 for the evaluations of A and E in Ainf, the triple satisfies
    φ(Y) = Y A0,   τN ρ(g) σ(Y) = σ(τ)N Y ε(g),   E0N ε(g) σ(A0) = σ(E0)N A0 φ(ε(g))
    for every g ∈ GK. Whenever F is de Rham, its bounded-pole preframe supplies an invertible Galois-invariant period matrix over BdR, proving that the rationalized representation of ρ is de Rham.
  4. Adapted Hodge bases, diagonal characterization, and power-sum expansion. Every de Rham representation R over L admits at each embedding τ : K ↪ L an adapted Hodge basis (Q, V, p, q) over a finite extension K′/K whose jump exponents pi − q match the de Rham graded multiplicities multV(R, τ, n). A matrix u ∈ Ma(ℂℓ) acts as the Hodge–Tate operator for scalar z ∈ ℂℓ if and only if it diagonalizes in the reduced Hodge basis θD(V):
    u · θD(V) = θD(V) · diag(zpi−q).
    Finally, whenever an integral lattice (ρ, b, e) reads r (meaning Ia = ∑i ei bi and r(g) bi = ∑s ρ(g)s,i bs) and U ∈ Md(ℂℓ), u ∈ Ma(ℂℓ) are the Hodge–Tate operators for ρ and σ(r), every integer power n ≥ 0 satisfies
    un = ∑i=1d (Un e)i · σ(bi).
Proof

Step 1: Compact-image finite field, stable spanning lattices, and uniform trace bounds. Because Γ is compact and r is continuous, the image subgroup H = r(Γ) is a compact subgroup of GLa(ℚ̄ℓ), so all matrix entries of H lie in a single finite extension L/ℚℓ. Choose a finite subset t0 ⊆ Γ whose images generate the ℤℓ-span of r(Γ), put t1 = t0 ∪ {1}, and let B consist of Ia together with a ℚℓ-basis of Ma(L). The finite product set t = {r(h) b | h ∈ t1, b ∈ B} lies in Ma(L), contains B (hence spans Ma(L) over ℚℓ and contains Ia in its ℤℓ-span), and satisfies r(g)(r(h) b) = r(g h) b = ∑k ∈ t0 ck r(k) b; grouping terms fiberwise by y = r(k) b ∈ t proves left r(Γ)-stability over ℤℓ. For any finite basis b1, …, bd, choosing K with ∑i ‖tr(bi)‖ ≤ ℓK and using ‖wi‖ ≤ 1 for wi ∈ ℤℓ yields the uniform trace-norm bound ‖tr(∑i wi bi)‖ ≤ ℓK.

Step 2: Breuil–Kisin datum construction and concrete period-ring core fitting. For a finite extension K/ℚℓ, 𝒪K is a discrete valuation ring finite over ℤℓ containing the Witt vectors W(kK) of its residue field (injectivity of W(kK) → 𝒪K holds because any nonzero Witt vector factors as ℓn times a unit, neither of which vanishes in a characteristic-zero domain). Picking a uniformizer π ∈ 𝒪K with Eisenstein minimal polynomial E(X) ∈ W(kK)[X] and lifting π via a compatible ℓ-power root sequence defines a Frobenius-equivariant evaluation W(kK)⟦X⟧ → Ainf sending X to [π♭] and E(X) to a distinguished element generating ker θ, assembling the Breuil–Kisin datum. To fit an abstract period-ring triple to a concrete core, lift the algebraic-closure embedding into BdR+ using injectivity of BdR+ → BdR, restrict the crystalline inclusion to Acris → BdR+, identify the tilted Teichmüller roots via perfection uniqueness, verify uniqueness of the Fontaine logarithm t = log([ε]), match Frobenius on the maximal unramified extension by checking prime-to-ℓ roots of unity (where Witt Frobenius raises Teichmüller lifts to the ℓ-th power), and identify the cyclotomic characters χQ(g) and χ(g) by showing |χQ(g) − χ(g)|ℓ ≤ ℓ−(m+1) for every m ≥ 0 via primitive ℓm+1-th roots of unity.

Step 3: Integral period equations and de Rham descent. For any ring R with commuting endomorphisms fr, ga, invertible matrix T ∈ GLd(R), and equations eN fr(T) = T A, Z ga(T) = T ε with fr(Z) = Z, computing T · eN ε ga(A) = eN Z ga(T) ga(A) = eN ga(e)N Z ga(fr(T)) = ga(e)N (eN fr(T)) fr(ε) = T · ga(e)N A fr(ε) and left-cancelling the unit T gives eN ε ga(A) = ga(e)N A fr(ε). Applying this to the frame equations in W(ℂℓ♭) and invoking injectivity of Ainf ↪ W(ℂℓ♭) (together with Hausdorffness of ℤℓ-homomorphisms into ℓ-adically separated rings) proves all three equations in the integral frame display. When F is de Rham, the bounded-pole preframe produces an invertible Galois-invariant period matrix Q over BdR, transporting directly to the de Rham condition on the rationalized representation of ρ.

Step 4: Adapted Hodge bases, diagonal characterization, and power-sum expansion. Given a de Rham representation R over L, an embedding τ : K ↪ L, and σ ∈ Gℚℓ satisfying σ ∘ τ = idK, pick an adapted basis d1, …, dm of DdR(R) at τ, choose a finite extension K′/K fixing σ(L), and project each di to wi = ΦS(di) ∈ BdRa via the coordinatewise σ-evaluation map ΦS induced by φS : L ⊗ℚℓ BdR → BdR. Multiplying by a normalized element e ∈ L ⊗ℚℓ BdR with φS(e) = 1 killing ker ΦS establishes BdR-linear independence of w1, …, wm, and applying ΦS to the adapted spanning expansion establishes BdR-spanning, forcing m = a and yielding an adapted Hodge basis (Q, V, p, q) over K′ whose jump counts pi − q equal the de Rham multiplicities multV(R, τ, n). Next, observe in any integral domain with ring map θ sending a nonzero element t to 0 that tn w = tm v with n ≠ m and (θ(v) = 0 ⟹ v = 0) forces θ(w) = 0 (by cancelling tmin(n,m)). Writing any test vector x = Q · av in the adapted Hodge basis makes each coordinate av,k invariant under the compositum extension K′ · K″, hence in the image of BdR+-sections; comparing ξ-powers in BdR+ applies the domain cancellation lemma to kill θD((W y)k) whenever pk − q ≠ p − q, proving the diagonal equivalence (the converse evaluates the Hodge–Tate test directly on the columns of Q and V). Finally, when an integral lattice (ρ, b, e) reads r, let K′ be the finite extension adjoining all entries of σ(bi) and form the matrices Bk with (m, i)-entry σ(bi,m,k). Testing the Hodge–Tate property of u on the period vectors xm = ∑i o(σ(bi,m,k)) Qi,j and using equivariance of (ρ, b) gives u Bk θD(V) = Bk U θD(V). Cancelling the invertible matrix θD(V) gives u Bk = Bk U for every k, which translates to u(∑i xi σ(bi)) = ∑i (U x)i σ(bi). Since ∑i ei σ(bi) = Ia, induction on n ≥ 0 proves the exact power-sum expansion.

Worked check (Hodge–Tate diagonalization and power-sum expansion in rank two). Take a = 2, d = 2 with diagonal basis matrices b1 = diag(1, 0), b2 = diag(0, 1) and identity coordinates e = (1, 1), so e1 b1 + e2 b2 = I2. Choose Hodge–Tate jumps p1 − q = 0 and p2 − q = 3, infinite-order scalar z = 2, reduced Hodge basis θD(V) = I2, and operators u = U = diag(20, 23) = diag(1, 8). Clearly u · I2 = I2 · diag(1, 8). At n = 2, the lattice coordinate vector is U2 e = (1, 64), and the power-sum expansion recovers 1 · diag(1, 0) + 64 · diag(0, 1) = diag(1, 64) = u2; taking matrix traces gives tr(u) = 1 + 8 = 9 and tr(u2) = 1 + 64 = 65, uniquely identifying the jump multiset {0, 3}.
