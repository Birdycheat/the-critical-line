Why does a reducible characteristic polynomial put the centralizer in two blocks?

In the comparison of marked pairs, a pair with reducible polynomial must contribute zero for the chosen Hecke test. The local argument starts over any field k. Let γ be an invertible operator on a finite-dimensional, nonzero k-vector space W. Suppose its characteristic polynomial χ is separable and reducible over k. Factor it as χ = p q with both factors monic of positive degree. Separability makes p and q coprime. We shall find a proper subspace and a complement that every operator commuting with γ preserves.

Set W1 = ker p(γ) and W2 = ker q(γ). By the Euclidean algorithm choose polynomials r, s such that r q + s p = 1. Evaluation at γ gives two projections.

E1 = r(γ)q(γ),   E2 = s(γ)p(γ),   E1 + E2 = I.

Indeed p(γ)q(γ) = 0 by Cayley–Hamilton. Thus E1 has image in W1, acts as the identity there by the displayed sum, and kills W2. Interchanging the factors proves the corresponding assertions for E2. Each vector is the sum of its two projections and their images have zero intersection. Both images are nonzero. Over a splitting field choose a root λ of p. It is an eigenvalue of γ because det(λI − γ) = 0. Its eigenvector is killed by p(γ), so det p(γ) vanishes in the splitting field and hence already in k. This proves W1 ≠ 0 over k. A root of q gives W2 ≠ 0 in the same way. Cayley–Hamilton theorem, Stacks Project, Lemma 10.16.1.

Write T for the centralizer of γ in GL(W). Each element of T commutes with the projections, as does its inverse, so it preserves both images. Choose a basis of W1 followed by a basis of W2 and let c have these basis vectors as its columns in the original coordinates. With a = dim W1 and b = dim W2, we have

c−1Tc = Cent(c−1γc) ⊆ GLa(k) × GLb(k) ⊆ P.

The middle group acts by block diagonal matrices. The parabolic subgroup P stabilizes the first summand and consists of block upper-triangular matrices. It is proper since both blocks have positive dimension. The element c−1γc is itself block diagonal. Later in the orbital sum it is conjugated again by elements of P. The resulting upper-right block can be nonzero, which is why the next calculation allows it.

How does conjugation turn into a rectangular change of variables?

In two blocks of positive sizes a and b, write V = Mata×b(k). Let γ be any element of the block upper-triangular subgroup with separable characteristic polynomial. Write A, B for its diagonal blocks and C for its upper-right block. The first block acts on the preserved subspace and the second on its quotient. Both are invertible because det γ = det A det B. For X ∈ V, let u(X) have identity diagonal, upper-right block X and lower-left block zero. These matrices form the unipotent radical N of the subgroup, with u(X)u(Z) = u(X + Z).

Conjugation by u(X) leaves the diagonal blocks unchanged and gives upper-right block C + AX − XB. Right multiplication by u(Y) gives the same diagonal blocks and upper-right block C + AY. Equating these expressions determines the map Φ.

Φ(X) = X − A−1XB,
u(X)−1 γ u(X) = γ u(Φ(X)).

Sylvester’s argument. We claim that every Y ∈ V occurs exactly once. If Φ(X) = 0, then AX = XB. By induction AjX = XBj, so h(A)X = Xh(B) for every polynomial h. Taking h = χA gives XχA(B) = 0 by Cayley–Hamilton. The two characteristic polynomials are coprime because their product is the separable characteristic polynomial of γ. A Bézout identity for these factors evaluated at B gives an inverse to χA(B), since χB(B) = 0. Now X = 0. The linear map Φ has zero kernel on the finite-dimensional space V, hence is bijective. This proves the identity in both directions, including when C ≠ 0. Cayley–Hamilton theorem, Stacks Project, Lemma 10.16.1.

Now take k to be a nonarchimedean local field. The cuspidality assumption on the locally constant, compactly supported test f requires the following integral to vanish for every proper two-block decomposition, every g, h in the general linear group and every additive Haar measure μ on the corresponding V.

∫V f(g h−1u(Y)h) dμ(Y) = 0.

For g = γ and h = 1 the argument of f runs through γN. The bijection Φ identifies this translate with the orbit of γ under conjugation by N. It also explains why the hypothesis is imposed for all g and h. Replacing f(x) by f(dxd−1) replaces these two parameters by dgd−1 and hd−1. The same vanishing then applies after the changes of basis made in the orbital sum.

What does one coset contribute to the constant-term integral?

Keep the local field, the test f, the element γ and the isomorphism Φ above. Suppose f is invariant under multiplication on both sides by a compact open level subgroup K. Put Λ = {X ∈ V ∣ u(X) ∈ K}. The map u is a homeomorphism from V to N, with inverse given by the upper-right block. Its image is closed in the general linear group, being specified by fixed values for the other three blocks. Consequently Λ, the preimage of N ∩ K, is a compact open additive subgroup.

Let H = N ∩ K. In the group quotient by H, the cosets represented in N are exactly u(X)H. Two such cosets agree if and only if u(X)−1u(X′) = u(X′ − X) belongs to H, or equivalently X′ − X ∈ Λ. Since Φ is a linear isomorphism, it gives the second bijection below.

N/H ≃ V/Λ ≃ V/Φ(Λ),
u(X)H ↔ X + Λ ↔ Φ(X) + Φ(Λ).

For Z ∈ Λ, replacing X by X + Z conjugates u(X)−1γu(X) by u(Z) ∈ K. Bi-invariance leaves the value of f unchanged. Thus f(γu(Y)) is constant on each cell Φ(X) + Φ(Λ). Indeed, every point of the cell is Φ(X + Z) for some Z ∈ Λ. There are only finitely many cells on which this value is nonzero. The map Y ↦ γu(Y) identifies V with the closed translate γN. The intersection of this translate with the compact support of f pulls back to a compact subset of V. Its image in the discrete space V/Φ(Λ) is finite.

Each cell has the same Haar measure μ(Φ(Λ)). This measure is finite by compactness and positive because the cell is a nonempty open set. Integrating each constant value over its cell yields

∫V f(γu(Y)) dμ(Y)
= μ(Φ(Λ)) ∑[X]∈V/Λ f(u(X)−1γu(X)).

With the normalized absolute value on k, the linear change-of-variables formula gives μ(Φ(Λ)) = |det Φ|k μ(Λ). Thus the displayed integral vanishes exactly when the finite sum vanishes. The cuspidality hypothesis supplies this vanishing on every required fibre. It allows individual summands to be nonzero. François Loeser, Arizona Winter School Lecture Notes on p-adic and motivic integration, equation (1.1.1).

In the calculation over ℚ₅, the test is −4 on K = GL₂(ℤ₅), +1 on K diag(1/5,5) K and zero elsewhere. The diagonal blocks of γ = diag(1,6) give Φ(X) = −5X. Here Λ = ℤ₅, so each cell has mass 1/5 when μ(ℤ₅) = 1. Five terms of value −4 and twenty of value +1 give integral (−20 + 20)/5 = 0. The same test at the translate diag(5,1/5) has integral 25. This last value records exactly which hypothesis would fail if one checked only the first fibre.
