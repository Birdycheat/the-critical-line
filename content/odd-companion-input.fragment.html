How does an odd-rank Hecke system give a Galois representation?

Let Ω be an algebraic closure of the CM field E and put Γ = Gal(Ω/E). Fix an odd rank m, an automorphic representation U with its weights, and a coefficient identification ι from an algebraic closure of ℚℓ to ℂ. Choose two split places u and v of E above ℓ whose restrictions to the maximal totally real subfield E⁺ are distinct. Keep this pair fixed as the finite sets of test places vary. The construction gives one representation ru,v for all those sets. The three-place argument will identify the companions from different pairs.

The simultaneous polynomial congruences supply one weighted list for all polynomial tests. The rank extraction from cohomology and the local tests give the geometric representations used here. At a spherical split place w away from ℓ, let Bw be the Satake multiset and multiply each entry by (Nw)(m−1)/2. For an arithmetic Frobenius lift σ and a positive integer b, let xj = σ−b. The spherical Hecke operator indexed by these choices measures the sum of the bth powers of the normalized entries. Transport this number back by ι and denote it by θj. We want these numbers to be traces of one representation.

Two arithmetic inputs enter here. The first constructs representations ρi for auxiliary automorphic systems with prescribed local types and suitably separated weights, using geometric Galois representations. For each auxiliary system i the geometric input gives an open dense subset Vi of Γ and finitely many exceptional places. At an eligible spherical place outside the exceptions, the auxiliary representation is unramified. For each arithmetic Frobenius lift σ there that belongs to Vi, the polynomial at σ−1 agrees with the auxiliary Satake polynomial. The input also gives stronger matches at prescribed test places. The second is a congruence theorem for the Hecke eigenvalues ηi of these systems. With any fixed finite set of test places prescribed, it gives this approximation for each ε > 0. There exist finitely many indices F and coefficients ci such that the same bound holds for every polynomial P with integer coefficients in the Hecke coordinates.

|P(θ) − ∑i ∈ F ci P(ηi)|ℓ ≤ ε.

The theorem also places all θj in one finite extension L of ℚℓ because the chosen operators have rational coefficients. To construct a trace at g ∈ Γ, approach g by indices j at which any prescribed finite list of the auxiliary systems agrees with its Galois traces. Such indices exist in every neighborhood. For a neighborhood H of g, take a nonempty open subset of H−1 and intersect it with the finitely many Vi. The intersection is still nonempty and open. Exclude all exceptional places, the primes over ℓ, and the finite fibres above real-subfield primes excluded by the auxiliary level. The degree-one Frobenius argument still supplies an arithmetic Frobenius σ in the intersection. Taking exponent one gives a test xj = σ−1 in H at which all the selected systems match. Taking P to be the single coordinate j puts θj within ε of the continuous sum ∑ ci tr ρi(xj). Shrink the neighborhood until the sum varies by at most ε. Any two such θj then differ by at most ε in the ultrametric norm. Completeness of L gives a limit T(g). The approximation by the same continuous sums is uniform in g, hence T is continuous.

Any integral polynomial in finitely many values T(g₁), …, T(gk) obeys the same estimate. Fix the approximating list F for ε. For each gh, choose indices subject to two conditions. Their group coordinates must belong to a chosen neighborhood of gh, and they must match every system in a chosen finite list containing F. As the neighborhoods shrink and the lists enlarge, the group coordinates converge to gh and the numbers θj converge to T(gh) by the construction of the limit. Make these choices independently for the finitely many arguments of P. Every chosen coordinate matches every system in F, so the original polynomial estimate holds. Polynomial evaluation and the auxiliary traces are continuous, and the ball of radius ε is closed. The estimate therefore holds at the limiting tuple. In particular, a polynomial identity satisfied by every rank-m matrix trace holds for T, since its absolute value is at most every ε > 0. This gives T(1) = m, T(gh) = T(hg) and the alternating trace identity in m+1 variables. These conditions define a rank-m pseudocharacter. The last identity is the alternating sum over permutations, with one trace of an ordered product for each cycle. For matrices it is zero because the antisymmetrizer on the (m+1)st tensor power of an m-dimensional space is zero.

Realizing the trace. Extend T linearly to the group algebra over the algebraic closure of ℚℓ. In each term of the alternating trace identity, every argument appears exactly once. The expression in the extended trace is therefore multilinear, and its vanishing on all tuples of group basis elements implies its vanishing on the whole algebra. Divide by the ideal of elements A with T(AB) = 0 for every B. The pseudocharacter realization theorem makes this quotient a finite product of matrix algebras. On each block a central linear form is a scalar multiple of trace, as one sees on matrix units. If the scalar is c, substituting the same rank-one idempotent into all m+1 slots of the alternating identity gives c(c−1)⋯(c−m) = 0. Thus c is a nonnegative integer. If it were zero, every matrix A supported in that block would satisfy T(AB) = 0 for every B, since AB stays in the block. The trace pairing has zero radical on the quotient by construction. Every surviving block consequently has positive multiplicity. Take c copies of the defining module of that block. The total dimension is T(1) = m and the resulting semisimple representation r has trace T. For continuity, choose group matrices r(hk) spanning their image algebra. The trace pairing on it is nondegenerate because its restriction to each matrix block is the ordinary trace pairing times its positive multiplicity. Hence every entry of r(g) is a fixed linear combination of the continuous functions T(g hk). Rouquier, Caractérisation des caractères et pseudo-caractères, Lemma 4.1 and §4.

A rank-three congruence. For s ≥ 1, the representations of the additive group ℤ₅ given by diag((1+5s)g,1,1) have traces congruent to 3 modulo 5s. Integer powers extend continuously to ℤ₅, since taking fifth powers increases the divisibility of the difference from 1 by at least one power of 5. An integral polynomial in any finite list of these traces is consequently congruent to its value on the constant list of threes. Here one system with coefficient 1 gives accuracy 5−s simultaneously for every polynomial, and the limiting companion is the three-dimensional trivial representation.

How does one recover unramifiedness at a specified place?

Keep the chosen pair and its representation r = ru,v and fix w ∈ Qd away from ℓ where U is spherical. In the construction choose the finite test set {w}. The congruence and geometric inputs then supply a family whose every member is unramified at this w and matches its own Satake parameters there. The trace of the same r is uniformly approximated by finite linear combinations of their traces. Indeed the construction of T passed the estimate for any approximating subfamily to Γ, with the original coefficients. We may now use the subfamily attached to {w}.

Choose s in inertia at a place of Ω above w. Every auxiliary ρi in this subfamily sends s to the identity. Its traces at sg and g therefore agree for every g. Letting the uniform trace error tend to zero gives

tr((r(s) − 1)r(g)) = 0   for every g ∈ Γ.

The tested matrices span the image algebra, and r(s) − 1 belongs to it. Nondegeneracy of the trace pairing gives r(s) = 1. The quantifier over g matters. For instance, in the standard two-dimensional representation of SL₂(ℤℓ) plus a trivial line, let s have diagonal entries 1 and upper off-diagonal entry 1 in its two-dimensional block. Let g be its transpose. Both have trace 3 in dimension three, whereas sg has trace 4. Testing the translate g detects the nonidentity s.

The same subfamily gives the power traces at σ−b for every b ≥ 1 and every Frobenius lift σ at w. Here each approximant matches its own Hecke coordinate at the chosen index, so its two weighted sums coincide. Sending ε to zero in the estimates for θj and for T(xj) gives equality of the two limits. The first m power sums pb recover the characteristic polynomial. Indeed for roots αj put H(z) = ∏(1−αjz). Expanding −zH′/H as ∑b≥1 pbzb and comparing coefficients after multiplication by H determines each coefficient from those preceding it. Division by 1,…,m is valid in characteristic zero. Thus r has exactly the normalized Satake polynomial at w.

For example, a rank-three local multiset {1,1,−1} at norm 5 becomes {5,5,−5} after normalization. Its first three power sums are 5, 75 and 125. The elementary symmetric functions are 5, (5²−75)/2 = −25 and (125−5 · 75−25 · 5)/3 = −125. They give (X−5)²(X+5), retaining the repeated root.

How do three places above ℓ give one companion with the full local data?

The odd-rank approximation first chooses two auxiliary places above ℓ, called u and v. They must lie above different primes of E⁺. For the representation ru,v thus obtained, the unramified matches hold at all the allowed spherical places away from ℓ. At a spherical w above ℓ the local theorem also gives crystallinity, the prescribed filtration jumps at each embedding inducing w, and the Weil–Deligne polynomial, provided the prime under w in E⁺ differs from those under u and v.

Why do the filtration jumps survive a weighted approximation of traces?

Fix a spherical place w above ℓ away from the two auxiliary primes. Write K = Ew for the local field. At each embedding τ of K into the algebraic closure of ℚℓ, every member of the approximating family for {w} has the same multiset of filtration jumps Jτ. A jump h occurs once for each dimension of the graded space Filh/Filh+1 of the de Rham periods at τ. The assertion to prove is that the local restriction of the previously constructed r is crystalline and has precisely those multiplicities. The given convergence concerns linear combinations of global traces. The coefficients can vary with the accuracy, and the representations in a combination can act on different spaces.

From traces to periods. First form the ℤℓ-lattice spanned by the global matrices r(g). Left multiplication by r(g) preserves this lattice, which contains the identity matrix. The lattice comparison theorem uses global semisimplicity and the uniform trace estimates to approximate this action modulo ℓq for each q. Its approximating lattice lies in a finite product of matrix spaces for the auxiliary representations. The lists of representations can be made nested by adjoining the earlier lists and giving the added terms coefficient zero in the current sum. Restrict the resulting actions and comparison maps to the local group at w. On each rationalized approximating lattice, the group acts on the matrix columns by the given auxiliary representations. Thus these columns carry crystalline representations. Taking the maximum of |h| over all Jτ gives a single bound on all their jumps, through every level of approximation.

The bounded-weight Breuil–Kisin comparison for these lattices is the period-theoretic input. It gives integral matrices describing the periods at each level and a frame on the limiting lattice. For every precision a sufficiently deep level agrees with that frame to the required precision under the comparison maps. If the frames on all the levels satisfy the crystalline condition, so does the limiting frame. Its invariant periods span the limiting lattice after extending scalars to the crystalline period ring. To see why this yields periods for r itself, work over a finite coefficient field of definition and view its vector space over ℚℓ. For each vector v, evaluation A ↦ Av on the rational matrix lattice is equivariant and takes the identity to v. After extending scalars to the period ring, this map sends invariant periods to invariant periods. Its image contains 1 ⊗ v, so these invariant periods span the whole target as v varies. This is the period criterion for crystallinity. Brinon and Conrad, Theorem 5.2.1 and Remark 5.2.3 on admissibility over a period ring.

Recovering each jump. Fix τ. Choose a local Galois element for which the period construction gives a nonzero ℓ-adic scalar z of infinite multiplicative order. In a period basis adapted to the filtration the associated operator has eigenvalues zh, one for each jump h. Every auxiliary member therefore gives the same nth power trace Sn = ∑h∈Jτ (zh)n, counting repetitions. Write Xn for the corresponding trace on the target representation. Write U for the operator on the auxiliary matrix lattice, whose dimension may exceed the rank of the target. The period comparison bounds its entries and those of the level operators U′ by one constant B ≥ 1. For each precision q it supplies a level and a map Π with ‖UΠ − ΠU′‖ ≤ Bℓ−q, where the norm is the largest absolute value of an entry. The telescoping sum for UnΠ − ΠU′n has terms Ua(UΠ − ΠU′)U′n−1−a. The ultrametric triangle inequality bounds the sum by its largest term. For each fixed power the error thus tends to zero, even if the dimensions of the level lattices grow.

Period operators commute with equivariant evaluation on vectors. If e represents the identity in lattice coordinates, evaluation of Une on each vector therefore gives the nth power of the target operator on that vector. Applying the matrix trace functional to Une recovers Xn. The comparison map preserves that vector and the weighted trace functional to within ℓ−q. Together with the power estimate this gives the following bound with Kn independent of q. The scalar Cq is the sum of the level coefficients in the coefficient embedding used for τ, since every auxiliary member has the very same Sn.

|Xn − CqSn|ℓ ≤ Knℓ−q,   X₀ = S₀ = m.

At power zero this estimate gives |1−Cq|ℓ ≤ K₀ℓ−q/|m|ℓ. Division is valid even when ℓ divides the positive rank. Thus Cq tends to 1. Holding n fixed and letting q increase now gives Xn = Sn. The power-sum recursion recovers the multiset of eigenvalues from its first m positive power sums. Since z has infinite order, zh determines the integer h, including its sign. This recovers the entire multiset Jτ. For an arithmetic check, the lists {0,1,2} and {0,1,4} give identical power sums at z = −1 because 2 and 4 are both even. At z = 2 their first sums are 7 and 19. This is the role of the infinite-order choice.

How does the same companion acquire the local Weil polynomial?

Crystallinity gives the local period space on which Frobenius acts. Its Weil polynomial requires another comparison. For each local Weil element σ, each integer N ≥ 0 and each ε > 0, the congruence theorem supplies a finite set F of these approximants and coefficients cρ in the ℓ-adic coefficient field. Their weighted sum of global traces approximates tr r(g) to within ε for every g ∈ Γ. Using the same F and the same coefficients for every 0 ≤ j ≤ N, the weighted sum of their jth Weil–Deligne power sums at σ approximates the prescribed jth power sum to within ε. Both local quantities are compared in the ℓ-adic absolute value after transport of the complex power sums by ι−1. The joint closure theorem gives equality of these power sums with those of the Weil–Deligne representation of the limit. The zeroth sum gives the dimension, and the positive sums give the polynomial by the coefficient recursion. This gives the local comparison above ℓ at the chosen place.

How can the chosen place also be one of the original auxiliary places?

Here E contains √(−d), −d is a square in ℚℓ, [E⁺∶ℚ] is even, and E/E⁺ is unramified at finite places. All places above ℓ are split. The remaining field assumption provides three places w₁, w₂, w₃ above ℓ with pairwise distinct underlying primes in E⁺. Fix once and for all the representation from the first two. For an arbitrary spherical target place w above ℓ, at most one of these three lies over the same real-subfield prime as w. Choose the other two for a new auxiliary pair. The corresponding companion now has the strong local conclusion at w. Both companions match the same source polynomial at almost all places of absolute degree one away from ℓ, by the two splitting tests and sphericity. Their characteristic polynomials agree on Γ by continuity and density. Brauer–Nesbitt identifies the semisimple representations. The intertwining isomorphism carries the inertia action, filtered period modules and Weil–Deligne polynomial to those of the companion fixed at the outset. The trace argument for Brauer–Nesbitt.

ru,v ≃ rw₁,w₂   with u, v chosen away from the prime below w.

Why the real-subfield prime matters. Take E⁺ = ℚ(√2,√3), E = E⁺(i), d = 1, and ℓ = 97. The real degree is four. The quadratic extension E/E⁺ is unramified at finite places. Its defining polynomials X²+1 and X²+X+1 have discriminants −4 and −3, respectively. The latter generates the same extension since √3 ∈ E⁺. At every finite place at least one of these discriminants is a unit, so the corresponding quadratic algebra is unramified there. Modulo 97 the numbers 14, 10 and 22 square to 2, 3 and −1, respectively. Their negatives give the other simple roots. Thus the primes of E⁺ above 97 are labeled by sign pairs for (√2,√3), and each splits into two in E. Take the auxiliary real-subfield primes (+,+), (+,−), (−,+). A target over (+,+) uses (+,−) and (−,+), regardless of which root of −1 labels the target itself. This gives the required conclusion for either target on the originally fixed companion. Sutherland, Theorem 12.19 and §12.4 on discriminants of orders.
