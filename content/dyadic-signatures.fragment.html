How many sign patterns can a dyadic class miss?

Let f > 1 be odd, n ≥ 2, M = f2n and L = ℚ(ζM). Write G = (ℤ/M)× and let J = −1 act by complex conjugation. On the ideal class group C = Cl(𝒪L), use multiplicative notation. Put K = {x ∈ C ∣ x(Jx) = 1} and D = {x/(Jx) ∣ x ∈ C}. Then D ⊆ K. A class in K has trivial product with its conjugate. The index [K : D] measures the additional requirement that the class be a quotient by conjugation. To estimate it, the proof records signs in the maximal real subfield L+. Represent x ∈ K by an ideal I. The product I·JI is principal, and it admits a real generator a. Indeed, for an initial generator b, the quotient Jb/b is a unit of absolute value 1 at every embedding, hence a root of unity. In this tower the map v ↦ v/(Jv) from units onto roots of unity is surjective. Choose its value to be Jb/b. Then a = bv is fixed by conjugation. Two real generators of the same ideal differ by a real unit, so they determine the same coset of S. Multiplying I by a principal fractional ideal (z) multiplies its product with its conjugate by the totally positive factor zJz. Multiplying I by a fractional ideal of the form A/(JA) leaves that product fixed. These observations explain why signs modulo S measure classes modulo D. The quotient (1−ζc)/(1−ζ) and its inverse are both integral geometric sums, using an inverse of c modulo M for the latter. Multiplication by ζ−k centers the exponents in pairs and makes the unit uc real. For the representative 0 < t < M, summing the geometric progression gives

Take f = 3, n = 3 and c = 13. Order the pairs ±t by 1, 5, 7, 11. The floors ⌊13t/24⌋ are 0, 2, 3, 5, giving u13 the pattern 0011. Since 13−1 ≡ 13 modulo 24, multiplication by its inverse sends these four pairs to 11, 7, 5, 1. The correction k = 6 is even, so Θ(1+[J]) gives 1100. One can check this directly from the four lower-half coefficients 1, 3, 4, 6. Every translate of 0011 is either 0011 or its complement, since {±1,±5} is an index-two subgroup.
