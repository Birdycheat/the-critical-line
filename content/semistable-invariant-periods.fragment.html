Why do the invariant periods form an invertible matrix?

To construct the first integral frame we start with the rational representation. Let ℓ be a prime, K a finite extension of ℚℓ inside an algebraic closure and GK its absolute Galois group. Write K₀ for the maximal unramified subfield of K. Let V be a semistable representation of GK of dimension d over ℚℓ. A basis, taken from the given lattice when one is specified, gives matrices ρ(g). In the semistable period ring Bst the invariant tensors are the columns v satisfying ρ(g)g(v) = v for every g. Here g acts on each coefficient of the column. Semistability gives d independent such columns over K₀. We need them to form a basis after allowing arbitrary coefficients in Bst.

Regularity of the period ring. We use two parts of the regularity theorem. The invariant elements of the fraction field of the domain Bst form K₀. Also, a nonzero element of the ring whose ℚℓ-line is stable under GK is a unit. The first assertion detects dependence of columns. The second controls the inverse of their determinant. Brinon and Conrad, CMI Summer School Notes on p-adic Hodge Theory, Theorem 9.2.10 and Proposition 9.2.11.

Choose a K₀-basis of invariant columns v₁, …, vd. Suppose that some are dependent over the fraction field. Choose a relation with the fewest nonzero coefficients and divide by one coefficient to make it 1. Apply g to the coefficients and to the columns, then multiply the resulting column equation by ρ(g). Each column returns to itself. Subtracting the initial relation removes the coefficient 1. Minimality forces all remaining coefficient differences to vanish, for every g. The coefficients would therefore lie in K₀, contradicting the choice of a basis. Thus the columns are independent over the fraction field.

Q = (v₁ … vd),   ρ(g)g(Q) = Q,   g(det Q) = det(ρ(g))−1 det Q.

The determinant is nonzero by independence. Its multiplier det(ρ(g))−1 belongs to ℚℓ×, so regularity makes det Q a unit in the period ring itself. The adjugate formula now puts Q−1 in the same ring. For example, on the cyclotomic line ρ(g) = χ(g), the fundamental period t satisfies g(t) = χ(g)t. The single column t−1 satisfies the required equation and its inverse is t. The entry of a period matrix can therefore move under Galois even when its tensor column is invariant. The logarithmic derivative measures how these invariant columns depend on the polynomial variable of Bst.
