Which marked base points have a coincidence above them?

The calculation on one torus fibre gives a weight once the two maps agree on the base. We now identify the base points at which to use it. Work on the standard complex unitary model with parameters a = m = 1, at the infinite place v of positive index one, with the distinguished embedding prescribed by the datum. Fix the auxiliary prime p. Take principal levels N and N′ at least 3, prime to p, with N dividing N′. Write K and K′ for their level groups away from p, and Kp for the prescribed integral subgroup at p. The Hecke element h must satisfy hK′h−1 ⊆ K.

A complex point at level N′ determines a rational polarised form B up to equivalence. Within this class write its coordinates as (b, bp, ℓ). Here b gives the marking away from p, bp gives the local lattice marking, and ℓ is a positive complex line at v. The right action of K′ × Kp changes the finite representatives. The rational similitudes of B of positive multiplier act on the left on all three coordinates. The period and lattice description of this particular unitary model identifies its points with the resulting classes. The classical moduli interpretation describes the underlying polarisation, endomorphisms and level orbit. J. S. Milne, Introduction to Shimura Varieties, Theorem 8.17.

Coincidence criterion. The identity leg and the h-leg have the same image in the base of level N if and only if some rational similitude γ of B satisfies the following three conditions. Write γv for its complex action, γp for its image away from p, and γp for its image at p.

γvℓ = ℓ,   b−1γpb ∈ h−1K,   bp−1γpbp ∈ Kp.

Proof. The first leg forgets the deeper marking. In rational coordinates the second replaces b by bh−1 and preserves the other two coordinates. Equality of their images therefore asks whether a rational γ takes (bK, bpKp, ℓ) to (bh−1K, bpKp, ℓ). Reading the three coordinates gives the criterion. The geometric input for this description of the second leg is the marked lattice isogeny. Its inclusion identifies the rational periods, transports the lattice at p, and gives the marking bh−1 away from p. Conversely, the two finite conditions identify the target lattices with their markings, and the line condition makes the identification complex linear. An isometry of the marked periods represents the same point of the fine moduli space.

Every coincidence on the fibre power projects to such a base point. Conversely, above each such point the zero tuple is a coincidence, since the isogenies and each diagonal endomorphism send zero to zero. For the weighted count assume in addition that every g in h−1K has a component away from p with commutative centralizer in the local algebra. Take each diagonal label divisible by 2 in the ring of integers. The tower trace uses nonzero labels as well, so its twisted leg is finite étale. In the period calculation, regularity and the label conditions give the nonvanishing tangent determinants. The tangent space of the coincidence locus is the kernel of the difference of the two differentials. It vanishes, so each coincidence is isolated. The target is separated and the source is of finite type over ℂ. The coincidence locus is consequently a closed scheme of finite type of dimension zero and has finitely many points. We may sum the fibre weights over their finite set of base points.

How does the marking remove the stabilizer from the count?

Keep a representative x = (b, bp, ℓ). For a level subgroup U away from p, let ΓU(x) consist of the rational similitudes that preserve ℓ and both marked cosets bU and bpKp. These elements are the automorphisms of the marked point. The marked orbit sum gives each representative the factor 1/|ΓU(x)|. It multiplies the sum over the rational elements that pass the three tests. For a finite stabilizer this factor follows from orbit–stabilizer. A conjugacy orbit of size |Γ|/|Γγ| in the inner sum contributes its common weight divided by |Γγ|, where Γγ fixes γ under conjugation.

Rigidity at full level. For U = K or K′ the group ΓU(x) is trivial. In fact, the two finite conditions give an automorphism w of the polarised period lattice Λ preserving the full marking at that level. The line condition makes w complex linear on the period space. It preserves the positive definite metric obtained from the alternating polarisation and the complex structure. For each vector of a lattice basis its image belongs to the finite intersection of a sphere for this metric with the lattice. The powers of w therefore form a finite group. The full marking gives w ≡ I modulo the level on all of Λ. By Minkowski’s lemma the principal congruence subgroup of GL(Λ) of any level at least 3 has no nonidentity element of finite order. Thus w fixes Λ pointwise. The lattice spans the rational representation, on which the endomorphism algebra acts faithfully. The algebra component of the similitude is the identity, and the similitude equation for the nondegenerate form forces its multiplier to be 1. J.-P. Serre, Bounds for the orders of the finite subgroups of G(k), Lecture I, §1.2, Lemma 1.

There is also at most one γ passing the three tests for a fixed representative at level K′. If γ and δ both work, their quotient γ−1δ preserves ℓ and the marked coset at p. Away from p, write gγ = b−1γpb and similarly for δ. Then gγ−1gδ = (hgγ)−1(hgδ) belongs to K. The quotient belongs to ΓK(x), so δ = γ. Here rigidity at the target level proves uniqueness, while rigidity at the source level gives |ΓK′(x)| = 1 in the orbit sum.

For each contributing base point let Pγ be the minimal polynomial over the centre of the algebra component of γ, and let μγ be its eigenvalue on ℓ. Changing the rational representative conjugates γ and carries ℓ to the new line, so both quantities agree. Changing the finite representative by u ∈ K′ preserves the condition away from p because h(u−1gγu) = (hu−1h−1)(hgγ)u, with both exterior factors in K. Right multiplication by an element of Kp preserves the local test by conjugation inside that subgroup. Grouping the finite sum by its projection to the base now contributes exactly one fibre weight at each of these marked pairs (Pγ, μγ). Distinct base points can yield the same pair and each keeps its contribution.

How many deeper markings contribute to a double coset?

At level K the test is the indicator of Kh−1K. At level K′ a specific lift of the marking must satisfy the sharper condition hg ∈ K. To count these lifts put J = K ∩ h−1Kh. The assumptions N ∣ N′ and hK′h−1 ⊆ K give K′ ⊆ J. The principal levels are compact open and [K ∶ K′] is finite.

Counting lemma. In any group, under these subgroup and finite-index hypotheses, for every element g one has

#{uK′ ∈ K/K′ ∣ hu−1gu ∈ K} = [J ∶ K′] · 1Kh−1K(g).

Proof. Replacing u by uk for k ∈ K′ multiplies the tested element on the left by hk−1h−1 and on the right by k. Both lie in K. If a lift passes, writing hu−1gu = k0 gives g = uh−1k0u−1 ∈ Kh−1K. Conversely suppose g = ah−1b with a, b ∈ K. In hu−1gu the last factor bu lies in K. The remaining condition is h(a−1u)−1h−1 ∈ K, equivalently a−1u ∈ J. The acceptable cosets are precisely ajK′ with jK′ ∈ J/K′. Multiplication by a gives the asserted bijection.

To apply this lemma, fix the base marking (b, bp, ℓ) at level K. Its lifts at level K′ replace b by bu, for uK′ ∈ K/K′. Every lift arises this way by the equivalence of marked coordinates. A rational similitude identifying two lifts would fix the original marking, whose stabilizer is trivial. Thus these cosets give distinct lifts. For a fixed γ the line, its eigenvalue and the condition at p are the same on every lift. Only the away-from-p condition varies, with g = b−1γpb. The lemma therefore multiplies each surviving weight by [J ∶ K′]. Summing over the finite support gives the deeper-level geometric sum as this index times the marked sum for the double-coset test. The normalized Hecke action divides by the same index.

A finite group check. In the permutation group on four letters let K fix 4, K′ = {1} and h = (3 4). The subgroup J fixes both 3 and 4, so J = {1, (1 2)}. For g = h exactly those two among the six possible markings pass. For g = 1 the test reads h ∈ K and none passes.
