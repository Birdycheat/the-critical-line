How can small ideals bound a regulator?

The flag coefficient contains one copy of the residue of ζE at 1 for each block of the flag. We first estimate this residue for any totally complex number field E of degree 2f. Write D for its absolute discriminant, Y = √D, h for its class number and R for its regulator. We will prove that for every real s > 1,

hR ≤ Ys (s/(s−1))2f.

Use one embedding σv from each complex conjugate pair, and write ℓv(x) = 2 log |σv(x)|. Their sum is log |NE/ℚ(x)|. On units the sum is zero. Delete the last coordinate. The unit logarithms then form a lattice Λ in ℝf−1 with covolume R, in the same normalization as the coefficient-line mass. We will cover its quotient torus once for each ideal class. Sutherland, Proposition 15.11 and Theorem 15.12, Dirichlet’s unit theorem.

Fix a class C and a nonzero integral ideal 𝔟 in its inverse class. For any point t of ℝf−1, append a last coordinate so that the full vector b has sum log(N𝔟 · Y). In ℂf take the product of open discs with radii exp(bᵥ/2). Its Euclidean volume is πfN𝔟 · Y. The lattice 𝔟 has covolume 2−fN𝔟 · Y. Thus the disc product has volume strictly greater than 22f times the covolume. It is convex and symmetric, so Minkowski’s lattice point theorem supplies a nonzero x ∈ 𝔟 with ℓv(x) < bᵥ for every v. The ideal 𝔞 = (x)𝔟−1 is integral and lies in C. The sum of the coordinate gaps is log(Y/N𝔞), so N𝔞 ≤ Y. Sutherland, Theorem 14.13, Minkowski’s lattice point theorem.

For each such 𝔞 fix a generator y of 𝔟𝔞. Since y/x is a unit, translating t by its unit logarithm and subtracting the first f−1 coordinates of ℓ(y) leaves the first f−1 gaps bᵥ−ℓv(x). They are nonnegative and their sum is at most log(Y/N𝔞). Thus one translated simplex per small ideal covers the torus. A simplex in k = f−1 coordinates given by uᵢ ≥ 0 and ∑uᵢ ≤ L has volume Lk/k!, by successive integration. In dimension zero its volume is one. Projecting a measurable simplex to the torus can only decrease its volume, as one sees by cutting it along translates of a fundamental domain. Hence R is at most the sum of these simplex volumes over the small ideals in C.

Sum over the h classes. Each nonzero integral ideal of norm at most Y enters once. For u = Y/N𝔞 ≥ 1 the exponential series gives (log u)k/k! ≤ u ≤ us. Extending the sum to all nonzero integral ideals therefore bounds hR by YsζE(s). Above a rational prime p there are at most 2f prime ideals, each of norm at least p. Indeed, the integer ring modulo p has p2f elements and maps onto the product of their residue fields by the Chinese remainder theorem. Unique factorization of ideals now bounds the Euler product by ζ(s)2f. Finally ζ(s) ≤ 1 + ∫1∞t−s dt = s/(s−1), proving the estimate.

What does one zeta residue cost per flag block?

Put d = D1/(2f), so Y = df. Let w be the number of roots of unity in E. The analytic class number formula identifies the coefficient of the simple pole, ρE = lims↓1(s−1)ζE(s), with (2π)fhR/(wY). In particular ρE > 0. The mass summed over the ideal classes of coefficient lines is ΔρE with Δ = 2−fY. Each rank-j block contributes this single residue when its volume is expanded. Its remaining zeta factors are the ordinary convergent values ζE(2),…,ζE(j). Sutherland, Theorem 19.12, analytic class number formula.

We need a positive lower bound for L = log d. Minkowski’s ideal-class bound applied to the principal class gives 1 ≤ (2f)! (2f)−2f(4/π)fY. In the factorial ratio the first f factors are at most ½ and the rest at most 1. Hence Y ≥ (π/2)f, giving d ≥ 3/2. Consequently L ≥ 2/5, since log x ≥ 2(x−1)/(x+1) for x ≥ 1, as follows by differentiation. Sutherland, Theorem 14.20, Minkowski’s bound for an ideal class.

Take s = 1+1/L in the bound for hR. Now Ys−1 = exp(f) and s/(s−1) = 1+L. Dividing by Y in the residue formula and using w ≥ 1 gives ρE ≤ [2πe(1+L)²]f. The lower bound for L gives 1+L ≤ (7/2)L. Since 2πe < 18 and 18(7/2)² < 16², we obtain

0 < ρE ≤ (16 log d)2f,   ρEa ≤ exp(2fJ(3+n))   if   log d ≤ exp(n).

The second inequality is the one used for one ordered flag type. There are a positive rank jumps with sum J, so a ≤ J. The ambient rank is n. Using 16 ≤ exp(3) gives ρE ≤ exp(2f(3+n)) before raising to the power a. For jumps (1,2), there are two residues and just one ordinary zeta value, ζE(2). The estimate spends the larger budget for J = 3.

The ordinary values have a smaller cost. From the same Euler-product comparison, ζE(r) ≤ ζ(r)2f for every integer r ≥ 2. Using log x ≤ x−1 and summing the nonnegative geometric series gives ∑r≥2 log ζ(r) ≤ ∑m≥2 1/(m(m−1)) = 1. Thus the ordinary values in any one block have product at most exp(2f), and all blocks together cost at most exp(2fJ).

For a check of the normalization, take E = ℚ(√−3) with f = 1 and D = 3. Minkowski’s ideal-class bound is 2√3/π < 2, so every class contains the unit ideal and h = 1. The Eisenstein integers have six units, the six lattice points of norm one, and the unit rank is zero, giving R = 1. The residue is π/(3√3). The coefficient-line mass has the extra factor Δ = √3/2, giving π/6. This example uses the residue theorem for a totally complex field. The flag application additionally assumes the finite-unramified CM data of the dimension estimate.
