How is a regular shifted model placed among the columns?

Fix G = U(p,q), 1 ≤ q ≤ p, and m = p + q. Take a regular lowering λ′ = λ − es under the gap hypotheses for the shifted models. Both parameters must lie in the range of the kernel construction. The initial opposite-sign gap is at least 200m³, and lowering one entry costs at most one. Thus both gaps exceed the threshold for the weighted coefficient estimates. For each parameter use its positive kernel, a nonzero initial vector in its lowest compact type and the resulting derivative module, denoted M and M′. Their Hilbert closures carry π and πs. We will embed M′ into the columns of M by a complex-linear map ι intertwining the derivatives and K. Its image will be exactly the eigenspace of D at as = −λs − (m−1)/2.

A compact map and a positive pairing. Write T, T′ for the lowest compact types and Ψ, Ψ′ for their kernels. Their weights satisfy Λ′ = Λ − es. By the Pieri rule the compact type of weight Λ′ occurs in T ⊗ V*, and interchanging the two tensor factors gives a nonzero K-equivariant map ι0 ∶ T′ → V* ⊗ T. For an orthonormal basis (bi) of T′ consider

I = ∑i,j ∫G conj⟨ι0bi,(ρ(g) ⊗ Ψ(g))ι0bj⟩ ⟨bi,Ψ′(g)bj⟩ dg,

with ρ(g) = (g−1)ᵗ. The integral converges by the weighted bound, since |ρ(g)cd| ≤ ‖g‖F. Write g = k exp Y. The equivariance of the two kernels and of ι0 removes k, because the sum over an orthonormal basis does not change under a unitary change of basis. What remains is tr(SΨ′(exp Y)) with S = ι0*(ρ(exp Y) ⊗ Ψ(exp Y))ι0. Here ρ(exp Y) = exp(−Yᵗ) is positive definite, and the two kernels are positive semidefinite at exp Y by the first section. So S ≥ 0 and the trace of a product of two positive operators is nonnegative. At g = 1 the integrand is ∑‖ι0bj‖² > 0. By continuity Re I > 0.

From the pairing to a projection. The integrand of I pairs a coefficient of the column action with a coefficient of Ψ′. By the reproducing formula of the realization theorem, coefficients of Ψ′ are inner products in the closure of M′. Hence some coefficient of the column action, φ(f)(g) = ∑c⟨ηc,(A(g)f)c⟩ with A(g) the column action, has a nonzero component in that closure. Each such φ(f) is smooth and square integrable by the weighted bound. Let p(f) be its orthogonal projection onto the closure of M′. A finite-dimensional K-stable subspace of that closure lies in M′, so p maps columns into M′. It commutes with the derivatives and with K, and it is not zero.

The signed adjoint. Let jc = ±1 be the sign of coordinate c. For x ∈ M′ define ιx by ⟨(ιx)c,y⟩ = jc⟨x,p(y[c])⟩ for y ∈ M, with the column notation y[c] of the spectral splitting. The right side vanishes on all compact types above some level, so compact finiteness of M supplies a representing vector in M. The signs are what make ι commute with the derivatives. On smooth vectors the formal adjoints satisfy Eab* = jajbEba, and the column action carries the same pair of signs. The kernel of ι is a submodule of the irreducible module M′. It is not everything because p ≠ 0, hence ι is injective.

The eigenvalue. The tensor Casimir acts on ι(M′) by |λ′|² − |ρ|² = |λ|² − |ρ|² − 2λs + 1. By the expansion of the tensor Casimir it also equals |λ|² − |ρ|² + m + 2D there. Since m + 2as = 1 − 2λs, we get Dι = asι.

Filling the eigenspace and the ratio of formal degrees. Let P be the Lagrange projection onto the as-eigenspace given by the spectral splitting. The residue calculation gives its partial-trace scalar rs = ∏t≠s(λs − 1 − λt)/(λs − λt). Take the formal degrees d of π and d′ of πs for one Haar measure on G. For the lowering, the copy of πs accounts for a contribution d′/d to the partial trace. The degree defect gives d′/d ≤ rs. Equality forces every projected column Pf into ι(M′). For the reverse estimate start from πs, tensor with V and raise λ′ + es = λ. The same argument now places the original degree in the numerator and gives d/d′ ≤ ∏t≠s(λs − λt)/(λs − 1 − λt) = 1/rs. Hence

d′/d = rs,   ι(M′) = {f : Df = asf}.

Indeed rs ≥ d′/d > 0, so taking reciprocals in the reverse estimate gives the missing inequality rs ≤ d′/d. In the finite model of the spectral splitting the two eigenspaces are the line of the identity matrix and the traceless matrices. Their dimensions divided by dim ℂ² = 2 are 1/2 and 3/2. These equal r1 = (3/2 − 1 + 1/2)/2 and r2 = (−1/2 − 1 − 3/2)/(−2), so dimensions play the part of formal degrees.

How does an embedding of derivative modules become an identity on the group?

Work in the two unitary models above and let ι ∶ M′ → Mm be any complex-linear injection intertwining the derivatives and K. Write (A(g)F)c = ∑d ρ(g)cdR(g)Fd for the column action on the Hilbert space. Although ι is defined on derivative vectors, their coefficients agree on the whole group. Given a column f, there exists z ∈ M′ such that for every x′ ∈ M′

∑c ⟨fc,(A(g)ιx′)c⟩ = ⟨z,R(g)x′⟩   for all g ∈ G.

Conversely, given z ∈ M′, one can choose a single column f for all x′ and g.

The identity element. For the first identity, the functional y ↦ ∑c⟨fc,(ιy)c⟩ on M′ vanishes on compact types above some level. Indeed f lies in finitely many derivative layers, the compact Casimir is symmetric on columns, and ι intertwines it. Compact finiteness then gives a representing vector z, which does not depend on x′. For the converse, injectivity makes ⟨ιy,ιy′⟩ a definite form on each finite-dimensional eigenspace of the compact Casimir. Solving ⟨ιy0,ιy⟩ = ⟨z,y⟩ there gives the column f = ιy0.

The coefficient identity extends from 1 along a compact one-parameter subgroup and then along a noncompact one. At each point the two orbit series have equal Taylor coefficients because ι intertwines the derivatives. Their radii admit a lower bound independent of the point and the vector, so finitely many steps cover each segment. This reaches every element of G by polar decomposition, since g = exp(Z) exp(Y) with Z skew-Hermitian in the compact blocks and Y Hermitian in the noncompact directions.

Why the orbit series propagate the identity along a segment

Suppose a pair f, z gives the identity at h for every y ∈ M′. Along a compact or noncompact direction X, the coefficient of tk on either side at h exp(tX) is that identity applied to Xky/k!. On columns the derivative includes the factor exp(−tXᵗ) from the dual defining representation. In a compact direction the generators preserve a finite-dimensional layer, so its exponential series is entire. For a noncompact Y, the sum of the squared noncompact derivatives of a vector is the quadratic form of the compact Casimir minus the full Casimir. The former is bounded on layer j by a quadratic in j, and the latter acts by a scalar. Iteration gives

‖Yky‖ ≤ ‖Y‖Fk ∏l<k(C + √2(j+l)) ‖y‖   for y in the jth layer.

After division by k!, the product bound gives convergence for |t| < 1/(√2‖Y‖F + 1), regardless of the initial layer j. Taylor’s formula with remainder identifies the series with the orbit on this interval. Translation by h then preserves the convergence.

Back to the comparison. Take z = x′ = y in the converse identity. Then ⟨y,πs(g)y⟩ = ∑c,d ρ(g)cd⟨fc,R(g)(ιy)d⟩, a finite sum of products of an entry of ρ(g) and a coefficient of π. This is the reverse direction of the coefficient comparison. For the forward direction, split a column into eigenvectors of D. Each surviving eigenvector lies in the image of ι for its shift, and the first identity converts its coefficient into a coefficient of πs.
