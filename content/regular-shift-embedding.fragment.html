How is a regular shifted model placed among the columns?

Fix a regular lowering λ′ = λ − es. It has its own kernel Ψ′, lowest type T′ and generated module M′, and its Hilbert closure carries the model πs. Write as = −λs − (m−1)/2 and jc = ±1 for the sign of coordinate c. The coefficient comparison needs a map ι from M′ to the columns with entries in M with four properties. It commutes with the derivatives and with K, is injective, satisfies Dι = asι, and every column with Df = asf lies in its image. The construction produces ι from an integral and then proves that it is large enough.

A compact map and a positive pairing. The lowest weights differ in one coordinate: Λ′ = Λ − es. By the Pieri rule the compact type of weight Λ′ occurs in T ⊗ V*, so there is a nonzero compact map ι0 : T′ → T ⊗ V*. For an orthonormal basis (bi) of T′ consider

I = ∑i,j ∫G conj⟨ι0bi,(ρ(g) ⊗ Ψ(g))ι0bj⟩ ⟨bi,Ψ′(g)bj⟩ dg,

with ρ(g) = (g−1)ᵗ. The integral converges by the weighted bound, since |ρ(g)cd| ≤ ‖g‖F. Write g = k exp Y. The equivariance of the two kernels and of ι0 removes k, because the sum over an orthonormal basis does not change under a unitary change of basis. What remains is tr(SΨ′(exp Y)) with S = ι0*(ρ(exp Y) ⊗ Ψ(exp Y))ι0. Here ρ(exp Y) = exp(−Yᵗ) is positive definite, and the two kernels are positive semidefinite at exp Y by the first section. So S ≥ 0 and the trace of a product of two positive operators is nonnegative. At g = 1 the integrand is ∑‖ι0bj‖² > 0. By continuity Re I > 0.

From the pairing to a projection. The integrand of I pairs a coefficient of the column action with a coefficient of Ψ′. By the reproducing formula of the realization theorem, coefficients of Ψ′ are inner products in the closure of M′. Hence some coefficient of the column action, φ(f)(g) = ∑c⟨ηc,(A(g)f)c⟩ with A(g) the column action, has a nonzero component in that closure. Each such φ(f) is smooth and square integrable by the weighted bound. Let p(f) be its orthogonal projection onto the closure of M′. A finite-dimensional K-stable subspace of that closure lies in M′, so p maps columns into M′. It commutes with the derivatives and with K, and it is not zero.

The signed adjoint. For x ∈ M′ define ιx by ⟨(ιx)c,y⟩ = jc⟨x,p(y[c])⟩ for y ∈ M, with the column notation y[c] of the spectral splitting. The right side vanishes on all compact types above some level, so compact finiteness of M supplies a representing vector in M. The signs are what make ι commute with the derivatives. On unitary vectors Eab* = jajbEba, and the column action carries the same pair of signs. The kernel of ι is a submodule of the irreducible module M′. It is not everything because p ≠ 0, hence ι is injective.

The eigenvalue. The tensor Casimir acts on ι(M′) by |λ′|² − |ρ|² = |λ|² − |ρ|² − 2λs + 1. By the expansion of the tensor Casimir it also equals |λ|² − |ρ|² + m + 2D there. Since m + 2as = 1 − 2λs, we get Dι = asι.

Filling the eigenspace and the ratio of formal degrees. Let P be the Lagrange projection onto the as-eigenspace given by the spectral splitting. The residue calculation gives its partial-trace scalar rs = ∏t≠s(λs − 1 − λt)/(λs − λt). Let d and d′ be the formal degrees of π and πs for the same Haar measure. The image of ι supplies a copy of πs in the coefficient space of P, and the degree defect gives d′/d ≤ rs. Equality would force every column Pf into ι(M′). The same construction runs in the other direction. Start from πs, tensor with V and raise λ′ + es = λ. This gives d/d′ ≤ ∏t≠s(λs − λt)/(λs − 1 − λt) = 1/rs. Hence

d′/d = rs,   ι(M′) = {f : Df = asf}.

The product formula for formal degrees is not needed here, because the two inequalities pin down the ratio. In the finite model of the spectral splitting the two eigenspaces are the line of the identity matrix and the traceless matrices. Their dimensions divided by dim ℂ² = 2 are 1/2 and 3/2. These equal r1 = (3/2 − 1 + 1/2)/2 and r2 = (−1/2 − 1 − 3/2)/(−2), so dimensions play the part of formal degrees.

How does an embedding of derivative modules become an identity on the group?

The coefficient comparison is an identity of functions on G, while ι is defined through derivatives. Let ι be any injective map from M′ to columns that commutes with the derivatives and with K. Write (A(g)F)c = ∑d ρ(g)cdR(g)Fd for the column action. Two identities hold. For every column f and every x′ ∈ M′ there is z ∈ M′ with

∑c ⟨fc,(A(g)ιx′)c⟩ = ⟨z,R(g)x′⟩   for all g ∈ G.

Conversely, for all z, x′ ∈ M′ there is a column f with the same identity. Neither positivity nor integrability is used.

The identity element. For the first identity, the functional y ↦ ∑c⟨fc,(ιy)c⟩ on M′ vanishes on compact types above some level. Indeed f lies in finitely many derivative layers, the compact Casimir is symmetric on columns, and ι intertwines it. Compact finiteness then gives a representing vector z, which does not depend on x′. For the converse, injectivity makes ⟨ιy,ιy′⟩ a definite form on each finite-dimensional eigenspace of the compact Casimir. Solving ⟨ιy0,ιy⟩ = ⟨z,y⟩ there gives the column f = ιy0.

One-parameter subgroups. Suppose the identity holds at h for every vector of M′. Take X in the Lie algebra of G. For small |t| both sides at h exp(tX) are power series in t. On the right R(exp tX)y = ∑ tkXky/k!. On the left the column action contributes the series of exp(−tXᵗ) multiplied by the same orbit series. Since ι commutes with the derivatives, the coefficient of tk on each side is the identity at h applied to Xky. The radius of convergence must not depend on h or on y. In a compact direction Xk grows at most geometrically on a finite layer. In a noncompact direction, the Casimir minus the compact Casimir is the sum of the squared noncompact derivatives. This bounds that sum on the jth layer by a quadratic in j and gives

‖Yky‖ ≤ ‖Y‖Fk ∏l<k(C + √2(j+l)) ‖y‖   for y in the jth layer.

Dividing by k! and using Taylor’s formula with remainder for the orbit, the series converge for |t| < 1/(√2‖Y‖F + 1). Finitely many steps then cover a segment. The compact group is connected, so every g = k exp Y is exp(Z) exp(Y) with Z compact. Move first along exp(tZ), then along exp(Z) exp(tY).

Back to the comparison. Take z = x′ = y in the converse identity. Then ⟨y,πs(g)y⟩ = ∑c,d ρ(g)cd⟨fc,R(g)(ιy)d⟩, a finite sum of products of an entry of ρ(g) and a coefficient of π. This is the reverse direction of the coefficient comparison. For the forward direction, split a column into eigenvectors of D. Each surviving eigenvector lies in the image of ι for its shift, and the first identity converts its coefficient into a coefficient of πs.
