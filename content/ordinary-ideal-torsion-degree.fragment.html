How can one compute ideal torsion using a single endomorphism?

We next need the total rank of the group killed by 𝔴. If an ideal I has several generators, its torsion is the common kernel of their actions. It is easier to take the kernel of a single endomorphism. We can arrange this by multiplying I by an auxiliary ideal J whose torsion is already counted by the prime-to-q level data.

Auxiliary ideal lemma. Let C be the ring of integers of a number field, q a rational prime and I any nonzero integral ideal. There are a nonzero g ∈ C, an integral ideal J and an element t ∈ I such that

IJ = (g),   J + (q) = C,   1 − t ∈ J.

Proof. At each prime 𝔭 dividing (q)I, let h be the exponent of 𝔭 in I, possibly zero. Prescribe a residue g ≡ z𝔭 modulo 𝔭h+1, choosing z𝔭 in 𝔭h outside 𝔭h+1. These powers differ because nonzero ideals in a Dedekind domain have unique prime factorization. The Chinese remainder theorem supplies a single g satisfying the finitely many congruences. It has exactly the prescribed valuations at these primes, hence belongs to I and is nonzero. Factorization now gives an integral ideal J = (g)I−1. Every prime dividing (q)I has exponent zero in J. In particular I and J are comaximal. Write 1 = t + (1 − t) with the first summand in I and the second in J. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, Theorem 3.7 on ideal factorization and the Chinese remainder theorem.

It follows that I = (g,t) and J = (g,1 − t). For a ∈ I, the term a(1 − t) belongs to IJ = (g), and at belongs to (t). Their sum is a. For a ∈ J the same decomposition has at ∈ (g) and a(1 − t) ∈ (1 − t). Thus we know two generators for each ideal and a single generator for their product.

In the example above 2. Take I = 𝔴 in the biquadratic example and put g = 1 − β + βθ. At 𝔴 the first summand has valuation two and βθ has valuation one, so g has valuation one. At 𝔳 it reduces to 1. The relative norm of g down to ℚ(β) is (1 − β)² − 2β² = 3(1 − β). Taking the second norm gives 18. As 𝔴 has norm 2, the auxiliary ideal has norm 9. Here the auxiliary ideal has no factor above 2, while the complementary factor of (2) in the construction of the projector was 𝔳².

Why does the ideal-torsion group have rank equal to a power of the ideal norm?

Ideal-torsion degree theorem. Let A be the abelian scheme in the marked unitary moduli problem with parameters m, n and coefficient ring C = 𝒪F. For a nonzero ideal I of C, the points killed by all its coefficients form a finite flat subgroup A[I] of constant rank Nm(I)mn. The rank is the dimension of the coordinate algebra on a fibre. When I lies over the residue characteristic, this dimension can exceed the number of geometric points. That distinction is what makes the local length calculation necessary.

Two kernels whose ranks can be computed. Write h = mn. The base S in this moduli problem inverts every positive integer prime to q. On each geometric fibre the marking identifies the points killed by each such integer b with Λ/bΛ, compatibly with the action of C. The lattice Λ is free over ℤ of rank h[F : ℚ]. The determinant of a single nonzero coefficient g gives the rank |NmF/ℚ(g)|h of its kernel. For an ideal J prime to (q), one can count the kernel in the marked torsion itself. It is finite étale of rank Nm(J)h.

Why do the determinant and the marked torsion give these two ranks?

The kernel of a single coefficient. Put N = NmF/ℚ(g). This nonzero integer satisfies N/g ∈ C by the characteristic equation of multiplication by g on C, whose constant term is ±N. On a geometric fibre, the composite of [g] and [N/g] in either order is [N]. Multiplication by a nonzero integer on an abelian variety is finite and surjective. Thus [g] is surjective and its kernel is a closed subgroup of the finite group scheme killed by N. It is an isogeny, hence finite and flat. This argument allows q to divide N. Milne, Abelian Varieties, Proposition 7.1 and Theorem 7.2.

Choose a prime ℓ distinct from q. For every a ≥ 1 the marking identifies the action of g on Λ/ℓaΛ with its action on the ℓa-torsion of the fibre. The latter is the reduction of the ℓ-adic Tate module modulo ℓa. The characteristic polynomials on the lattice and on the Tate module therefore agree modulo every ℓa, so they agree over ℤℓ. The degree theorem for an abelian variety identifies the Tate characteristic polynomial with the integral polynomial P characterized by P(b) = deg([g] − [b]) for integers b, with degree zero when the difference is not an isogeny. In particular its value at zero gives the degree of [g]. Milne, Abelian Varieties, Theorem 10.9 and Proposition 10.20.

The rational lattice Λ ⊗ ℚ is an F-vector space, since C ⊗ ℚ = F. Its rational dimension is h[F : ℚ], so its F-dimension is h. In any F-basis, g acts by multiplication by g on each of the h copies of F. On one copy the rational determinant is N by definition of the field norm. Taking the absolute value of the determinant on the whole lattice gives deg([g]) = |N|h.

From the fibres to the base. The endomorphism [g] is proper since A is proper and separated over S. Its geometric fibres over points of its target are finite, so it is finite. On each fibre of A over S, flatness of the endomorphism descends from an algebraic closure of the residue field. The fibrewise criterion now gives flatness of [g] on A, since its source is flat and locally of finite presentation over S and its target is locally of finite type over S. The endomorphism is locally of finite presentation as a morphism between smooth S-schemes. Hence it is finite locally free. Surjectivity also holds on every fibre. Pullback by the identity section gives a finite locally free kernel over S of rank |N|h, as computed on every geometric fibre. The Stacks Project, proper maps with finite fibres, the fibrewise criterion for flatness and finite locally free morphisms.

The kernel of a prime-to-q ideal. Put M = Nm(J) = |C/J|. Multiplication by q is invertible on this finite group because J + (q) = C. Thus M is prime to q and is a unit on S. The order M annihilates the additive group C/J, so (M) ⊆ J. In particular A[J] lies in A[M]. This last group is finite locally free by the single-coefficient calculation and étale because multiplication by M is étale on every geometric fibre. Inside it, take the equalizer of each generator of J and the zero endomorphism. Each equalizer is open and closed, being a pullback of the diagonal of the separated étale scheme A[M] over S. There are finitely many generators, and their common equalizer therefore represents A[J] as a finite étale subgroup. If M = 1, then J = C. Being killed by 1 means being the identity, so the rank is 1. The Stacks Project, étaleness of integer multiplication on a fibre and the fibre criterion for étaleness.

For M > 1 the geometric marking identifies the points of A[J] with the classes killed by J in Λ/MΛ. A ℤ-basis generates Λ over C, and Λ embeds in its rational lattice, where every nonzero coefficient is invertible. Thus Λ is finite and torsion free over the Dedekind domain C. At each nonzero prime 𝔭 its localization Λ𝔭 is free of rank h. The Stacks Project, finite torsion-free modules over Dedekind domains.

For 𝔭 dividing (M), let π generate 𝔭 locally. Set t = ord𝔭(M) and s = ord𝔭(J), so 0 ≤ s ≤ t. A class modulo πt is killed by πs exactly when it is divisible by πt−s. The subgroup of such classes is

πt−sΛ𝔭 / πtΛ𝔭 ≅ (C𝔭/πsC𝔭)h.

Each of the s successive quotients by powers of π in one coordinate is the residue field of size Nm(𝔭). The local factor therefore contributes Nm(𝔭)sh points. The Chinese remainder theorem multiplies these counts over 𝔭, and the product is Nm(J)h. Since a finite étale scheme over an algebraically closed field has one copy of that field per point, this is the rank of A[J] on every geometric fibre. The Stacks Project, Chinese remaindering and étale schemes over a field.

Splitting the kernels. Choose IJ = (g) and t ∈ I with 1 − t ∈ J as in the auxiliary ideal lemma. The joint kernel of [g] and [t] represents I-torsion because these two coefficients generate I. The joint kernel of [g] and [1 − t] similarly represents J-torsion. For every test scheme and point x killed by g, send

x ↦ ( [1 − t]x, [t]x )   in   A[I] × A[J].

For every a ∈ I, the product a(1 − t) belongs to IJ, so a kills the first point. Every b ∈ J kills the second since bt ∈ IJ. Adding the two coordinates gives back x. Conversely, for y killed by I and z killed by J, the element y + z is killed by g. Applying the displayed map gives (y,z), because [t]y = 0 and [1 − t]z = 0. Both formulas are group-scheme morphisms. The test works also for points with nilpotent coordinates. Thus A[(g)] is the product of these two group schemes over the base.

Recovering the rank of the first factor. The product is finite flat. Its first factor sits inside it as the section y ↦ (y,0) of the projection. This section is closed since the second factor is finite, hence separated over the base. On an affine piece of the base the first factor is therefore a closed subscheme of a finite affine scheme. Its coordinate ring is a finite module, and the projection splits the restriction onto that ring. The product has a finite projective coordinate module. Its direct summand is again finite projective, so the first factor is finite locally free. On each field fibre the coordinate algebra of a product is a tensor product, so the two dimensions multiply. The positive rank Nm(J)h can now be cancelled. Indeed (g) = IJ and I, J are comaximal, so C/(g) ≅ C/I × C/J. The cardinality of C/(g) is the absolute determinant of multiplication by g on the integer lattice C, which is |NmF/ℚ(g)|. Hence

rank A[I] = |NmF/ℚ(g)|h / Nm(J)h = Nm(I)h.

For the ideals of norms 2 and 9 above, take t = −8. We have −8 ∈ 𝔴 since 2 ∈ 𝔴, and 1 − t = 9 ∈ J since |C/J| = 9. Thus a point x killed by g splits as ([9]x, [−8]x). Its first coordinate is killed by 𝔴, its second by J, and their sum is x. The ranks give 18h/9h = 2h. In the ordinary-point theorem m = 1 and I = 𝔴, giving total rank Qn. The argument elsewhere in this chapter determines how much of that length occurs at each geometric point.
