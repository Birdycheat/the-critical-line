Why does right translation act unitarily on the closure of the generated module?

Fix a kernel Ψ of parameter λ and a nonzero vector v0 in its lowest compact type T. For u ∈ T write fu(g) = ⟨v0,Ψ(g)u⟩. Their span V is where the desired representation starts. We shall recover Ψ by taking coefficients of the right action on a Hilbert space H. To construct H, first close V under the infinitesimal operators Eab, obtaining a module M, then take its closure in L²(G). These derivatives exist in Hilbert norm. Indeed the functions are smooth and square integrable, their compact derivatives stay in V by equivariance, and the full Casimir acts there by the kernel scalar. The unweighted derivative estimate proved below puts each iterated derivative in L². Right translation and the fundamental theorem of calculus on each one-parameter subgroup then give the derivatives in norm.

Right translation is unitary on L²(G). The modular character of G is trivial on the compact group and on the exponentials of the noncompact directions. By the polar decomposition it is trivial everywhere, so right translation preserves Haar measure. On compactly supported continuous functions translation is continuous in L² by uniform continuity on compact sets. Density and the isometry extend continuity to every vector.

H is invariant. Since R(g) is continuous, it suffices to send generated vectors into H. Write g = k exp Y. For a nonzero noncompact direction Y, the layer estimate gives a Taylor radius of at least 1/(√2‖Y‖F) at every time, for every generated vector. At time zero the coefficients are iterated derivatives in M, so the Taylor sums lie in H. Now suppose R(exp(t0Y)) sends every vector of M into H. The Taylor coefficients at t0 are translates by this very operator of derivatives in M. They too lie in H, which is closed. Finitely many steps shorter than the same radius reach time one. For the compact factor write k = exp Z. Each layer is finite-dimensional and stable under the derivative along Z, so that series converges for every t. The restriction of R to H is therefore a unitary representation, written σλ.

How far above the lowest type can a compact type lie?

Organize the generated module by derivative layers. L0 = V, and Lk+1 adds to Lk the images of Lk under the noncompact derivatives. Each layer is finite-dimensional and stable under the compact derivatives, and M is their union. Write κ(η) = |η + ρc|² − |ρc|² for the compact Casimir scalar of highest weight η, and let h > 2m be the opposite-sign gap.

Layer bound. Let w ∈ Lk+1 be a nonzero compact highest-weight vector of weight μ, orthogonal to Lk. Then μ − Λ is a sum of k + 1 noncompact roots, and

κ(μ) ≥ κ(Λ) + 2(k+1)(h − m + 1).

Two consequences are used throughout. A compact highest-weight vector of weight Λ in M lies in V, since its components beyond V would violate the bound. A compact highest-weight vector of weight μ lies in the layer of index ⌈(κ(μ) − κ(Λ))/(2(h − m + 1))⌉, so every compact type of M occurs in a bounded layer.

To see the size of one step, lift the new vector to a tensor with one noncompact slot for each derivative. In the compact Casimir each slot pairs with the lowest type. The Dirac inequality excludes the lowering summands, and each surviving slot contributes at least 2(h − m + 1).

Deriving the contribution of a noncompact slot

Lifting to a tensor. Multiplication X1 ⊗ ⋯ ⊗ Xk+1 ⊗ v ↦ X1⋯Xk+1v sends tensors of noncompact directions with a last factor in V onto Lk+1 modulo Lk. It intertwines the diagonal compact action. Project away from the tensors that land in Lk. Because the projected map is injective and equivariant, w lifts to a nonzero tensor P. This tensor is a compact highest-weight vector of weight μ, and it is orthogonal to every tensor landing in Lk. Among the nonzero coefficients of P, indexed by roots eaⱼ − ebⱼ, choose one maximizing ∑ⱼ(aⱼ − bⱼ). The highest-weight equations kill every compact raising operator on that coefficient, since a compensating term would require a larger index. Its weight in V is therefore Λ. Subtracting it from μ leaves precisely the indicated sum of roots.

Splitting the compact Casimir. On tensors the compact Casimir of the diagonal action is a sum of three terms: the Casimir of the slots, the Casimir on V, and twice the sum over slots of the mixed operator C between that slot and V. The first is nonnegative and the second is κ(Λ). On P the left side is κ(μ). It remains to bound each mixed term from below by (h − m + 1)‖P‖².

Raising and lowering directions. On one noncompact slot tensored with V, the mixed operator acts on the summand of highest weight Λ ± α, with α = ea − eb and a < b of opposite signs, by θ with 2θ = ±2⟨λ + ρn,α⟩ + 2 − m. Since ⟨λ,α⟩ ≥ h and ⟨ρn,α⟩ ≥ 2 − m/2, the raising summands have θ ≥ h − m + 1 and the lowering summands have θ ≤ −h − 1. The lowering part contributes nothing to M. Its image in L1 would contain a compact highest-weight vector of weight Λ − α on which the Casimir acts by |λ|² − |ρ|². The Dirac inequality excludes this. Here the lowered parameter λ − α is still strictly decreasing. Across opposite signs its gap loses at most two, and within each sign the integral compact highest weight preserves strict order after the shift by ρc. The exterior module contains an extremal vector of weight ρhol − ρn, where ρhol is the half-sum for the holomorphic noncompact directions. Tensoring with the lowered compact type gives a highest vector of weight λ − α − ρc + ρhol. On it twice the Dirac square acts by |λ − α|² − |λ|² = 2 − 2(λa − λb) < 0. That square is nonnegative because the Dirac operator is symmetric for a positive form. A slot can be moved to the last position at the cost of lower layers only, since the bracket of two noncompact directions is compact. So a tensor whose slices in one slot are lowering lands in Lk. Orthogonality of P to such tensors puts all its slices in the raising part, where the mixed operator is at least h − m + 1.

What does the layer bound say on U(1,1)?

Take signs (+,−), so ρc = 0, κ(η) = |η|² and Λ = (λ1 + 1/2, λ2 − 1/2). The mixed operator on the two noncompact directions has eigenvalues ±(λ1 − λ2 + 1). For the tensor P = E12⊗(k+1) ⊗ w the slot Casimir is 2(k+1)². Then 2(k+1)² + |Λ|² + 2(k+1)(Λ1 − Λ2) = |Λ + (k+1)(e1 − e2)|² = κ(μ). Dropping the slot term recovers the bound. The Dirac step can be seen directly. Let u have weight Λ − (e1 − e2), and suppose the Casimir acts on u by |λ|² − 1/2. Expanding the Casimir gives 2E12E21u = 2(λ1 − λ2 − 1)u. Infinitesimal unitarity for a noncompact pair then gives ‖E21u‖² = −(λ1 − λ2 − 1)‖u‖² < 0, a contradiction.

Why is the model irreducible, and why are its compact-finite vectors generated?

All three arguments below rest on one fact from the layer bound. A compact highest-weight vector of weight Λ in M lies in V. They also use infinitesimal unitarity, ⟨Eabx,y⟩ = sgn(a,b)⟨x,Ebay⟩, obtained by differentiating the unitarity of R.

The generated module is irreducible. Let N ⊂ M be stable under every Eab. If N meets V nontrivially, irreducibility of V under the compact derivatives gives V ⊂ N, hence N = M. Otherwise let fw be the highest-weight vector of V. On the finite-dimensional, compact-stable space N ∩ Lk, where Lk is the kth layer, the functional ⟨fw,·⟩ is represented by a vector z. By infinitesimal unitarity, the diagonal derivatives act on z by the weight Λ and the compact raising derivatives kill it. So z lies in V ∩ N = 0, and N is orthogonal to fw. The orthogonal of N in M is stable and contains fw, so it is all of M, and N = 0.

The lowest type occurs once in the closure. Let j : V → H be compact-equivariant with j(V) orthogonal to V. Differentiating the equivariance shows that y ↦ ⟨j(fw),y⟩ is again a highest-weight functional of weight Λ on M. It vanishes on V. The representing-vector argument shows that it vanishes on M. The vectors v ∈ V with j(v) orthogonal to M form a compact-stable subspace containing fw, hence all of V. Since j(V) ⊂ H and M is dense in H, j = 0.

The closure is irreducible. Let S ⊂ H be closed and invariant, with orthogonal projection P. Its orthogonal complement is invariant too. On smooth vectors both are stable under the derivatives, which are limits of difference quotients. Hence V ∩ S⊥ is 0 or V. If it is V, then H ⊂ S⊥ and S = 0. If it is 0, apply the previous paragraph to the component of P|V orthogonal to V. It vanishes, so P maps V into V. Each v − Pv then lies in V ∩ S⊥ = 0, so V ⊂ S and S = H.

Compact-finite vectors are generated. Take an irreducible compact type τ in H. Its character average Pτ projects onto the sum of all copies of τ and preserves every finite-dimensional compact-stable layer. The layer bound puts all highest vectors of that type in M in one fixed Lk. Compact invariance puts their whole copies there. Consequently Pτ maps M into Lk. By continuity, density of M and closedness of that finite-dimensional layer, Pτ(H) also lies there. Each type thus has finite multiplicity. A finite-dimensional compact-stable subspace is a sum of finitely many such types and lies in the largest of their layers. This is the compact finiteness used for the embedding of the shifted models.

Why do the coefficient functions reproduce the kernel?

The square integrability of the model and the weighted bound both use one identity. For u, u′ in T and g ∈ G,

⟨fu′,R(g)fu⟩ = ‖fw‖² ⟨u′,Ψ(g)u⟩,

where w is the unit highest-weight vector of T. Normalize Haar measure dk on K to have total mass one and put d = dimℂ T. Compact Schur orthogonality will determine the projection onto the lowest type. Rescaling Haar measure on G multiplies both sides of the displayed formula by the same factor.

The Gram identity. Right equivariance Ψ(xk) = Ψ(x)T(k) gives R(k)fu = fT(k)u. So the map u ↦ fu intertwines two unitary actions of K. Its Gram operator commutes with T and is a scalar by Schur’s lemma. Evaluating on w gives ⟨fu′,fu⟩ = ‖fw‖²⟨u′,u⟩. This is the formula at g = 1.

The residual has no lowest-type component. Project R(g)fu orthogonally onto the finite-dimensional space V; the projection is some fq. The residual x = R(g)fu − fq lies in H and is orthogonal to V. Put a = Ψ(g)u − q. The average B(u′) = ∫K⟨T(k)a,u′⟩R(k)x dk is a compact-equivariant map from T to H. Its image is orthogonal to V, because R(k)−1 preserves V. The lowest type occurs in H only once, by the multiplicity-one argument above, which does not use this formula. Hence B = 0.

Evaluation at the identity. The residual has the continuous representative x(y) = fu(yg) − fq(y). Since B(v0) = 0 in L², the continuous function h ↦ ∫K⟨T(k)a,v0⟩x(hk) dk integrates to zero over every compact set, and so vanishes everywhere. At h = 1, left equivariance gives x(k) = ⟨v0,T(k)a⟩, so ∫K|⟨v0,T(k)a⟩|² dk = 0. Schur orthogonality on K gives d∫K|⟨v0,T(k)a⟩|²dk = ‖v0‖²‖a‖², hence a = 0. Thus the projection of R(g)fu onto V is fΨ(g)u, and the Gram identity gives the formula.

Computing one reproducing coefficient on SU(1,1)

Take Ψ(x) = x11−6 and choose g with diagonal entries 5/4 and off-diagonal entries 3/4. Then (xg)11 = (5/4)x11(1 + z), where z = (3/5)x12/x11. At fixed y = |x11|², the phase of z is uniform and |z| < 3/5. Only the constant term of (1 + z)−6 survives the angular average. With radial Haar measure dy on [1,∞) and angular mass one, the resulting integral is (4/5)⁶∫₁∞y−6 dy = 4096/78125. Dividing by ‖Ψ‖² = 1/5 recovers Ψ(g) = (4/5)⁶.

In what sense is the model the discrete series of parameter λ?

For this large-gap family, the following five properties characterize the representation attached to λ. It is a nonzero irreducible unitary representation. Its coefficients are square integrable. It contains a compact type of highest weight Λ = λ + ρn − ρc. That type is minimal in Vogan's sense: |Λ + 2ρc|² ≤ |μ + 2ρc|² for every compact type μ that occurs. The uniqueness argument below recovers the Casimir scalar from these properties. The constructed space is nonzero because its lowest type embeds, and the preceding argument gives irreducibility and unitarity.

Square integrability. By the reproducing formula, ⟨fw,R(g)fw⟩ is a multiple of ⟨w,Ψ(g)w⟩, which is square integrable by the second threshold. In an irreducible unitary representation one nonzero square-integrable coefficient suffices. Fix y0 and consider the coefficient map x ↦ ⟨y0,σ(·)x⟩ on the vectors where it is square integrable. Its graph is closed and invariant. Let P be its orthogonal projection in H ⊕ L²(G). The first coordinate of P(x,0) defines a positive operator commuting with σ and taking values in the coefficient domain. It is nonzero since the graph contains a pair with nonzero first coordinate. By Schur’s lemma it is a scalar multiple of the identity, so the domain is the whole space and every coefficient of y0 is square integrable. Inversion exchanges the two entries and preserves Haar measure, so for any fixed y the coefficient with second entry y0 is square integrable. Apply the same graph argument again, this time with y fixed and with the nonzero test vector y0. This gives square integrability for every second entry.

The lowest type and its minimality. The space V is a compact type of highest weight Λ, and every copy of this particular type in H equals V. Let μ ≠ Λ occur. Choose the first layer Lj+1 containing its highest vector. Subtract its projection to Lj. The nonzero remainder has the same highest weight because this projection commutes with the compact action. The layer bound gives κ(μ) − κ(Λ) ≥ 2(j+1)(h − m + 1), where κ is the compact Casimir scalar. Moreover μ − Λ is a sum of j + 1 noncompact roots, each pairing with ρc to at least −m. Since |x + 2ρc|² = |x + ρc|² + 2⟨x,ρc⟩ + 3|ρc|²,

|μ + 2ρc|² − |Λ + 2ρc|² ≥ 2(j+1)(h − 2m + 1) > 0.

This is where the hypothesis h > 2m is used.

Uniqueness. Conversely, any representation with these five properties is unitarily equivalent to σλ. Minimality excludes compact types of weight Λ − (ea − eb) in the first layer. The Dirac square then makes the Casimir operator act by |λ|² − |ρ|² on the lowest type, and irreducibility spreads this to all smooth vectors. Take a compact isometry φ between the two lowest types. Form the direct sum and generate a closed subrepresentation from the diagonal lowest type {(v,φv)}. In each coordinate the compact type is Λ and the full Casimir acts by |λ|² − |ρ|². The diagonal type inherits both properties. Project the antidiagonal copy {(v,−φv)} onto this generated subrepresentation. That projection is compact-equivariant and remains orthogonal to the diagonal lowest type. Multiplicity one makes it zero. The antidiagonal is thus orthogonal to every group translate of the diagonal. Expanding this inner product gives ⟨φv′,σ(g)φv⟩ = ⟨v′,σλ(g)v⟩ for all g. The translates of the lowest type are total by irreducibility, so R(g)v ↦ σ(g)φv extends to a unitary intertwiner. Combined with the kernel, this proves existence for every parameter with h > max{2m, 16mq + 8√(2q)}. When q = 0 the group is compact and K = G, and this case is treated separately.

Why do the derivatives of a coefficient keep its weighted square integrability?

The weighted bound for generated vectors needs more than the kernel. A coefficient can be square integrable with a weight while its derivatives grow. The following estimate uses compact stability and the Casimir equation to control every derivative with the same weight. Let W be a space of smooth functions on G with three properties. It is stable under the compact derivatives Eij, those with i and j of the same sign. All its members are eigenfunctions of the Casimir operator C for one eigenvalue. Finally, ∫G ‖g‖F4a|w|² dg < ∞ for every w ∈ W, for some fixed a ≥ 0. Then every derivative Ep1⋯Epkw has the same weighted bound, and

∫G EijF · conj(F′) dg = sgn(i,j) ∫G F · conj(EjiF′) dg

for any two such derivatives F, F′. Here sgn(i,j) is +1 for a compact pair and −1 otherwise. The sign comes from conjugation: Eij(conj f) = −sgn(i,j) conj(Ejif), because JEij*J = sgn(i,j)Eji. The argument applies to any dimension of W and any common Casimir eigenvalue.

The estimate comes from the signed second-order operator T = 2CK − C. Integration by parts bounds the weighted first derivatives of u once u and Tu have finite weighted norm. Compact stability keeps T in the same derivative layer, which lets the estimate be iterated.

The cutoff, integration by parts and the bound on one derivative

Cutoffs with bounded derivatives. Measure size by ℓ(g) = log ‖g‖F². On G we have ‖g‖F² ≥ tr(Jg*Jg) = m, so ℓ ≥ 0. The right derivative of ℓ along X is 2 Re⟨g,gX⟩/‖g‖F², which has absolute value at most 2‖X‖F. Let χ be smooth and decreasing, equal to 1 on (−∞,1] and to 0 on [2,∞). The cutoff χ(ℓ/R) has compact support and derivatives of size O(1/R), uniformly on G. This is why the logarithm of the size is used rather than the size. For a smooth function ψ vanishing outside a compact set, ∫G RXψ dg = 0. Indeed G is unimodular, so the integral of ψ(g exp tX) does not depend on t.

A Gårding inequality. Let ρR = χ(ℓ/R)² e2aℓ, so that 0 ≤ ρR ≤ ‖g‖F4a and |EijρR| is at most a constant cij times χ(ℓ/R)e2aℓ once R ≥ 1. Integrate the derivative of ρRu conj(Eiju) along Eij. The Leibniz rule and the conjugation rule give

∫ρR|Eiju|² = −∫(EijρR) u conj(Eiju) + sgn(i,j)∫ρR u conj(EjiEiju).

Sum over all pairs i, j, both compact and noncompact. The last terms combine into ∫ρR u conj(Tu) with T = ∑ sgn(i,j)EjiEij = 2CK − C, where CK is the compact Casimir. Bound the first terms by the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality and absorb half of the left side. Write ‖u‖a for the weighted norm. The weighted integral of ∑|Eiju|² against ρR is then at most (1 + ∑cij²)‖u‖a² + ‖Tu‖a², uniformly in R. As R grows, ρR increases to the full weight, and monotone convergence shows that every Eiju is weighted square integrable whenever u and Tu are.

Induction on the length of a derivative. Let Vk be the span of all derivatives of members of W by words of length at most k. Moving a compact derivative to the right through a word changes the word only by words of the same length, by the commutation relations, so CK preserves Vk. The Casimir operator commutes with every Eab, so it acts on Vk by the eigenvalue of W. Hence T maps Vk to itself. If Vk is weighted square integrable, the inequality applies to every u ∈ Vk and puts Vk+1 in the same space. The adjoint identity then follows by the same integration by parts with the cutoff χ(ℓ/R) alone. Its error term is O(1/R) times an integrable function, and dominated convergence removes the cutoff.

Which Casimir controls the derivative on U(1,1)?

For f(g) = g11−k with an integer k ≥ 2 one finds E11f = −kf, E12f = E22f = 0 and Cf = (k² − k)f, while Tf = (k² + k)f. At a = 0 the summed identity reads ∑‖Eijf‖² = ⟨f,Tf⟩, so the noncompact derivative has ‖E21f‖² = k‖f‖². In polar coordinates with x = cosh²r, Haar measure is proportional to dx. The two sides are k²∫1∞(x−1)x−k−1dx = k/(k−1) and k∫1∞x−kdx.

Weighted coefficients of the generated vectors. The signed column pairing and the embedding of the shifted models assume that ‖g‖F⟨y,R(g)x⟩ is square integrable for all x, y ∈ M. Take a = 1/2, so the weight is ‖g‖F². Fix y in V and let W consist of the coefficients ⟨y,R(·)x⟩ with x ∈ V. By the reproducing formula these are multiples of coefficients of Ψ. The third threshold with s = 2 makes them square integrable with this weight. The space W is stable under the compact derivatives and consists of Casimir eigenfunctions, so the lemma applies. A derivative of a coefficient is the coefficient of the derivative, ⟨y,R(·)Eabx⟩ = Eab⟨y,R(·)x⟩. Hence the claim holds for y ∈ V and all x ∈ M.
