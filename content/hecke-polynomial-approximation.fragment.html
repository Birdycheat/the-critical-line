How can one approximation work for every Hecke polynomial?

The Galois construction compares sums and products of Hecke eigenvalues. A finite weighted list of auxiliary systems must work for every polynomial at once. Fix the algebraic weight and the level away from the auxiliary split place above ℓ. At that place the approximants can have deeper level. Write θ for the joint eigenvalues of our form and ηi for those of auxiliary eigenforms. In this passage scalars are read through the fixed identification with the algebraic closure of ℚℓ, and |·| means the ℓ-adic absolute value.

Use the integral family of linear evaluations ex of forms, indexed by a group point and a weight-space test vector. Three properties of this family enter. A nonzero form has a nonzero evaluation, every form in the fixed space has bounded evaluations, and each chosen integral Hecke operator Tj preserves any common bound on the evaluations of a vector. The local denominator bound on each term gives the last property. Normalize the target eigenform f so that e0(f) = 1 at one detecting coordinate. Let R ≥ 1 bound all its evaluations. The density theorem away from the auxiliary place approximates f by a finite sum ∑ifi of allowed eigenforms, with pointwise error given by evaluations of f times a factor bounded by ε/R. Thus the residual has every evaluation bounded by ε.

For any polynomial P with integer coefficients put u(P) = P(θ)f − ∑iP(ηi)fi. For a constant integer this is a multiple of the residual by a scalar of absolute value at most one. Addition preserves the bound ε by the ultrametric inequality. Multiplication by a variable Xj replaces u(P) by Tju(P) because every vector in the sum is an eigenvector for that operator. These three operations construct every integral polynomial. Evaluating at the same coordinate e0 gives

|P(θ) − ∑η cη P(η)| ≤ ε,   cη = ∑i : ηi=η e0(fi).

The finite support and all the coefficients have been chosen before P. The coordinate index set may be infinite because each polynomial uses only finitely many variables. If two forms have the same system, they contribute to the same coefficient. We write θ ∈ 𝒜(S) when for each ε > 0 such a finite weighted list exists in the set S of systems.

How can one remove systems that fail a local test?

Suppose η ∈ 𝒜(T), and let Q be an integral polynomial that is nonzero at η. We want to retain only points x ∈ T with Q(x) ≠ 0. Start with an approximation to η at the finer tolerance ε|Q(η)|. Apply it to PQ and divide by Q(η). The terms with Q(x) = 0 drop out. Each surviving coefficient changes in the same way for every test polynomial:

c′x = cx Q(x)/Q(η),   |P(η) − ∑x c′x P(x)| ≤ ε.

For a finite calculation in ℚ₅ approximate evaluation at 5 by the average of evaluations at 0 and 10. This rule is exact on constants and linear polynomials. On every higher power both the target value and the average are divisible by 25 in ℤ₅. It therefore gives error at most 1/25 on every integral polynomial. Take Q(X) = X. The point 0 disappears and the new coefficient at 10 is (1/2)·10/5 = 1. For P = X the error is now |5−10|₅ = 1/5. It is obtained from the old error on X², namely |25−50|₅ = 1/25, by division by |5|₅. This one finite rule illustrates exactly the precision lost in filtering.

There are two approximation stages in the arithmetic argument. Suppose θ ∈ 𝒜(S) and, for each η ∈ S, η ∈ 𝒜(T) with Q(η) ≠ 0. Choose an outer list at tolerance ε/2 with coefficients aη, and put M = ∑|aη|. For every η on this finite list choose a filtered inner rule with error δ = ε/[2(1+M)]. Sum the rules, giving a shared point x the sum of all products aηc′η,x that reach it. Adding and subtracting the evaluations at η gives

|P(θ) − ∑x c″x P(x)| ≤ ε/2 + Mδ ≤ ε.

The nonvanishing condition holds at the intermediate points where we divide. The outer target θ can lie on the zero set of Q. After filtering we may also forget coordinates. Lift each polynomial in the remaining variables to a polynomial in the original variables, and add the coefficients of all points that project to the same remaining system. The estimate is identical.

Which local conditions survive the approximation?

Keep two split auxiliary places u,v over distinct primes of the real subfield above ℓ. Fix finite sets Z,W of eligible spherical places away from those auxiliary primes, where Z prescribes genericity tests. The power-sum places in W are above ℓ. Both sets consist of split places spherical for the target. In the odd-rank application we test genericity on Z ∪ W. In addition to the original Hecke coordinates indexed by J, introduce one coordinate for every pair (w,n) ∈ W × ℤ, and one detector coordinate for each place z in the finite test set:

θj,   aw,n · power sum at (w,n),   bz · detector at z.

The power-sum operator has the indicated Satake eigenvalue. A spherical Hecke operator at z detects nonvanishing of the chosen genericity polynomial on the Satake parameters. Each nonzero multiplier aw,n or bz puts the corresponding operator within the integral bounds required by the frame. The power-sum multipliers are fixed with Z,W before the family of original Hecke operators is chosen. They may depend on the place and the exponent. The product Q of the detector variables is an integral polynomial because the test set is finite. As each multiplier is nonzero, Q detects simultaneous success at every tested place.

First apply fixed-weight density with generic local systems and the eigenform argument to approximate the extended system by intermediate ones on which Q is nonzero. For each of these, the weight variation at v and the character construction at u provide a second approximation, with the required lacunary weight and auxiliary local type. These operators are supported away from both places, so the same extended eigenvalue coordinates can be tested in this second step. Apply the filtering and composition formula to preserve the first condition while imposing the second. Finally forget the detector coordinates. Each retained system still has a representative that passes every local test, and weighted evaluations approximate all integral polynomials in the original Hecke variables and the scaled powers. On weights outside the specified auxiliary primes the construction keeps the original weights. This supplies the simultaneous approximation used in the trace limit, under the automorphic family hypotheses and the local cuspidality criterion stated below.

Why do all the original Hecke coordinates lie in one finite extension?

The trace limit needs completeness of its coefficient field. Having each eigenvalue in some finite extension of ℚℓ would allow the degrees to grow with the operator. For a family of rational Hecke operators at the chosen weight and level we can bound the degree of every finite list at once.

Choose a nonzero fixed eigenform for the original system. The polynomial construction below realizes every field conjugate of its eigenvalue system on a nonzero form, all in one finite-dimensional space M. Rationality of the operators is used here so that applying an automorphism of ℂ carries an eigenvalue to its conjugate for the very same operator. The finite adelic values determine the form, so a nonzero such value still detects each conjugate. Distinct joint systems give linearly independent eigenvectors. Indeed, in a finite relation fix one system χ. For each other system ψ choose a coordinate j at which they differ and apply the factor Tj − ψjI. The product kills every other vector and multiplies the chosen one by a nonzero scalar. Thus χ has zero coefficient in the relation. The full tuple of eigenvalues has at most dim M different conjugates.

Transport the tuple back by ι to the algebraic closure of ℚℓ. For every finite set A of coordinates let LA be the field they generate over ℚℓ. It has [LA : ℚℓ] embeddings into the algebraic closure. One can count them by adjoining the generators in succession: each minimal polynomial in characteristic zero has exactly its degree many distinct roots. Each embedding extends to an automorphism of the algebraic closure, hence, through ι, to a field automorphism of ℂ. Distinct embeddings differ on at least one of the generating coordinates, and therefore yield different full conjugate tuples. Milne, Fields and Galois Theory, Proposition 7.4.

[LA : ℚℓ] ≤ # conjugate tuples ≤ dim M.

These positive integer degrees have a maximum. Take a finite set attaining it and adjoin any further coordinate. The resulting field contains the previous one and has the same degree, so the coordinate was already there. This gives a single finite extension, complete in its ℓ-adic norm, for all the original rational Hecke coordinates. For instance, in ℚ₅(√2) the infinite sequence 1+n√2, n ≥ 0, has two conjugate tuples. Already the coordinate at n=1 generates degree two, since 2 is a nonsquare modulo 5. The same upper bound of two works for every finite set of indices. A. Sutherland, 18.785, Proposition 10.6, completeness in finite dimension.
