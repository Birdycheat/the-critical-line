What does a single complex fibre determine about its marked lattice?

Let A → X be a complex abelian scheme of relative dimension g, with an action of an order 𝒪, a relatively ample line bundle and sections η(a) killed by N > 0. Fix a complex representation ρ of 𝒪 on ℂg. A marked period description at y ∈ X(ℂ) consists of an integral 𝒪-lattice L with alternating form E, marking representatives t(a) ∈ L, a period embedding P ∶ L → ℂg of full lattice image, and a holomorphic group homomorphism e, with invertible derivative at zero, onto Ay(ℂ) with kernel P(L). The period and exponential intertwine the order actions. The imaginary part of a positive Hermitian form pulls back to E. Compatibility with the given line bundle means that, at every positive division level, its Weil pairing is the Riemann-form pairing computed below. The exponential sends P(t(a))/N to η(a)y.

Fibre comparison. For two such descriptions of the same polarized, marked fibre, there is a marked isometry w ∶ L → L′ and an invertible complex-linear map U with P′ ∘ w = U ∘ P and e′ ∘ U = e. Indeed the two exponentials are coverings with simply connected source. Lift the identity of the fibre, choosing the lift taking zero to zero. It is holomorphic since the exponential admits local holomorphic inverses. Its defect of additivity varies continuously in the discrete kernel and vanishes at the origin. Thus the lift is additive and equals its complex derivative at zero. The lift in the opposite direction is its inverse by uniqueness. Its restriction to the kernels gives w. Lifting each order endomorphism through the same coverings proves 𝒪-linearity. Linear lifts of homomorphisms of complex tori.

For every integer k > 0, the division points represented by P(x)/k and P(z)/k also have the representatives P′(wx)/k and P′(wz)/k. Their Weil pairing for the given line bundle has a unique scalar value. With the Hermitian form linear in its first variable, the commutator formula reads

exp(−2πi E(x, z)/k) = exp(−2πi E′(wx, wz)/k).

The integer difference Δ of the two form values is divisible by every k. Taking k = |Δ| + 1 gives Δ = 0. For the marking, the single level N suffices. Equality of the sections implies that P′(w(t(a)) − t′(a))/N lies in P′(L′). Injectivity of P′ gives wt(a) ≡ t′(a) modulo NL′. Conversely, a marked isometry transports a description to the same fibre by taking P′ = P ∘ w−1 and keeping e. Under a simultaneous change of complex coordinates by U the formulas become P′ = U ∘ P ∘ w−1 and e′ = e ∘ U−1, where the new coordinates carry the transported order action. For the fixed representation ρ such a change commutes with ρ, as one sees on the real span of the periods.

The commutator sign and an elliptic period lattice

In an Appell–Humbert frame put Fa(z) = exp(−πH(z, a)) for a period vector a. In comparing translations by a/k and b/k, the ratio of Fa(z)Fb(z + a/k) to Fb(z)Fa(z + b/k) has exponent −π(H(a,b) − H(b,a))/k. Hermitian symmetry gives −2πi Im H(a,b)/k. The common fibre frames and constant factors cancel in this quotient.

Take Pτ(a, b) = a + bτ for Im τ > 0. With H(u, v) = u conj(v)/Im τ, the imaginary part is E((a, b), (c, d)) = bc − ad. In particular E((1,0),(0,1)) = −1 and the ordered division points 1/k, τ/k have pairing exp(2πi/k). Reversing them inverts this root of unity. As τ varies, the abstract form and the residues of the marked basis stay fixed.

Why do finitely many marked lattices suffice?

Suppose X is smooth of relative dimension d over ℂ and quasi-compact. Fix which marked lattices are admissible. Assume that every y ∈ X(ℂ) has an admissible period description, and that every admissible lattice with a compatible period P occurs with that period. Then there are finitely many marked isometry classes of admissible lattices admitting a period. A representative indexes a sheet on which the period can vary.

Proof. For each y, let Cy be the set of admissible marked lattices admitting a description at y. Choose one such lattice. Fibre comparison shows that every other member is marked isometric to it, and transport realizes every admissible member of that isometry class at y. Thus Cy is exactly that class restricted to the admissible lattices.

To prove local constancy, choose one marked lattice at the centre of a chart. The local lifting theorem continues its periods on a smaller ball about that point. The lattice, its order action, its form and its marking remain fixed. Smooth local charts therefore make y ↦ Cy locally constant in the complex topology. For any finite affine cover, the number of values is bounded by the numbers of irreducible components of its affine opens. Write the cover as X = ⋃j Spec Rj.

#{Cy ∣ y ∈ X(ℂ)} ≤ ∑j # Min(Rj).

Quasi-compactness gives the finite affine cover. Each Rj is a finitely generated complex algebra and has finitely many minimal primes. Every complex point on this affine factors through a quotient by at least one of those primes, since its evaluation kernel contains a minimal prime. The complex points of an integral affine variety are connected. Indeed its nonsingular locus is a connected nonsingular variety in the Zariski topology, hence connected in the complex topology, and this dense open is also dense for the complex topology. Its closure is still connected. Each minimal prime thus contributes at most one value of the locally constant class, giving the bound. See finiteness of the minimal primes and connectedness of the complex points of an integral variety.

Choose one marked lattice from each distinct value. Realization of every admissible period proves that every admissible lattice admitting a period occurs in this list up to isometry. An isometry between two representatives would identify their two class sets, so their labels would coincide. Every period on a representative lattice has the same discrete label.

What the local lifting theorem supplies

Fix a marked period description (P₀, e₀) at the centre b₀ of a holomorphic family. Over a locally finite-type complex base with sections killed by N > 0, the lifting theorem gives descriptions on the same abstract lattice throughout a smaller neighbourhood. It prescribes Pb₀ = P₀. The construction also matches the given exponential over the centre.

In centred fibre coordinates c, the preliminary exponential Fb has derivative equal to the identity at zero. Put T = d(c ∘ e₀)₀, an isomorphism, and set eb(z) = Fb(Tz). At the centre take the local inverse branch u = Fb₀−1 ∘ e₀ fixing zero. Its defect of additivity lies in the discrete kernel and tends to zero, so it vanishes near zero. There u(z) = 2nu(2−nz) tends, as n grows, to du₀(z) = Tz. The exponentials now agree near zero and hence everywhere on the central fibre by subdividing any z by a power of two. The corrected exponential is jointly holomorphic in (b, z).

Lift the identity section starting at the periods of an integral basis at the centre. The compact-cube exhaustion and uniform-frame argument in the kernel-exhaustion proof show that these holomorphic periods exhaust each nearby kernel. For example, the exponential z ↦ [2z] on ℂ/(ℤ + τℤ) has periods ½ and τ/2, so matching it at τ = i still allows the second period to vary. On a connected ball equality of torsion sections and their Weil values persists. A holomorphic change of coordinates equal to the identity at the centre then restores the prescribed order action ρ.

Why the continued periods generate the whole nearby kernel

Continue a basis of the central period lattice by locally lifting the identity section through the relative exponential. Write Tb for the real matrix with these continued periods as columns and Kb for the kernel. At the centre b₀ the columns are a real basis and Kb₀ = Tb₀ℤ2g. The determinant stays nonzero nearby. Thus the continued columns generate a full lattice contained in Kb. Equality is the point to prove.

The local inverse of the relative exponential near the identity gives a single ball about zero containing no nonzero kernel vector on nearby fibres. The kernel relation is closed under limits there, since the exponential is continuous and the identity section is closed. If equality failed arbitrarily near the centre, choose bₖ → b₀ and a missed vector in each Kbₖ. Pass to real coordinates using Tbₖ and subtract the integer parts. The reduced vector Tbₖaₖ is still a missed kernel vector with aₖ ∈ [0,1)2g.

A subsequence converges in the closed cube to a. Closedness gives Tb₀a ∈ Kb₀, so the central basis gives a ∈ ℤ2g. A coordinate of a can equal one. Subtract Tbₖa on each nearby fibre. The differences remain missed kernel vectors but now converge to zero, contradicting the common ball. This proves equality after shrinking the neighbourhood.

Where does full level enter the period description of a point?

For a fine period family there is a third hypothesis, separation. If two period descriptions are intertwined by a marked isometry and an invertible complex-linear map, the points they describe are equal. With this hypothesis choose, for each admissible period on each representative lattice, a point realizing it. Any other realization of the same period gives the same point by separation. The resulting maps from the sheets cover X(ℂ) by the representative property. Their images are disjoint because comparison on one fibre identifies its marked lattice class. On a single sheet, two periods give the same point exactly when such an intertwining pair exists. This is the fibre criterion used in the classification by finite cosets and a positive line. There one chooses a period description of a given point, and in the reverse direction places the lattice constructed from a triple on a representative sheet. The size of the finite list plays no part in this point-set argument. The archimedean period calculation works with its lattice already fixed.

For the further holomorphic dependence of the chosen point and its exponential on the period, assume N ≥ 3 and that the labels t(a) cover L/NL on each admissible lattice. The extra rigidity concerns the exponential at a fixed period. The isometry relating two exponential maps then acts trivially on L/NL. It preserves the positive definite quadratic form q(x) = Re H(Px, Px), since q(x) = Im H(iPx, Px) and its complex lift preserves the imaginary part on the real span of the lattice. Each basis vector has finite orbit in a bounded lattice set, so the isometry has finite order. The torsion-freeness theorem for principal congruence subgroups now makes it the identity. The complex lift is the identity on the real span and the two exponentials agree. This uniqueness identifies locally transported exponential maps with the chosen ones in the holomorphic parameter theorem. At level 2 the automorphism −1 of an elliptic period lattice fixes every residue, and the exponential maps e(z) and e(−z) illustrate the ambiguity. The finite-class bound above already holds for every N > 0.
