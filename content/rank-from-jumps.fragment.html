How does a local filtration put a matrix in the tangent space of the global image?

We have a representation ρ on a space of dimension D and want one of dimension m. Fix the place over ℓ and the embedding with the separated filtration jumps. Write k₁,…,kD for the full list of jumps, with repetitions. Among these we know m distinct values whose disjoint nonempty subsets have different means. The first task is to exhibit these numbers as eigenvalues of a single matrix subject to the equations of the global image.

Let C be the completion of an algebraic closure of ℚℓ. After choosing the local coefficient model and restricting to the Galois group of a finite extension containing its coefficients, the de Rham comparison gives a basis of C-columns P. Write χ for the cyclotomic character, which describes the action on roots of unity of ℓ-power order, and put θ = χ−1. If g(P) denotes the action on each entry of the basis matrix, the comparison reads

ρ(g) g(P) = P diag(θ(g)k₁, …, θ(g)kD).

The inverse in θ fixes the sign convention. The cyclotomic line itself has its de Rham jump at −1. The Tate–Sen theorem implies that if an element a of C satisfies g(a) = θ(g)da throughout the restricted Galois group for an integer d ≠ 0, then a = 0. A nonzero cyclotomic power still has infinite image on inertia after any finite extension. Brinon and Conrad, Theorem 2.2.7, Example 6.3.1 and Proposition 6.3.2.

Take a polynomial f in the matrix entries that vanishes on the global image. We will prove that it vanishes at P diag(tk₁,…,tkD) P−1 for every t ∈ C×. Call this matrix μ(t). Since its diagonal powers multiply coordinate by coordinate, μ(tu) = μ(t)μ(u). Such an algebraic map from the multiplicative group is called a cocharacter. The polynomial q(Y) = f(Y P−1) vanishes at every local matrix ρ(γ)P. Give a matrix entry in column j the weight kj. Multiplying the columns by tkj multiplies a monomial of total weight d by td.

Why does each part of q of a single weight vanish separately? Take all polynomial relations on the points ρ(γ)P, restricting to the finite list of monomials in q. Their coefficient lists form a vector space. Applying g to each coefficient of weight d and multiplying the result by θ(g)d preserves this space. Indeed, evaluating this transformed relation at ρ(γ)P gives g applied to the old relation at ρ(γg)P, by the displayed identity and the choice of a group fixing the matrix coefficients of ρ.

Choose a nonzero relation with minimal support inside the support of q. Normalize one coefficient of weight b to 1. Apply the operation and divide by θ(g)b. Subtracting the original relation cancels its normalized coefficient, so minimality forces the difference to vanish. Each other nonzero coefficient a of weight d thus satisfies g(a) = θ(g)b−da. Tate–Sen forces d = b. Subtract a multiple of this single-weight relation from q to eliminate one coefficient and repeat. This writes q as a sum of single-weight relations. Each vanishes at P by taking γ = 1, and therefore still vanishes after the columns of P are multiplied by their respective powers of t. This proves f(μ(t)) = 0.

The Zariski closure of the image is obtained by imposing all its polynomial relations. The calculation puts the whole curve μ in that closure. To find its tangent at the identity, substitute t = 1 + ε with ε² = 0. This substitution is legitimate because f(μ(t)) is a Laurent polynomial vanishing on the infinite set C×. For every integer k, (1 + ε)k = 1 + kε, using (1 + ε)−1 = 1 − ε for negative powers. Hence μ(1 + ε) = I + εX, where X = P diag(k₁,…,kD) P−1. For every relation f, its first derivative at I vanishes in the direction X. This is the defining condition for the tangent space, also called the Lie algebra of the image closure.

Can the tangent matrix be chosen over the coefficient field?

The preceding matrix has entries in the complete field C. The global representation and its defining equations have coefficients in K = ℚ̄ℓ, the algebraic closure before completion. To work with the global image algebra over K, we need a tangent matrix over K with m lacunary eigenvalues. Treat the D² entries of the matrix Y and the m proposed eigenvalues z₁,…,zm as unknowns. Each tangent equation is linear in the entries of Y with coefficients in K. The equations det(ziI−Y) = 0 are polynomial as well.

For each pair of disjoint nonempty index sets A, B, unequal means amount to requiring that the following polynomial be nonzero. There are finitely many pairs. Multiply all the corresponding polynomials to form L, taking the empty product as 1.

LA,B(z) = |B| ∑i∈A zi − |A| ∑j∈B zj.

Adjoin one further unknown u with equation uL = 1. The known matrix over C, its chosen eigenvalues, and u = 1/L satisfy all the equations, so the ideal they generate is proper. Take a maximal ideal containing it. By Hilbert’s Nullstellensatz, the residue field of this polynomial algebra at a maximal ideal is finite over K. Since K is algebraically closed, the residue field is K itself. The images of the unknowns solve all the equations in K. In particular L is invertible there. Singleton pairs show that the new eigenvalues are distinct, and the remaining factors preserve all the unequal means. From here on, write X and e₁,…,em for this choice. The later argument only uses these relations on the ei. The Stacks Project, Hilbert Nullstellensatz, Theorem 10.34.1.

Why can the tangent matrix have only the prescribed eigenvalues?

For a matrix M, let vec(M) be the column of its entries in a fixed order. The assertion that some nonzero polynomial of degree at most m kills M says exactly that the following columns are dependent. A dependence relation supplies the coefficients of such a polynomial, and evaluating any annihilator gives the converse.

vec(I), vec(M), …, vec(Mm).

Dependence is equivalent to the vanishing of every minor of size m + 1 of this column matrix. Indeed, independent columns have full column rank, so row reduction selects m + 1 independent rows. Each minor is a fixed polynomial in the entries of M. At a dense set of Galois elements the given annihilator makes them all zero. The matrix entries vary continuously, so these same minors vanish everywhere on the image. In particular they belong to the polynomial relations defining its closure. This step allows the annihilator to vary with the Galois element.

We now show why the bound also holds for every tangent matrix Y. For a polynomial relation f define a new polynomial by taking the coefficient of s in f(M(I + sY)). At an image matrix M, the polynomial Z ↦ f(MZ) vanishes on the image because it is closed under multiplication. Tangency at I therefore kills that coefficient. Repeating this operation preserves the space of all relations. In the formal power series f(exp(sY)), the coefficient of sn is the value of the n-fold operation at I divided by n!. This follows by differentiating along the formal series, whose derivative is exp(sY)Y. Every coefficient vanishes. Characteristic zero permits the division and the argument requires only formal series.

Apply this to each minor. Over the Laurent series field, the columns for exp(sY) are dependent. Put Z(s) = (exp(sY) − I)/s. Substituting 1 + sT in an annihilator of exp(sY) gives one of the same degree for Z(s), since s is invertible in this field. All the corresponding minors vanish. But the entries of Z(s) are power series with constant term Y. Taking the constant term of each minor gives that minor at Y. Hence Y too admits an annihilator of degree at most m.

For our chosen X, an annihilator must vanish at its m distinct eigenvalues. Its monic minimal polynomial is therefore exactly ∏i(T−ei). Interpolating at those roots gives pi = ∏j≠i(X−ejI)/(ei−ej). At the roots its scalar polynomial takes value 1 at ei and 0 at the others. The identities ∑pi = I, pi² = pi and pipj = 0 for i ≠ j follow, since each difference of the corresponding polynomials vanishes at all the roots and hence is divisible by the minimal polynomial. These give m nonzero eigenspaces. Each can still have dimension greater than one.
