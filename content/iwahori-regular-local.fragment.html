Choosing the second place before the level

In the spectral trace test, a nonzero automorphic form already has a prescribed cuspidal type at a split place w0. Keep the same CM datum and quadratic-generator hypothesis. Given a finite set X of rational primes to avoid, choose an odd rational prime r outside X, different from the geometric prime p and away from the original level support. We may include the prime below w0 in X. The choice below gives one place from each conjugate pair above r. Fix these places before taking an admissible level N divisible by r whose principal subgroup stabilizes the chosen form.

Let E be the CM field, with complex conjugation indicated by a bar. The arithmetic data supply an integral element ω with ω + ω̄ = a, ωω̄ = b and Δ = a² − 4b < 0, where a, b are integers. Use the factorial argument of the geometric prime lemma with the quadratic polynomial of ω. Take an integer B exceeding 2, p, |Δ|, the members of X and the absolute norms of the primes in the original bad set. Include also the common arithmetic denominator described below. Put c = (B + |a| + 1)! and choose any prime divisor r of g = bc² − ac + 1. We have b ≥ 1 and bc > a, so g > 1. Every prime up to B divides c, so g is congruent to 1 modulo each of them. Thus r > B. The identity

(bc)² − a(bc) + b = b(bc² − ac + 1)

gives a root of Z² − aZ + b modulo r. Its second root is distinct because r does not divide Δ. No prime in the original bad set lies above r, since the norm of a prime above r is a positive power of r.

Fix one root ρ and let W consist of the places above r at which ω reduces to ρ. At every place above r the residue of ω is one of the two roots. Conjugation sends ω to a − ω, so it exchanges the two choices. In particular no such place is fixed by conjugation. The factorization of r in the ring of integers gives a finite nonempty set of places, and W selects precisely one from each pair. By the description of places over the real subfield, in the quadratic extension E/E+ these two places are the ones over the same real-subfield place. We may now fix w1 ∈ W for the regularity test.

Which arithmetic denominators enter the choice of B?

The rational pairing (x, z) ↦ TrE/ℚ(δxz) is nondegenerate. Here δ is the nonzero element defining the pairing. For x ≠ 0, evaluate at z = (δx)−1 to obtain [E : ℚ]. Thus every coordinate functional in an integral basis is represented by a rational vector. Clear their denominators simultaneously. Multiplying the resulting integer once more by the absolute norm of the prescribed nonzero correction ideal gives a positive integer e such that, after inverting e, the trace pairing is perfect and the prescribed correction element is a unit. Taking B ≥ e puts both conditions at r.

Why does every matrix in the double coset have distinct eigenvalues in the local field?

At any chosen place w ∈ W, the split factor is GLn(Ew). Let K denote this completion, or any nonarchimedean local field with valuation ring 𝒪, uniformizer π and normalized valuation v(π) = 1. Write I for the Iwahori subgroup of GLn(𝒪) whose reduction modulo π is upper triangular. Fix strictly decreasing integers b₀ > ⋯ > bn−1 and t = diag(πbi). Every matrix in ItI has one eigenvalue in K of each valuation bi.

Write such a matrix as x t y and put C = yx ∈ I. Then x−1(x t y)x = tC. Each principal minor of C is a unit because its reduction is the determinant of an upper triangular matrix with nonzero diagonal. In the characteristic polynomial χ(X) = ∑k=0n ckXn−k, the coefficient ck is (−1)k times the sum of principal minors of size k. Indeed, multilinearity of det(X id − tC) selects the scalar X in the complementary columns and leaves precisely the selected principal minor. The principal minor on the rows and columns indexed by J contributes π raised to ∑j∈J bj times a unit. The least valuation occurs only for the final k rows. Thus

v(ck) = bn−k + ⋯ + bn−1,   v(c₀) = 0.

To isolate the valuation bh at a fixed index 0 ≤ h < n, replace X by πbhZ. The order of the term indexed by k becomes v(ck) + (n − k)bh. For 0 ≤ k < n, the next order minus the current one is bn−k−1 − bh. The minimum is therefore attained at exactly two consecutive indices, n − h − 1 and n − h. Call this common minimum M. The polynomial π−Mχ(πbhZ) has integral coefficients even when bh or M is negative. Exactly the powers Zh+1 and Zh survive modulo π. Thus the reduction is Zh(A Z + B) with A, B nonzero in the residue field. At z = −B/A its derivative equals A zh ≠ 0. Since 𝒪 is a complete discrete valuation ring, Hensel’s simple-root lemma lifts z to an integral root with nonzero residue, hence to a unit. Multiplying by πbh gives the desired root in K. The n valuations are distinct, accounting for every root with multiplicity one. The Stacks Project, Lemmas 10.153.3 and 10.153.9 (lifting simple roots over complete local rings).

In dimension two over ℚ3, the choice t = diag(1, 3−1) gives valuations 0 and −m on ItmI for m ≥ 1. At m = 0 the identity matrix lies in I and has a repeated eigenvalue. This explains the positive-power hypothesis in the support assertion.

How does a Hecke product stay in a double coset with regular spectrum?

Keep the upper Iwahori subgroup I and the diagonal matrix t with strictly decreasing exponents bi. Write Xm = ItmI. The product of an element of X₁ and an element of Xm has an intervening factor h ∈ I. To place the product in Xm+1, factor h = u d l, with u upper unipotent integral, d diagonal with unit entries, and l lower unipotent with entries below the diagonal divisible by π. Elimination starting with the lower right entry gives this factorization. Each trailing principal minor is a unit since reduction modulo π is upper triangular and invertible, so every pivot used in the elimination is a unit. The entries below the diagonal reduce to zero throughout.

t h tm = (t u t−1) d tm+1 (t−ml tm).

For i < j the conjugation of the upper entry multiplies it by πbi−bj. For i > j the lower entry acquires πm(bj−bi). Both factors lie in I for all m ≥ 0. This proves X₁Xm ⊆ Xm+1. In an adelic convolution (f ∗ h)(g) ≠ 0 implies that f(y)h(y−1g) ≠ 0 for some y. Therefore a local restriction on both factors propagates to the product. In particular, starting with a test function supported locally inside I, iterated convolutions with a function supported locally inside X₁ have support inside Xm after m steps. At positive m, the diagonal exponents m bi are strictly decreasing and the root-valuation lemma applies. For the centrally translated test below, the matrix in this double coset is dwm prw(g), where dw is a nonzero scalar. The eigenvalue valuations of prw(g) are therefore m bi − m vw(dw). Their differences survive the translation.

For I ⊂ GL₂(ℚ3) and t = diag(1, 1/3), the indicator T₁ of ItI takes value 1 at t, while (T₁ ∗ T₁)(t) vanishes because the determinant has valuation −1 on ItI and −2 on It²I. The global argument needs the support inclusion. Cancellation in a complex-valued convolution can further reduce its support.

Why is the local test invertible on fixed vectors?

The support calculation restricts which elements enter the geometric trace. On the spectral side we also need an operator that preserves a nonzero vector space and acts invertibly on it. For a nonarchimedean local field K, normalize Haar measure by vol(I) = 1 and write Tg for the indicator of IgI in the complex Hecke algebra. For a diagonal t with weakly decreasing exponents we construct a Hecke function φ satisfying φ ∗ Tt = 1I.

For an adjacent transposition s, the possible changes of the intermediate subspace in a flag form a projective line over the residue field of size Q. If the initial and final flags coincide there are Q choices for a different intermediate flag. For two end flags differing only at that position there are Q − 1 choices different from both. The two steps leave every other subspace fixed, so these are all possible end flags. With vol(I) = 1, this gives

Ts ∗ Ts = Q 1I + (Q − 1)Ts.

Thus Q−1(Ts − (Q − 1)1I) is its inverse. The length-additive multiplication rule for permutation double cosets expresses the operator of a reduced word as the product of the simple operators. It follows that every permutation gives a unit. An element normalizing I has only one right coset in its double coset, so its inverse gives the inverse translation. Iwahori and Matsumoto, §3.1, Theorem 3.3 and Proposition 3.4(i).

Let tj have its first j diagonal entries equal to π and the others equal to 1. There is a cyclic permutation matrix wj (a power of the coordinate cycle) for which wjtj preserves the standard periodic lattice chain as a set, and therefore normalizes its stabilizer I. Multiplication by a normalizing element is ordinary translation of the double coset. Writing tj = wj−1(wjtj) expresses its indicator as the convolution of a permutation unit with a translation unit. Number the entries from 1 to n in this factorization. For a general tuple b₁ ≥ ⋯ ≥ bn, factor its diagonal as the scalar πbn times the product of tj to the nonnegative powers bj − bj+1. The multiplication rule for two diagonals with weakly decreasing exponents identifies the corresponding product of Hecke operators with the operator of the diagonal. Thus its action on the I-fixed vectors of any complex representation is invertible.

Why does multiplying two decreasing diagonals give coefficient one?

Write t = diag(πbi) and v = diag(πai) with both sequences weakly decreasing. The upper–diagonal–lower calculation gives (ItI)(IvI) ⊆ ItvI, using t for the upper factor and v for the lower factor. The convolution is bi-invariant, hence constant on this double coset. Its integral is vol(ItI) vol(IvI).

The cosets x t I with x ∈ I are indexed by I/(I ∩ tIt−1). Membership in the intersection imposes the extra divisibilities πbi−bj ∣ gij for i < j. Count the entries after reducing modulo a power of π larger in exponent than every gap. The lower entries and unit diagonal entries have the same choices in both groups, and each upper entry contributes a factor Qbi−bj to the index. Thus vol(ItI) = Q∑i<j(bi−bj). The exponents add for tv, so vol(ItvI) equals the product of the two volumes. The constant is one.

How does an inverse on fixed vectors give a left convolution inverse?

Take the left regular action on compactly supported functions on G/I. Its I-fixed vectors are precisely the bi-invariant Hecke functions. Writing T for the indicator of the diagonal double coset, its action here is left convolution. Surjectivity gives φ with T ∗ φ = 1I. Then T ∗ (φ ∗ T) = T = T ∗ 1I. Injectivity gives φ ∗ T = 1I, the order used on the type in the global argument.
