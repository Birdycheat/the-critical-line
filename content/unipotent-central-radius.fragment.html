Why is the central error quadratic in the size of the short lattice?

Put j = dimE W and S = Λ ∩ W⊥. A central matrix Z is skew-adjoint and kills W⊥. It has image in W since ⟨Zv,w⟩ = −⟨v,Zw⟩ vanishes for every w ∈ W⊥. The rectangular block to round is therefore a map from V/W⊥ to W. Both have rank j. We will impose the skew-adjoint condition after rounding.

The source and target scales. The largest size in R is at most h. Self-duality of Λ and saturation identify Λ/S with the Hermitian dual of R with its induced metric. To see why this reverses the size, consider a saturated sublattice of Λ/S. Its dual is a quotient of R of the same rank, obtained by restricting the pairing. Their normalized determinant sizes multiply to one. The quotient has size at most h by the definition of the largest size of R, so the source sublattice has size at least 1/h. Passing to a finite-index sublattice can only increase that size. The ratio used in the matrix covering bound is thus h/(1/h) = h².

Apply that bound to Z/2. It gives a rational Φ killing S, sending Λ into R, and satisfying ‖Z/2 − Φ‖x ≤ j²√f d h²/2. Rational spanning extends the kernel condition to W⊥. The target condition extends to the image in W.

How can the rounded map be made skew-adjoint and integral?

Write Φ† for the adjoint with respect to the original Hermitian form. Take Z₀ = Φ − Φ†. Its adjoint is −Z₀. The kernel and image conditions on Φ give the same two conditions on Φ† by taking orthogonal complements. To check integrality, let v,w ∈ Λ. The pairing of Φ†v with w is integral because it equals the pairing of v with Φw ∈ Λ. Self-duality gives Φ†v ∈ Λ. Thus Z₀ belongs to the integral central lattice.

In each adapted frame the adjoint conjugate-transposes a matrix and multiplies its rows and columns by signs. This preserves the sum of squared absolute entries. Writing D = Z/2 − Φ, the skew-adjointness of Z gives Z − Z₀ = D − D†. The triangle inequality costs a factor of two. Isotropy gives j ≤ n/2, and the selected flag gap gives h² ≤ u/4. Consequently

‖Z − Z₀‖x ≤ j² √f d h² ≤ n² √f d u/16 = r₂.

The correction created by the first rounding has central value C′. Applying this estimate to C′/g gives the error g r₂ in the factorization below.

For a Gaussian hyperbolic plane, write E₁₂ for the matrix with a single 1 in its upper-right corner. For Z = (5i/4)E₁₂ the nearest Gaussian multiple to its half is Φ = iE₁₂. Subtracting the adjoint gives 2iE₁₂, at distance 3/4 in the ordinary matrix norm. The integral central matrix iE₁₂ is only 1/4 away. The proof needs a bound on the distance to its constructed point.
