Which Casimir eigenvalue does the stopping type carry?

The descent inside the spectral subspace has found a finite-dimensional compact type V among the smooth vectors of the restricted representation τ. Its stopping weight L′ and the parameter μ′ constructed from it will be written L and μ throughout this identification. Write H = U(r,s), n = r+s and K = U(r) × U(s). Order the coordinates by decreasing parameter. The first derivative layer is the span of V and all EabV for coordinates of opposite signs. The stopping condition says that this layer contains no nonzero compact highest vector of weight L−(ea−eb) for any such pair a < b.

Write ρc and ρn for the half-sums of compact and noncompact positive roots, and ρ = ρc+ρn for the full half-sum. The candidate parameter is μ = L−ρn+ρc. Casimir of the stopping type. If μ is strictly decreasing and the stopping condition holds, every v ∈ V satisfies

Ωv = (‖μ‖² − ‖ρ‖²)v.

To see how the absence of a lower weight determines a scalar, introduce an exterior algebra S with a generator for each pair consisting of a positive coordinate and a negative coordinate. Exterior multiplication adds a generator and contraction removes it. The spinor highest-weight lemma for this exterior tensor supplies a nonzero tensor x ∈ V ⊗ S of compact highest weight β = μ−ρc+θ for the diagonal compact action. Here θ has value s/2 on positive coordinates and −r/2 on negative ones. The two halves of the Dirac operator pair one noncompact derivative with exterior multiplication or contraction. Both preserve the diagonal compact highest-weight equations. On the representation factor they change the sum of the positive-coordinate weights by −1 and +1 respectively.

The first-layer weight lemma used at this point is as follows. At diagonal highest weight β, a nonzero tensor in the first layer with changed positive-coordinate sum would supply a compact highest vector of weight L−(ea−eb) for opposite signs and a < b. Indeed, expand the tensor in the exterior basis and choose a nonzero coefficient whose exterior weight has least dot product with μ. The compact raising equations force this coefficient to be highest because an additional term would come from a strictly smaller dot product. The first layer allows the original weight or a change by one noncompact root. The changed positive-coordinate sum excludes the original weight. An upward change by ea−eb with a < b would put the exterior weight at θ−ρn−(ea−eb). Its dot product with μ would be smaller than the minimum ⟨θ−ρn,μ⟩. To find that minimum, sum the negative values μi−μa over positive coordinates i and negative coordinates a. These are precisely the exterior generators to include in a subset with least dot product. The surviving possibility is exactly the forbidden lowering. Applying this lemma to each half gives Dx = 0.

In this exterior model the Dirac square acts on the tensor of highest weight β by 2D²x = −(Ω ⊗ 1)x + (‖β+ρc‖²−‖ρc‖²−2⟨θ,β⟩)x. This is the compact Casimir minus the full Casimir with the displayed correction for the exterior action. Each sign block of ρc sums to zero, so ⟨θ,ρc⟩ = 0. Ordering the positive coordinates first and the negative coordinates next gives the vector ρc+θ as a permutation of ρ, so ‖ρ‖² = ‖ρc‖²+‖θ‖². Substitution gives (Ω ⊗ 1)x = (‖μ‖²−‖ρ‖²)x. At least one exterior coordinate of x is nonzero, so at least one vector of V has this eigenvalue. Its eigenspace inside V is compact-stable because Ω commutes with every compact derivative. Irreducibility of V makes that eigenspace all of V. The factor 2 and the term involving θ come from the exterior-action convention in this calculation. For the twisted Dirac square and its specialization after the weight shift, see R. Parthasarathy, Proposition 3.1 and Proposition 3.2.

For U(1,1), take signs (+,−) and μ = (41/2,−39/2). Here ρc = 0 and θ = ρn = ρ = (1/2,−1/2), so L = β = (21,−20). The compact scalar in the tensor is 21²+20² = 841. The linear correction is 2⟨θ,β⟩ = 41. The resulting 800 agrees with (41²+39²−2)/4. This checks the scalar at a stopping type of these weights. The root descent supplies the stopping condition in the occurrence proof.

Why do the two compact types have matching coefficients on the whole group?

Choose any discrete-series model σ at the regular stopping parameter μ, using the existence just proved. Its lowest compact type W has the same highest weight L. The discrete-series parameter theorem gives the Casimir scalar ‖μ‖²−‖ρ‖² on its smooth vectors. On the restricted ambient representation τ the stopping-type calculation gives the same scalar on V. The highest-weight classification on the two compact unitary factors gives a compact intertwiner from V onto W. Its adjoint times itself is a positive scalar by irreducibility, so it can be rescaled to a unitary map φ. Hongyu He, signed Harish-Chandra parameters · P. Etingof, highest weights for the general linear group, §27.

Matching the compact types has so far determined their inner products only at compact group elements. The parameter gap now does additional work. The orthogonality lemma for a separated lowest type has the following hypotheses. In a unitary representation take a smooth, compact-stable irreducible type of weight μ+ρn−ρc on which Ω has the prescribed scalar. Require every gap across signs to exceed nrs+1. If a compact intertwiner from this type into the Hilbert space has image orthogonal to the type, its image is orthogonal to every group translate of the type. The preceding descent yields gaps exceeding n³+2n+1 for μ, which suffices. The finite-layer estimate and the passage to translated orthogonality below prove the lemma from the one-position and analytic estimates.

Apply it to the direct sum τ ⊕ σ. Inside that sum take the diagonal compact type of vectors (v,φv). It has scalar ‖μ‖²−‖ρ‖² because both components do. The map (u,φu) ↦ (u,−φu) commutes with the compact action and has image orthogonal to the diagonal, since φ preserves inner products. The lemma gives orthogonality to (τ(g)v,σ(g)φv) for every g ∈ H. Taking the difference of the two component pairings gives

⟨u,τ(g)v⟩ = ⟨φu,σ(g)φv⟩   (u,v ∈ V).

At the identity this says that φ preserves inner products. At arbitrary g it describes how far translates of the compact type point in each of its original directions. Those values determine the lengths of finite sums of translates, which is what allows the compact map to extend to the required Hilbert spaces.

How do equal coefficients put the smaller representation inside the restriction?

Let Cτ be the closed span of vectors τ(g)v with v ∈ V and g ∈ H. Define Cσ in the same way using φV = W. For a finite list of pairs (gi,vi), let xi = τ(gi)vi and yi = σ(gi)φvi. By unitarity the inner product of xi and xj is the coefficient of gi⁻¹gj on vi, vj. The coefficient identity gives the same entry for the two y vectors. Write M for this common matrix of inner products. Then

‖∑ aixi‖² = a*Ma = ‖∑ aiyi‖².

A relation among the x vectors has squared norm zero, hence gives a relation among the y vectors. Subtracting two presentations shows that sending each x to its matching y defines a linear map on the span. The same identity makes it an isometry. It extends by completion to U from Cτ to Cσ. Its image is complete and contains the dense span of the y vectors, so it is onto. Left multiplication by h sends every label (g,v) to (hg,v) on both sides. Thus U intertwines on the spans and, by continuity, on their closures. This norm argument is the same one used to recover a cyclic representation from its coefficients. K. Kremnizer, Gelfand–Naimark–Segal construction, Theorem 4.1.

On the model side, Cσ is closed, invariant and contains the nonzero space W. Irreducibility of σ makes it its entire Hilbert space. On the ambient side, Cτ is precisely the closure generated from V by derivative words. One inclusion uses the analytic-vector invariance theorem. For these compact-finite smooth data with fixed Casimir, the derivative closure is group-invariant and contains V. For the reverse inclusion, a smooth vector in a closed invariant subspace has every real derivative in that subspace by difference quotients. Complex linearity and repetition put every derivative word on V in Cτ.

j = inclusion ∘ U⁻¹,   jσ(h) = τ(h)j.

This is the direction needed in branching. The source of j is the whole discrete series. Its image is a closed copy of that series in the restricted ambient space. The construction works for each chosen model of μ. The coefficient test can now start with this map.

Three generators of a plane. In a two-dimensional Euclidean model let y₁, y₂, y₃ be unit vectors at angles 0°, 120° and 240°. In ℝ³ take x₁ = (2,−1,−1)/√6 and its two cyclic permutations in order. Each family has diagonal inner products 1 and off-diagonal inner products −1/2. The relation with all three coefficients equal to 1 has zero norm in both families. Every permutation of the three labels acts by an isometry on each plane. The map j sends yi to xi and intertwines those permutations. In the ambient ℝ³ its image is exactly the plane of coordinate sum zero. This example isolates the role of relations, the direction of the embedding and its possible proper image.
