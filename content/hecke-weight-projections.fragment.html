How can one decompose every member of the family at once?

Fix a target dominant weight ν in the weight-variation theorem. For each pair of matrices y1, y2 ∈ GLa(ℂ) and each vector v of the original weight space W, the two-matrix family gives an automorphic function B(y1, y2, v). Its dependence on v is linear. All these functions together span a finite-dimensional complex space S. We need one list of irreducible representations valid for every choice of both matrices and of v.

Finite decomposition theorem. On the definite unitary group attached to a CM extension, every finite-dimensional space S of scalar automorphic forms lies in a finite direct sum of irreducible automorphic subrepresentations of the right translation action. The forms are smooth at the finite places and each spans a finite-dimensional space under translations at infinity. The representations in the sum may themselves have infinite dimension.

Here is where admissibility enters. The group at infinity is compact, so the finite-dimensional span of the archimedean translates of a form splits into irreducible types by taking invariant orthogonal complements. Fix one such type A. Its multiplicity space consists of linear maps from A to automorphic functions intertwining the action at infinity. Right translation at finite places acts on this space. Integrating the pointwise Hermitian pairing over the compact adelic quotient gives the positive, translation-invariant Petersson inner product, which induces an inner product on this multiplicity space. At each compact open finite level J its fixed space has finite dimension. Indeed, an intertwiner at this level is determined by one functional on A at each of the finitely many adelic class representatives. This finiteness at fixed type and level is the admissibility property used below. Finite class number and evaluation at representatives.

To see why each vector in the multiplicity space has a finite decomposition, take a vector fixed by J and the representation C generated by its translates. Every invariant subspace N splits off orthogonally in C. For a given vector, choose a compact open stabilizer H and project inside the finite-dimensional fixed space CH onto NH. The remainder is orthogonal to all of N, since pairing an H-fixed vector with n gives the same result as pairing with the average of n over H. This average is a finite sum because the action is smooth and H is compact. If C splits into two nonzero summands, the original generating vector has a nonzero J-fixed projection generating each summand. Both have strictly smaller J-fixed dimension. Induction on this integer gives the finite decomposition.

Pairing each irreducible constituent of the multiplicity space with its archimedean type gives an irreducible representation of the full adelic group. Indeed, the matrix operators of the irreducible finite-dimensional type span its endomorphisms by Burnside’s theorem. Acting by its matrix units on the tensor product isolates each coefficient, so a subrepresentation is the type tensored with an invariant subspace of the multiplicity constituent. Evaluation of the intertwiner is a nonzero equivariant map from this irreducible tensor product to automorphic functions, hence it is injective. Take the union of the finite lists for a basis of S. If an irreducible space in the list meets the sum of its predecessors nontrivially, their intersection is invariant and contains that entire space. Removing such repetitions gives the desired direct sum. P. Etingof et al., Introduction to Representation Theory, Theorem 2.5(i).

For each constituent U let PU take the U-coordinate in this sum. Since every summand is invariant, taking this coordinate commutes with every right translation on the sum. Thus the projected families satisfy the following identity at every argument.

BU(y1, y2, v) = PU(B(y1, y2, v)),   B = ∑U BU.

In particular, the finite level still fixes each value. At the chosen embedding τ let e(x) denote the matrix of an archimedean element x, and let ρ be its action on W. Right translation by x takes a family member to the member with arguments (e(x)y1, e(x)y2, ρ(x)v). Multiplying only y1 on the right by an upper triangular matrix b multiplies the member by χν(b), the product of its diagonal entries raised to the corresponding powers νi. Linearity and commutation with translations preserve both identities.

Projection also preserves the bounds on the exponents of a circle parameter. Indeed, point evaluations F ↦ F(g) span the dual of S. Otherwise a nonzero member of S would vanish at every g. Thus F ↦ (PUF)(g) is a finite linear combination of these evaluations. If each evaluated circle function is a sum of powers zm in a given range, the projected function uses the same range. We now keep the components on which the family has a nonzero value.

Why are the highest-weight partial sums bounded on both sides?

Fix a nonzero projected family and write it as F. Its values lie in one automorphic constituent U. Let κ be its highest weight at τ, and write sk(κ) for the sum of its first k entries, with s0(κ) = 0. Vary the first k diagonal entries together using t(z) = diag(z, …, z, 1, …, 1), where |z| = 1. Let x(z) be its lift at the chosen archimedean factor. The character χν(t(z)) is zsk(ν).

Partial-sum estimate. For every k between zero and the rank, the degree bound d chosen in constructing the family satisfies

sk(ν) − d ≤ sk(κ) ≤ sk(ν) + d.

The construction gives two different circle functions. After evaluation at any adelic point, the first has exponents at most sk(ν) + d, and the second has exponents at least −d.

F(t(z)y1, t(z)y2, ρ(x(z))v),   F(1, t(z)y2, ρ(x(z))v).

In the first function the weight coefficient contributes at most sk(ν), while the polynomial in the second matrix and the original weight space contribute at most d together. In the second function the weight coefficient is constant in z. The polynomial has only nonnegative powers and the original weight space has exponents bounded below by −d. These are exactly the two bounds preserved by the finite evaluations above.

To read the weight κ, choose a nonzero value of F and a projection p from U to a copy of the irreducible weight module Vκ that detects this value. The weight detection lemma supplies this projection. The other adelic factors commute with the chosen compact unitary factor, so their translates of one copy of Vκ span a sum of copies of the same type. By irreducibility they span U. The map p intertwines translations by that unitary factor. For any functional L on Vκ, the scalar functional Lp on the finite-dimensional space of family values obeys the same circle bounds by the evaluation argument.

For the upper bound, the vectors pF(y1, y2, v) span Vκ. Their span is nonzero and stable under the compact unitary group by the joint transformation law. The upper bound on every circle coefficient therefore holds for the highest-weight vector itself. Choose a functional nonzero on that vector. Its coefficient is a nonzero multiple of zsk(κ), which gives the upper inequality.

For the lower bound we first find a nonzero value with y1 = 1. Start at any nonzero value with first matrix y1 = ub, where u is unitary and b upper triangular. Gram–Schmidt on its columns gives this factorization. Removing b divides by the nonzero character χν(b). Translating by a lift of u−1 leaves a nonzero value F(1, y, v′). Choose p to detect this particular value and write w = pF(1, y, v′). For the weight action πκ, the character law on the first argument gives

πκ(t(z))w = zsk(ν) pF(1, t(z)y, ρ(x(z))v′).

Every scalar coefficient on the right has exponents at least sk(ν) − d. On the left every weight of Vκ is obtained from κ by subtracting positive roots. Subtracting a root moves a unit from an earlier entry to a later one, so the first k entries can only lose from their sum. Thus every coefficient on the left has exponents at most sk(κ), even for this particular w. Choose L with L(w) ≠ 0. Its coefficient is nonzero at z = 1. Its two exponent ranges must intersect, since a Laurent polynomial vanishing on the circle is zero. This is the circle test on a single nonzero weight-module vector, and it proves the lower inequality. P. Etingof, Highest Weight Modules and Verma Modules, Corollary 8.7 and Proposition 8.8.

Subtracting consecutive partial sums bounds the error of a single entry by 2d. The two errors can have opposite signs. For the dominant integer weights ν = (4, 1) and κ = (3, 3), the partial sums change from (4, 5) to (3, 6). With d = 1 their changes are −1 and +1, while the second weight entry changes by 2. This example shows exactly what the two partial-sum inequalities alone imply.
