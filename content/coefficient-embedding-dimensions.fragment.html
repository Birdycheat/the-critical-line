Which part of the period module does one coefficient embedding select?

The complex space of periods uses two scalar actions. The coefficient field L acts on the representation. The maximal unramified extension F of k = ℚℓ acts on the periods. Both act on D = Dpst(V), and their actions commute and agree on k. Here V is an L-representation of the absolute Galois group of a finite extension of k. Assume it is potentially semistable. Its period module is then finite-dimensional over F. We shall compute the dimension of the quotient obtained from a single embedding of L.

Work first in an algebraic closure Ω of k containing F. For embeddings s ∶ L → Ω and τ ∶ F → Ω over k, form W(s, τ) from Ω ⊗L,s D by imposing [z ⊗ cx] = [zτ(c) ⊗ x] for c ∈ F. Write [x] for [1 ⊗ x]. Its Ω-linear dual consists precisely of the additive functions in

H(s, τ) = {f ∶ D → Ω ∣ f(ax) = s(a)f(x),   f(cx) = τ(c)f(x)   (a ∈ L, c ∈ F)}.

Indeed, a functional λ gives f(x) = λ([x]). Conversely the rule [z ⊗ x] ↦ zf(x) respects the tensor product by the first scalar law and the quotient relation by the second. These two constructions are inverse. An F-basis of D gives a spanning family of classes [x], so the quotient is finite-dimensional and has the same dimension as its dual. With τ the inclusion of F, write d(s) for that dimension. The fixed field isomorphism ι ∶ Ω → ℂ transports this calculation to the complex Weil space when s is the given inclusion of L.

What does summing over all embeddings measure?

Fix τ to be the inclusion of F in Ω. On the space M of F-linear maps from D to Ω, let a ∈ L act by (Taf)(x) = f(ax). The two scalar actions on D commute, so Ta sends M to itself. Evaluating on an F-basis of D identifies M with one copy of Ω for every basis vector. Thus dimΩ M = dimF D.

Choose a primitive element α of the finite separable extension L/k. Its minimal polynomial P has distinct roots s(α), one for every embedding s. Since P(Tα) = 0, its eigenspaces split M as a direct sum. Indeed, the interpolation polynomial that is 1 at a chosen root and 0 at all other roots gives the projector onto that eigenspace when evaluated at Tα. These projectors sum to the identity. Every a ∈ L is a polynomial in α. Hence on the s(α)-eigenspace, Ta acts by s(a), exactly the first law defining H(s, τ). The Stacks Project, Lemma 9.19.1 (primitive element).

M = ⨁s H(s, τ),   ∑s d(s) = dimF D.

For a quadratic example take L = ℚ3(i), i² = −1. In a split space over Ω an operator for i could be diag(i, i, i, −i). It satisfies P(T) = T² + I = 0 and its eigenspaces have dimensions 3 and 1. The sum formula gives 4. To deduce that each summand contributes half the total, we must produce an invertible map that exchanges the two eigenspaces.

Why do inertia and Frobenius force the dimensions to agree?

Every automorphism g of Ω over k sends an embedding s to gs. We must compare H(s, τ) with H(gs, τ) while keeping the unramified embedding τ fixed. If g belongs to inertia, it fixes F pointwise. Then f ↦ g ∘ f gives a bijection between those two spaces. It is semilinear over Ω, so it carries a basis to a basis.

Now let g lift arithmetic Frobenius σ of F/k. Postcomposition applies σ to the unramified scalars too. Period Frobenius φ is L-linear and satisfies φ(cx) = σ(c)φ(x) for c ∈ F. It is injective on periods, and the images of an F-basis are linearly independent because σ is an automorphism. In finite dimension they form a basis, so φ is bijective. This gives the compensating map

H(s, τ) → H(gs, τ),   f ↦ g ∘ f ∘ φ−1.

The inverse power matters. On an input cx, φ−1 contributes σ−1(c), and g returns it to c. On an input ax with a ∈ L, φ−1 commutes with a, and g changes the factor s(a) to (gs)(a). The inverse transport is h ↦ g−1 ∘ h ∘ φ. We have proved d(gs) = d(s) for inertia and for a Frobenius lift.

Can a calculation over a finite field detect the wrong Frobenius?

To test the scalar law alone, take the field with 27 elements and a root t of T³ − T − 1 over 𝔽3. The polynomial has no root in 𝔽3, so it is irreducible. Its Frobenius σ sends t to t³ = t + 1. In the transport formula, keeping φ−1 sends the scalar t to σ(σ−1(t)) = t. Omitting it leaves t + 1. Using φ instead gives σ²(t) = t + 2. The three different answers distinguish all three choices.

These elements suffice on the finite set of coefficient embeddings. Take the finite normal closure of L/k in Ω. In its Galois group the image of inertia and the image of one Frobenius lift generate the group. Indeed, the quotient by the image of inertia is finite cyclic, generated by Frobenius. The group acts transitively on the embeddings because an isomorphism between two conjugate copies of L extends to the normal closure. Hence d(s) is constant. In the quadratic example, Frobenius interchanges i and −i because it acts on the fourth roots of unity by cubing. The transport rules out the multiplicities 3 and 1 for a period module of this kind. R. Sharifi, Algebraic Number Theory, Propositions 6.4.8 and 6.4.10. J. S. Milne, Fields and Galois Theory, Proposition 7.4.

Why does one complex Weil space recover the original rank?

Let a = dimL V and e = [L ∶ k]. Potential semistability gives dimF D = dimk V. This is the full-period dimension condition on the underlying rational representation. The tower law makes its right side ea. There are exactly e embeddings of L into Ω, one for each root of the separable minimal polynomial. Their sum counts this rational dimension, and the transport between embeddings makes their contributions equal. For each s,

e d(s) = dimF D = ea,   dimℂ W = d(s₀) = a.

Here s₀ is the chosen inclusion of L and e is positive, so cancellation is valid. This proves the dimension assertion for every finite-dimensional potentially semistable model, including rank zero. The trace comparison applies it before and after extending the coefficient field. The map on complex Weil spaces is surjective and intertwines the two operators. Since both spaces have dimension a, the map is an isomorphism and the operators have the same characteristic polynomial.
