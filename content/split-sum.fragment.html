Summing the split local integral one lattice at a time

At a split place, the unitary group can be written as GLa over the local field. A matrix takes the standard lattice to a new lattice, and two matrices give the same lattice exactly when they differ on the right by its stabilizer K. Give K Haar volume one. The local integral is now a sum over lattices. We need to know both what each lattice contributes and whether this infinite sum converges at the complex parameter used by the lift. The split chart and paired parameters From stabilizer cosets to the Haar integral

Choose a uniformizer ϖ, an element of valuation one, and write q for the residue-field size. The elementary divisors of a lattice give a unique weakly decreasing tuple of integers d = (d₁,…,da). Choosing integral bases separately in the source and the target makes the matrix diagonal with entries ϖdᵢ. Let Sd be the finite set of lattices with that tuple. Two integers extracted from it enter the kernel. The signed sum D = ∑dᵢ gives the determinant valuation and the twist zD, with |z| = 1. The sum k = ∑|dᵢ| measures the total displacement. For d = (1,−1), the twist is one although the displacement is two. With X = q−(a/2+s), the spherical section gives zDXk on every lattice in the cell. Count the first two shells in rank two

Take residue-field size 3 and the trivial coefficient and twist. The two tuples of displacement one are (1,0) and (0,−1). In the first cell, the sublattices of index 3 correspond to the four lines in the two-dimensional residue space. Multiplication by ϖ identifies the second cell with the first. The coefficient of X is therefore 8.

At displacement two the tuples (1,1) and (−1,−1) give one lattice each. The other three are (2,0), (1,−1) and (0,−2), all equivalent by central scaling for the purpose of counting. A lattice of type (2,0) is the kernel of a surjection onto the quotient of the local integer ring by (ϖ²). There are 9² − 3² primitive pairs of coefficients for such a map. Two maps have the same kernel precisely when a unit of the quotient relates them, and there are 9 − 3 such units. Each of the three cells has 72/6 = 12 elements. The counting series begins 1 + 8X + 38X². For the constant coefficient 1 of the trivial representation, the two Satake parameters here are √3 and 1/√3. Thus the product below becomes (1 − X²)(1 − 3X²)/[(1 − 3X)²(1 − X)²]. Its coefficients bound the absolute sum at this one place. To obtain a nonzero norm for a step in the chain, the proof also needs the continued global value and the residual integral.

Why the split-place sum converges at the required parameter

The published unramified doubling input evaluates a zonal integral far to the right. Twisting the representation by the determinant valuation multiplies every Satake parameter β by z. Why is an identity near zero enough? Apply the same doubling input to the trivial representation. Its Hecke operator counts the lattices in each cell, so its series has coefficient |Sd|. Group the two series by the nonnegative displacement k. Only finitely many tuples have a fixed k. On a positive interval these grouped coefficients determine the Taylor coefficients of their respective rational products. Both products are holomorphic for |X| < q−(a−½). Indeed, if c(1) ≠ 0, the bounds on β and β−1 are qa/2, so each linear Euler term has argument of modulus less than one on that disk. When c(1) = 0 the entire expression weighted by c(1) is identically zero. The counting product has its own Satake bound. Its Taylor series at |X| converges and has nonnegative coefficients. For each individual lattice in Sd, the absolute value of its contribution is at most C|X|k. Summing these bounds over the cell gives C|Sd||X|k. The convergent counting series therefore justifies summing the original complex terms lattice by lattice.
