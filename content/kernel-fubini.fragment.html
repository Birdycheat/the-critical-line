Why can we integrate the paired kernels in either order?

Let X = [G] be the source quotient and Y = [H] the target quotient, with their quotient Haar measures μ and ν. Each is the space of adelic points modulo rational changes of basis. Write Ki(x,y) for the theta kernel built from Φi. At a fixed y, the lift Θi(y) integrates Ki(x,y)ui(x) over X. Pairing two lifts now integrates over Y. To use the doubling identity we need the reverse order, pairing the two kernels on Y before integrating over the two source variables. What keeps the new integral finite?

Bound one kernel at a time. Take the same polynomial Φ on both sides of the doubled kernel. At (g,g), the doubling identity identifies |K(g,h)|² with the absolute value of that doubled kernel, because the determinant character has modulus one. The Siegel–Weil theorem on these data supplies integrability in h. Its regular Eisenstein value bounds the absolute value of the normalized target mean by a power of the doubled height. The height at (g,g) is at most a constant times a power of the height at g. Multiplying the mean by ν(Y) therefore gives a polynomial bound for ∫Y |K(g,y)|² dν(y). This step uses the rank and Witt-index assumptions of the global formula. By rational invariance we can take the height H(x) on the quotient, absorbing a constant in the bound. For each kernel choose ci ≥ 0 and an integer ai ≥ 0 such that its squared slice norm is at most ciH(x)aᵢ. Then Ni(x) = 1 + ciH(x)aᵢ bounds the slice norm itself. Rapid decay of the source cusp form means that every required power of H times |ui| is bounded. The source quotient has finite volume, so Mi = ∫X Ni|ui| dμ is finite. At every fixed y, the kernel also grows at most polynomially in x. The same decay proves that each defining lift integral exists there. Continuity and invariance in both rational variables give measurable kernels on the quotients. Change the order. Put F(x,x′,y) = K₁(x,y)u₁(x) K₂(x′,y)u₂(x′). For fixed x,x′, Cauchy–Schwarz in y gives ∫ |K₁(x,y)K₂(x′,y)| dν ≤ N₁(x)N₂(x′). The remaining bound separates into a function of x and a function of x′. Tonelli’s theorem applies to |F| because it is nonnegative, giving the absolute bound below. With equal kernels and equal inputs this bound also gives Θi ∈ L²(Y). We can now use Fubini for the complex integrand itself.

Substitution accounts for the single target-volume factor in the norm formula. The absolute bound above exchanges integrations over the two source copies and the target. Try the two orders on an infinite sum. Give X = {1,2,…} counting measure and Y = {+1,−1} equal probabilities. Let K(n,y) = n yn−1, u₁(n) = 21−n and u₂(n) = (z/2)n−1 with |z| = 1. The target slice norm is exactly n. Its weighted sum is ∑ n/2n−1 = 4, giving an absolute bound of 16. In the reversed order the inner kernel pairing is nm for equal parity and zero for opposite parity. At cutoff 2 and z = i, the two target lifts are (2,0) and (1 + i,1 − i). Pairing them gives 1 − i. Reversing the order gives the same value from the (1,1) and (2,2) terms. For an independent check on the limit, differentiating the geometric series gives ∑n≥1 n rn−1 = (1 − r)−2.
