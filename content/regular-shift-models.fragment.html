Which shifted weights give actual spaces of vectors?

In the column moment a matrix entry of the inverse of g multiplies a coefficient of π. Together they are a coefficient of X = V* ⊗ π, where V is the defining representation of G = U(p,q). To integrate its square we want to write it in terms of representations with a known formal degree. A list of candidate eigenvalues tells us how to label the pieces. We also need vectors in each piece and maps which recover every smooth column.

Regular unit shifts. Let 1 ≤ q ≤ p, m = p + q. Order the Harish-Chandra parameters λ₁ > ⋯ > λm and keep the sign attached to each position. These parameters lie on a common translate of the integers. Assume that opposite-sign entries differ by at least h ≥ 200m³. To form the sth candidate, subtract one from λs. The lattice condition is preserved. Only the gap to the next entry can close. Hence the last entry can always be lowered, and an earlier entry can be lowered exactly when its next gap exceeds one. Every opposite-sign gap after the move is at least h − 1. The analytic threshold used below is at most 43m³, so even the additional allowance m² + 3 for coefficient estimates fits inside the original gap.

The model at an allowed weight ν. Write K = U(p) × U(q) for the maximal compact subgroup. The lowest compact type is a finite-dimensional representation of K carrying a distinguished highest weight. For ν, take that type T, its unit highest-weight vector w, and its matrix kernel Ψν. The lowest-type construction gives square-integrable smooth functions g ↦ ⟨w,Ψν(g)v⟩ as v ranges over T. Start with their span, adjoin its derivatives under the complexified Lie algebra, and take the closure in L²(G). The discrete-series realization theorem for this kernel says that right translation preserves this closure, acts unitarily and irreducibly on it, and has parameter ν. Thus a regular lowering supplies an actual Hilbert space, which we call πs. The construction uses the existence and decay of the normalized kernels for the original and shifted weights.

Coefficient comparison at a regular shift. Call a vector K-finite when its compact translates span a finite-dimensional space. Let ρ(g) = (g−1)ᵗ. For K-finite v,w in π and any coordinate pair c,d, the product below has a finite expansion. In the other direction each regular model has a nonzero vector y whose diagonal coefficient ⟨y,πs(g)y⟩ is a finite sum of products of the type on the left.

ρ(g)cd ⟨w,π(g)v⟩ = ∑i ⟨bi,πs(i)(g)ai⟩.

On the space generated by derivatives, the tensor differential splits each input into finitely many eigenvectors. The central-character calculation labels their eigenvalues by shifts. The partial-trace and residue calculation removes the eigenspace of a singular shift. For a surviving eigenvector, the coefficient comparison with the model of that weight supplies one term on the right. Conversely the algebraic embedding of the shifted module realizes a pair of its vectors in tensor coordinates. Expanding the pairing over those coordinates gives the reverse identity. The embedding compares vectors in the two algebraic models before completion. G. Zuckerman, Tensor products of finite and infinite dimensional representations of semisimple Lie groups, on translation by finite-dimensional tensors.

How does a collision in the weights remove an eigenspace?

On the tensor columns generated by the lowest compact type, take D = −Ω, where Ω is the column differential used in the final projector. The minus sign comes from the action of a matrix on covectors. If position s survives, its candidate eigenvalue for D is as = −λs − (m − 1)/2. These numbers are distinct even when shifting a weight would create a tie. To determine which candidates are present, take the partial trace of an operator on columns, the sum of its diagonal operator entries. It acts on the original representation space.

Here are the two representation-theoretic ingredients. On the generated columns, the finite spectral decomposition of D gives projections Pb at a finite set of real values b, and each partial trace equals a scalar cb times the identity. Second, the central character prescribes the partial trace of each power Dk. Its value is the coefficient of zk+1 in ∏s(1 + z/(1 − asz)) − 1. We can compare these two evaluations using ordinary polynomials.

Introduce u = 1/z. The product becomes ∏t(1 + 1/(u − at)) − 1. Its partial fraction at as has numerator rs obtained by multiplying by u − as and evaluating there. The difference between the rational function and the sum of these partial fractions has no poles and tends to zero at infinity, so it vanishes. Expanding 1/(u − a) as z/(1 − az) now gives, for k ≥ 0,

∑b cb bk = ∑s rs ask,   rs = ∏t≠s (λs − 1 − λt)/(λs − λt).

To isolate a value v, take the union of the two finite supports. For each other value w multiply by the polynomial factor (X − w)/(v − w). The resulting polynomial is one at v and zero everywhere else on the union. Linear combinations of the power identities therefore equate the total weight at v on both sides.

One analytic fact is essential at this point. For the spectral projections here, a zero partial-trace scalar forces the entire projection to vanish. The coordinate calculation below proves this fact. In the coefficient model, testing the projection on one lowest-type vector gives the scalar as a sum of squared norms of representing vectors divided by a positive formal degree. If the scalar vanishes, all those vectors vanish. Every projected coefficient of a column supported on that test vector then vanishes. Vary the lowest-type vector and apply the commuting differential action to reach the generated columns. This positivity argument is what turns a statement about a trace into one about vectors.

If λs − 1 = λt, one numerator of rs vanishes. The interpolation and positivity show that there is no nonzero eigenvector at as. Conversely, when a lowering is regular every factor is nonzero, so the matching spectral projection is nonzero. For (6001,6000,0) the three partial-trace scalars are 0, 5999/3000 and 3001/3000. Their sum is 3, as it must be for the partial trace of the identity on three columns. Only the last two shifts contribute to the coefficient expansion.

How do identities of scalar functions recover a smooth column?

Fix an allowed shift and write σ = πs. Let A(g) denote the action on the smooth columns of X. Fix a coordinate b, a smooth vector w in π and a vector y in σ. Use the inverse action to define a scalar test fb,x(g) = ⟨w,(A(g−1)x)b⟩. Take inner products linear in the second slot. Against the same Haar measure as the formal degree, set

Pb,w,yx = ∫G fb,x(g) σ(g)y dg.

The gap bound supplies coefficient decay of order m² + 2. The single finite-dimensional matrix factor uses one power of the group size. The remaining majorant of order −(m² + 1) is integrable and its constant for fixed w is bounded by a finite sum of derivative norms of x. Since σ is unitary, the norm of the integrand is |fb,x(g)| ‖y‖. Substituting g = hu gives P A(h) = σ(h)P by left invariance. The derivative-norm estimate makes P continuous on smooth columns, so applying it to the smooth orbit of x gives the smooth orbit of Px.

Every regular model is detected. Compact-finite vectors in these discrete series are smooth, so their one-coordinate columns can enter the averaging map. Take the nonzero y in the reverse coefficient identity. Evaluate that identity at g−1 and multiply by ⟨y,σ(g)y⟩. The left side integrates to ‖y‖⁴/dσ by Schur orthogonality. Every term on the right is ⟨y,Px⟩ for one of the maps above and a column supported in one coordinate. The positive left side implies at least one such map is nonzero.

Together the maps detect every column. Suppose every map to every regular model kills a smooth column x. Choose a nonzero K-finite w in π. For a fixed coordinate c, multiply each fb,x by ρ(g)cb⟨w,π(g)w⟩ and sum over b. By the forward coefficient identity every integral is a finite sum of pairings with images of x, hence zero. Before integration, the factors ρ(g) and ρ(g−1) multiply to the identity. The single surviving coordinate gives, by mixed Schur orthogonality,

0 = ∫G ⟨w,π(g)w⟩ ⟨xc,π(g)w⟩ dg = ‖w‖² ⟨w,xc⟩ / dπ.

The common kernel is invariant. Apply the same calculation to A(h)x and multiply its vector of coordinate pairings by ρ(h)−1. This yields ⟨w,π(h)xc⟩ = 0 for every h. The closed span of the translates of the nonzero vector w is an invariant subspace, hence is the whole irreducible space of π. Each coordinate vanishes. M. Duflo and C. C. Moore, Schur orthogonality for square-integrable representations.

Why does one map per regular shift suffice?

Let B be the shifted model πs and T the original π. Translating B by the defining representation V raises the same coordinate back to λ. The averaging argument for this direction gives a nonzero map V ⊗ B → T. Tensor adjunction turns it into an inclusion κ from B into V* ⊗ T. In coordinates adjunction sends a map f to b ↦ ∑ded ⊗ f(ed ⊗ b), where the two bases are dual. Evaluation in each basis direction recovers f. The smooth kernel theorem makes this map injective. Indeed, if it killed a nonzero smooth vector, equivariance would kill its entire orbit span. Smooth approximation by that span controls any prescribed finite list of derivatives. Continuity in those derivative norms would then make the map zero on every smooth vector.

To bound the number of maps V* ⊗ T → B, use adjunction once more. A candidate now reads I from T to V ⊗ B. Apply I to each T-coordinate of κ. Reordering the two finite factors gives a map Φ from B to V ⊗ V* ⊗ B. Contracting the first two slots takes the trace of its matrix of vectors. This makes an endomorphism of B, hence a scalar by Schur's lemma. To see why the scalar determines I, suppose it is zero.

Write C(λ) for the scalar of the quadratic Casimir, the sum of squared coordinates of λ minus a rank-dependent constant. On Φ the total Casimir has scalar CB, and the partial Casimir on the V and B slots has scalar CT by construction of I. Expanding the square of the three-slot differential gives the sum of the two partial Casimirs, with one copy of the B-Casimir subtracted because both partials contain it, plus a cross term between the two finite slots. On a matrix of vectors that cross term is −2 times the map sending its trace to a scalar diagonal matrix. Indeed on the dual factor the generator Eab acts by −Eba. Thus on Φ the expansion reads Ctotal = CV,B + CV*,B − CB. On each row the partial Casimir for V* ⊗ B would have scalar 2CB − CT.

But the maps separating this last tensor have targets at regular second lowerings. Subtracting one in position s changes the sum of squares by −2λs + 1. A second subtraction in position t gives

C(λ − es − et) − [2C(λ − es) − C(λ)] = 2(λs − λt) + 2δst.

For t = s the difference is 2. For distinct positions it is nonzero by strict ordering. An intertwiner commutes with the Casimir, so its image of each row must be zero. Joint separation gives Φ = 0. Since κ is nonzero, one of its coordinates takes a nonzero value, which I must kill. The kernel theorem for smooth discrete-series intertwiners then gives I = 0. We have injected the space of candidate maps into the scalars. Existence from the preceding argument makes its dimension exactly one.

Choose one nonzero projection Ps per regular shift. Compositions with inclusions of other weights vanish because their central characters differ. Some projection detects a nonzero vector in the sth inclusion, hence its own projection does. Normalize that inclusion so the matching composition is the identity. Subtract ∑sκsPsx from a smooth column x. Every chosen projection kills the difference. Multiplicity one makes every map to every model a multiple of a chosen projection, so joint separation kills the difference itself. This is the smooth direct sum on which the cofactor projector is evaluated.
