How can witnesses with different scalar residues determine one matrix?

Work in ℤ₃[[t]], the ring of series with 3-adic integer coefficients, and let a generator g act by g(t) = w t while fixing the coefficients. Take w = 4. All matrices in this example act on the same two coordinates of one layer. The order of a witness is measured in powers of t. Its scalar normalization is a power of 3.

Let J have its sole nonzero entry, a 1, in the upper right corner, and put B = I + tJ. Since J² = 0, the inverse of g(B) is I − w tJ. The correction Δ = B g(B)−1 is therefore I + (1 − w)tJ. In particular Δ g(B) = B. This choice defines an action for all powers of the generator by taking the correction at gj to be B gj(B)−1.

Consider β₃ = 3(B + t³J) and a candidate β₄ = 3k(B + tdJ), with d ≥ 2. Multiplying the conjugate of the second matrix by Δ cancels its degree-one term against that of the original. The remaining defect is

Δ g(β₄) − β₄ = 3k(wd − 1) tdJ.

At w = 4, d = 4 and k = 2, the residues at t = 0 are 3I and 9I. Both pass their stated tests, including for every power of g. Divide each by its own scalar, as one can do in ℚ₃[[t]]. Their difference is (t⁴ − t³)J, which vanishes modulo t³. Allowing an arbitrary term first appearing at each witness’s own order gives the same normalized class at every lower order. Here the limit is B.

The setting d = 2 distinguishes two mechanisms. For w = 4 the factor w² − 1 = 15 is divisible by 3, but it is nonzero. Its term of degree two prevents β₄ from passing the test modulo t⁴, however large k is. For w = −1 the same factor vanishes exactly. The degree-two perturbation then passes at every order while altering the normalized class modulo t³. The infinite-order multiplier in the period proof rules out this ambiguity at every degree.
