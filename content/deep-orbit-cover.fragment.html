Which short sublattice gives the small orbit?

Fix a point x with least normalized sublattice size τ < ½. Write Λ for its lattice, g ≥ 1 for the level, f for the number of real places and d for the root discriminant. Let q bound the real Witt index at every place, the largest dimension of a totally isotropic subspace there. We seek a nonzero rational isotropic space W for which the unipotent radical N of the stabilizer of W has short representatives modulo rational translations and level g. The size we shall obtain is proportional to u = (2τ)1/q.

The canonical lattice filtration groups together the directions of Λ that enter at each successive normalized size. Below ½ its nonzero jumps give a flag 0 = S₀ ⊊ ⋯ ⊊ Sₐ. Label their sizes 0 < t₀ = τ < ⋯ < ta−1 and append the endpoint tₐ = ½. The canonical flag theorem makes every Si+1 saturated in Λ and puts its largest normalized size at tᵢ. For every lattice strictly containing Si+1 inside Λ, the normalized size of its quotient by Si+1 is at least ti+1. Self-duality gives total isotropy of Sₐ. Since the ranks strictly increase and the last rank is at most the local Witt index, 1 ≤ a ≤ q.

The product of the a ratios tᵢ/ti+1 is t₀/tₐ = 2τ. At least one is at most (2τ)1/a ≤ u, since 0 < 2τ < 1. Choose such an index i. Put R = Si+1, h = tᵢ, and l = ti+1. The rational span of R is the required W. Two inequalities will pay for two different rounds of approximation.

h/l ≤ u,   h² ≤ u²l² ≤ u/4.

The last step uses l ≤ ½ and u ≤ 1. For example, τ = 1/128 and a = q = 2 give u = ⅛. If the intermediate size is 1/16, both ratios are ⅛. For the second step h = 1/16, l = ½, and h² = 1/256 ≤ u/4 = 1/32.

Why are two rounds of approximation enough?

In the filtration 0 ⊂ W ⊂ W⊥ ⊂ V, each matrix of the nilpotent Lie algebra sends a term into the preceding term. Thus all products of three vanish. The matrices sending V into W and vanishing on W⊥ form the central layer. It contains every commutator. In particular exp Y = 1 + Y + Y²/2 and log(exp B exp A) = B + A + [B,A]/2. Both formulas follow by multiplying the quadratic polynomials.

Use the coordinates of the Hermitian space at all real places to write a real orbit element as exp Y. An integral matrix here is a rational matrix preserving Λ under its linear action. Write ‖·‖x for the square root of the sum of squared Hilbert–Schmidt norms in the frames at x. The first covering-radius theorem gives an integral nilpotent matrix Y₀ and a real central matrix C approximating Y/(2g). The second gives an integral central approximation to any real central matrix. With the gap inequalities just proved, their respective radii in this norm are

r₁ = n²f d u/(8√(2f)),   r₂ = n²f d u/(16√f).

The first estimate uses the upper size h of R and the lower size l of every extension quotient. It rounds modulo the real center with error at most r₁. The second uses h² ≤ u/4 and rounds inside the center with error at most r₂. The first comes from approximating maps W⊥/W → W and lifting to the nilpotent algebra. For the second, one approximates maps V/W⊥ → W and subtracts the adjoint of an integral approximation to half the central matrix. To follow the constants, see why the first lift costs √2 and why subtracting the adjoint costs 2. With the two radius bounds in hand, combine the roundings at level g.

Combining the two roundings at level g

Put B = 2gY₀ and A = Y − B − 2gC, so ‖A‖x ≤ 2gr₁. Multiplying exp B by exp A introduces [B,A]/2. To cancel it, the central quantity that must still be represented is C′ = 2gC + [A,B]/2. Round C′/g to an integral central matrix Z₀, and put Z = C′ − gZ₀. Then ‖Z‖x ≤ gr₂. With E = 2gY₀ + gZ₀, the two corrections give

exp Y = exp E exp A exp Z,   ‖A‖x + ‖Z‖x ≤ n²√f g d u/4.

Indeed the two costs are n²√f g d u times 1/(4√2) and 1/16. Their sum is at most ¼ because √2 ≥ 4/3. The full two-parameter family exp(sA) exp(tZ) stays in the same real unipotent radical for all real s,t. Thus the path joining the representative to x also lies on the orbit.

It remains to check what exp E does at the finite places. A central matrix has zero product with every matrix of this nilpotent algebra. Consequently exp E − 1 = g(2Y₀ + Z₀ + 2gY₀²). This sends Λ into gΛ. Replacing Y₀ and Z₀ by their negatives checks the same condition for the inverse. This explains the factor 2 in the first rounding even when g is odd. The rational element γ represented by exp E therefore has finite component satisfying xfin−1γfinxfin ∈ K(g), the level subgroup. Its inverse k also belongs to K(g). Restoring the frames, we get

(ν,1)x = γ y (1,k),   yfin = xfin.

Here ν is the given real unipotent element and y has the two real displacements just bounded. For a general adelic element m of the radical, strong approximation gives a rational δ in the radical such that xfin−1(δ−1m)finxfin lies in the open group K(g). Left rational invariance and right level invariance therefore identify each function value at mx with its value at ((δ−1m)∞,1)x. The small real representatives now control the entire orbit used for the cuspidal average.

Why can a rational correction put the finite component in the level?

The level allows an open neighborhood of the given finite component. To find a rational point in that neighborhood, use the same nilpotent coordinates as in the rounding argument. Exponential and logarithm are inverse polynomials, exp(T) = 1 + T + T²/2 and log(1 + T) = T − T²/2. They preserve rationality and are continuous over the finite adèles. A basis of the Lie algebra over the base field F thus reduces the question to approximating one finite adèle by a number in F, independently in each coordinate.

An open condition on a finite adèle prescribes precision at finitely many primes and integrality elsewhere. Multiply by a common nonzero integer d clearing all denominators. At the selected primes prescribe the required residue of the integral numerator modulo a sufficiently high prime-ideal power. At any further prime dividing d, prescribe divisibility by the local factor of (d) to preserve integrality after division. The Chinese remainder theorem gives a single numerator in the integers of F. Dividing by d gives the required rational coordinate. Applied to all coordinates and followed by exponentiation, this supplies δ in the desired open set.
