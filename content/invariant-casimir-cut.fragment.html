Which compact component inherits the trial bound?

The trial estimate bounds the sum of the squared errors in all matrix directions at once. The first task is to find a vector with one definite weight to which the estimate still applies. Write μ = td′, S = Ct, and let Dab act in the smooth restricted representation. The full Casimir Ω sums DabDba over the smaller group. Its compact counterpart ΩK sums only over pairs with the same sign. On a finite-dimensional compact-stable space containing the trial vector x, ΩK and all diagonal derivatives are commuting Hermitian operators. A diagonal commutator assigns the opposite weights to Dab and Dba, so their product has weight zero.

Decompose x into joint eigenvectors, grouping components with identical eigenvalues. To see why their energies add, expand the sum of derivative squares as a quadratic form. Its operator on smooth vectors is 2ΩK − Ω − 2∑a μaDaa + ‖μ‖²I. The signed adjoint of each matrix unit gives the first two terms. Ω commutes with every derived matrix unit, by cancellation in the commutator with the sum defining Ω. Thus this energy operator preserves all joint eigenvalues. Different joint eigenspaces are orthogonal, so their cross terms vanish even when the energy operator takes a vector outside the finite space. Simultaneous diagonalization here follows by diagonalizing one Hermitian operator and repeating on its invariant eigenspaces for the others.

Consequently the quotient for x is a weighted average of the component quotients, with weights proportional to the squared norms. Choose a nonzero component y with quotient at most S. Write ν for its diagonal weight and κ for its compact Casimir eigenvalue. The three contributions to its quotient are

Qμ(y)/‖y‖² = ‖ν−μ‖² + (κ−‖ν‖²) + Re ⟨(κI−Ω)y, y⟩/‖y‖².

The first term is the diagonal error. The second sums squared compact off-diagonal derivatives divided by ‖y‖². The third does the same for the opposite-sign derivatives. Each is nonnegative and at most S. The last inequality gives control on this one vector. Following a sequence of derivatives will require a bound throughout an invariant subspace.

A compact calculation. In the defining action of U(2) on ℂ² take μ = (1,0). Here Ω = ΩK = 2I, because ∑a,b EabEba = 2I. Both coordinate vectors have compact off-diagonal energy 1 and noncompact energy 0. Their diagonal errors are respectively 0 and 2, giving total quotients 1 and 3. The trial vector e₁+e₂ has quotient 2. Taking its first joint component improves the bound while fixing the weight.

A bounded inverse on the Hilbert space

Fix κ from the selected component and put U = κI − Ω. Work on the dense space of smooth vectors in the Hilbert space of the restricted representation. Write R = 2S > 0. In finite dimensions the function 1/(1+(u/R)²) is large near u = 0 and small far away. To use the same idea for the possibly unbounded derivative U, first construct a bounded operator T from

B = I + U²/R²,   Re ⟨Bz, z⟩ = ‖z‖² + ‖Uz‖²/R².

The identity follows from symmetry of Ω on smooth vectors. Cauchy–Schwarz gives ‖Bz‖ ≥ ‖z‖, so the assignment Bz ↦ z is a well-defined contraction. Why is the range of B dense? Let M be the closure of the range of B and Q its orthogonal projection. A group translation conjugates the two matrix units in each Casimir term. The tensor ∑ Eab ⊗ Eba is invariant under simultaneous conjugation because these bases are dual for the invariant trace pairing tr(XY). Thus translations preserve the range, and their inverses do too. Unitarity gives invariance of M and its perpendicular, so Q commutes with the group. R. Melrose, Functional Analysis, §9 on closed subspaces.

Any bounded operator commuting with the group takes smooth orbit maps to smooth orbit maps. Differentiation shows that it commutes with each derivative, hence with B on smooth vectors. For such z this gives BQz = QBz = Bz. Injectivity of B forces Qz = z. By density, M is the whole Hilbert space. Extend the contraction Bz ↦ z by taking limits of Cauchy sequences. The resulting operator T is self-adjoint because ⟨z, Bw⟩ = ⟨Bz, w⟩, and it commutes with the group by the same dense-range argument. On smooth vectors we therefore have both TBz = z and BTz = z.

A Casimir bound on every smooth vector of the cut

In the finite-dimensional picture a derivative eigenvalue u with |u| ≤ R gives an inverse eigenvalue at least 1/2. Use this threshold on the bounded self-adjoint operator T. Let A = T − ½I, and form its positive and negative parts P = max(A,0), N = max(−A,0). The continuous functional calculus assigns operators to these scalar functions by uniform polynomial approximation on the spectrum. It respects multiplication, takes the square of a real function to the square of a self-adjoint operator, and preserves commutation with group translations. Applying these facts to the two functions and to their square roots gives A = P − N, NP = 0, and both parts positive. R. Melrose, Functional Analysis, Theorem 3.5 and Remark 3.1.

Let W consist of the smooth vectors killed by N. It is stable under every derivative because N commutes with the group. For z in W, also Bz belongs to W. On that vector A equals its positive part. The identity TBz = z gives ½‖Bz‖² ≤ Re ⟨z, Bz⟩ ≤ ‖z‖ ‖Bz‖. Thus ‖Bz‖ ≤ 2‖z‖. Substitution in the preceding square identity yields

‖Uz‖ ≤ R‖z‖,   Re ⟨Ωz, z⟩ ≥ (κ−2S)‖z‖²   (z ∈ W).

This lower bound will apply at every step when roots move the starting weight. The existence of a starting vector in W still requires the estimate on y.

Why a vector of the original weight survives

Restrict the calculation to the smooth eigenspace ΩK = κ. There U is nonnegative as a quadratic form because its expectation is the sum of the squared opposite-sign derivatives. Both U and T preserve this eigenspace. Put V = U/R and x = Ty, so that y = (I+V²)x. The scalar inequality 1−1/(1+v²) ≤ v/2 for v ≥ 0 follows after clearing the denominator from v(1−v)² ≥ 0. On smooth vectors there is a direct version with no choice of eigenbasis. Put w = (I−V)x. Expansion using symmetry of V gives

Re ⟨Vy, y⟩ − 2 Re ⟨y−x, y⟩ = Re ⟨Vw, w⟩ + Re ⟨V(Vw), Vw⟩ ≥ 0.

Because Re ⟨Uy, y⟩ ≤ S‖y‖² and R = 2S, we get Re ⟨Ty, y⟩ ≥ ¾‖y‖². Hence Re ⟨Ay, y⟩ is strictly positive. If Py vanished, this expectation would equal −⟨Ny, y⟩ ≤ 0. Thus Py is nonzero, and NP = 0 places it in W. Applying P to the finite compact-stable space of y gives another finite compact-stable space. Commutation with derivatives shows that Py has weight ν and compact Casimir κ. These facts supply the starting vector for compact raising.

The constant ¾ is attained when U/R = diag(0,1) and y = (1,1). The two inverse eigenvalues are 1 and ½, so their mean is ¾. The middle coordinate in the example below has U/R = 1. It is retained by the kernel condition, while the coefficient of this coordinate in Py is zero. The distinction matters because the theorem uses the entire stable space for subsequent derivatives and a single nonzero vector to start them.

A compact highest vector within square-root distance

Take the surviving vector of weight ν in the cutoff W. A compact raising move applies a derivative Dab with a < b of the same sign, provided the resulting vector is nonzero. Its new weight is ω + ea − eb when the old one is ω. This follows by commuting the derivative with each diagonal unit. If F is the finite compact span of y, the starting vector Py lies in P(F). Every subsequent compact derivative stays in that finite-dimensional compact-stable space, which is contained in W because NP = 0. They preserve the compact Casimir eigenvalue κ because ΩK commutes with compact derivatives.

At any visited weight ω, the diagonal derivative squares are part of the sum defining κ on that vector, so ‖ω‖² ≤ κ. Meanwhile ∑c cωc decreases by b−a ≥ 1 at each move. It has a lower bound on the ball ‖ω‖ ≤ √κ. The chain must end at a nonzero vector annihilated by every compact raising operator. Call its highest weight L. Each move increases the pairing with the decreasing target μ by μa − μb ≥ 0. Expanding the square now controls how far the final weight can be from μ.

‖L−μ‖² = ‖ν−μ‖² + ‖L‖² − ‖ν‖² − 2⟨L−ν, μ⟩ ≤ S + (κ−‖ν‖²) ≤ 2S.

The two terms bounded by S are precisely the diagonal and compact off-diagonal errors already available before the cutoff. Their sum was itself bounded by S in the original energy decomposition. The separate estimates suffice for the radius √(2S) used in the descent. We have found a compact highest vector at coordinate distance at most s = √(2S) from the target. Its compact Casimir scalar is cK(L) = ‖L+ρK‖² − ‖ρK‖² = κ, where ρK is the half-sum of the positive compact roots. Indeed, in the Casimir sum the diagonal terms contribute ‖L‖², and each positive compact root contributes La−Lb by commuting the raising operator to the right.

Why opposite-sign lowering must stop

A compact highest vector lies in a finite sum of irreducible compact types. Within its finite compact span, the orthogonal complement of any stable subspace is stable by the compact adjoint relations. Splitting repeatedly gives irreducible summands. Projecting the highest vector onto one where it is nonzero preserves its weight and all its raising equations. Start with this type inside the invariant cutoff W. For a type of highest weight M, its first layer is spanned by the type and the vectors obtained by applying one noncompact derivative. Commuting a compact derivative past a noncompact derivative shows this layer is compact-stable and finite-dimensional.

If the layer contains a nonzero compact highest vector of weight M − ea + eb for a < b of opposite signs, select its irreducible summand and continue. All these spaces lie in W. Each coordinate changes by at most one on each move. Assume μa − μb ≥ τ for all a < b. For up to J moves every coordinate is within s+J of μ, so Ma−Mb ≥ τ−2(s+J). On subtracting the root α = ea − eb, expand the compact scalar to obtain

cK(M) − cK(M−α) = 2⟨M+ρK, α⟩−2 ≥ 2(τ−2(s+J))−2n−2.

Here ‖α‖² = 2 and each coordinate of ρK has absolute value at most n/2 by counting compact roots. Write h for the lower bound in the display. The full Casimir expectation on every unit highest vector in W is at least κ−2S, and its compact Casimir is at least this expectation by the noncompact square identity. After J moves the compact scalar would be at most κ−Jh. If Jh > 2S this is impossible. A terminal type therefore occurs in fewer than J moves, with highest weight L′ within s+J of μ in each coordinate. For example, with n = 2, τ = 1000 and S = 2 one can take J = 1. A single lowering would decrease the compact scalar by at least 1982 while the cutoff permits at most 4. Under those hypotheses the first compact type already has the terminal property.

To recover the parameter, each coordinate of L′ is an integer. Indeed the corresponding diagonal circle acts on that weight by exp(iθL′a), which is periodic with period 2π. Put μ′ = L′ + ρK − ρnoncompact. Each half-sum has coordinates bounded by n/2, and their difference has the required parity. In coordinate a, subtracting (n−1)/2 from the difference yields minus the number of earlier coordinates of the same sign minus the number of later coordinates of the opposite sign. In particular each μ′a belongs to ℤ+(n−1)/2. The separation assumption ensures strict decreasing order and enough gap for the representation theorem.

μ′a−μ′b ≥ τ−2(s+J)−2n   (a < b),   |μ′a−μa| ≤ s+J+n.

In the distant-parameter argument τ ≥ n³ + 4n + 2 + 2(s+J). The gap in the display exceeds n³ + 2n + 1. The identification of the terminal type uses precisely the absence of these highest vectors in the first layer. Its conclusion embeds every discrete-series model at μ′. To obtain an actual constituent one also needs such a model to exist. The large-gap existence step supplies such a model once the scale is sufficiently large. A. Knapp and P. Trapa, Representations of Semisimple Lie Groups, Lecture 7, Harish-Chandra’s discrete-series theorem.
