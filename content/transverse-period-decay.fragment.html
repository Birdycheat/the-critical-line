Why does a translated coefficient become small away from the stabilizer?

The bottom period contains one integral for each translate. Its size depends on how far the translate moves a distinguished vector z. Start at a single real place, in coordinates where z is a positive unit coordinate vector. Write G for the unitary group of the signed Hermitian form in m = n + 1 variables, and H for the unitary group on its orthogonal complement. The embedding ι extends each element of H by the identity on z. Put ℓ(g) = ‖gz‖₂ and let ‖·‖F denote the Frobenius norm. Because g preserves the signed form, ℓ(g) ≥ 1. Right multiplication by ι(h) leaves gz unchanged.

Local coefficient estimate. Let π and σ be the discrete series on G and H in the real branching construction. Here n ≥ 3, the smaller signature has at least one negative direction and at least as many positive as negative directions. Both lists of Harish-Chandra parameters have absolute values at most B, descend in steps of at least 2 and have gaps of at least 1000m³ between entries of opposite signs within either list. The signed lists satisfy the branching condition both as given and after lowering any one entry of the longer list by 1. In decreasing order of the combined entries, successive distinct levels with no entry strictly between them obey this rule for each pair of entries at the two levels. Their signs agree exactly when the entries come from different lists.

Choose a lowest K-type unit vector v₀ in π, where K is the maximal compact subgroup fixed by the signed coordinates. Put v = π(g₀)v₀, with ‖g₀‖F ≤ (8mB)10m, and Φ(X) = ⟨v,π(X)v⟩. For the model Haar measure μ₀ on H, write V₀ = μ₀{h : ‖h − 1‖F ≤ 1/4} and ℬ(C) = exp(Cm⁴ log(8mB)). Then for every g ∈ G the restricted translate is absolutely integrable and

∫H |Φ(gι(h))| dμ₀(h) ≤ V₀ ℬ(1000) ℓ(g)−(2n+7).

Where the two powers go. The large-gap estimate for a lowest K-type bounds |⟨v₀,π(X)v₀⟩| by exp(100m³) ‖X‖F−L, with L = 40m². Unitarity replaces X by g₀−1Xg₀ for the vector v. The Frobenius norm of the inverse of a signed unitary matrix equals the norm of the matrix, since the inverse is its conjugate transpose between two diagonal sign matrices. Submultiplicativity now costs at most ‖g₀‖F2L.

Use the unitary decomposition g−1 = ι(u)b, where β = ‖b‖F satisfies β² ≤ m + 2ℓ(g)². To see the decomposition, split gz into its positive and negative parts, of Euclidean lengths a and d. Thus a² − d² = 1. Up to separate ordinary unitary rotations in these two coordinate spaces, the matrix carrying z to gz is the block with rows (a,d) and (d,a), extended by the identity. If d = 0 a rotation alone suffices. The resulting matrix T has squared Frobenius norm m − 2 + 2(a² + d²). Since T−1g fixes z, inversion gives the required factorization with b = T−1.

Put x = ‖gι(h)‖F and s = ‖u−1h‖F. The unchanged column gives x ≥ ℓ(g), and b gι(h) = ι(u−1h) gives s ≤ βx. Reserve A = 4n² + 1 powers for integration on H. Since β ≤ √(3m) ℓ(g),

x−L ≤ s−A βA ℓ(g)−(L−A) ≤ (3m)A/2 s−A ℓ(g)−(2n+7).

The last inequality uses L − 2A ≥ 2n + 7. To integrate the factor s−A, left invariance removes u. In the real space of complex n-by-n matrices, balls of radius r = 1/(8R) around a maximal 2r-separated subset of {‖h‖F ≤ R} have disjoint interiors. Volume comparison in dimension 2n² bounds their number by (1 + 8R²)2n². Every group point in that set lies within 2r of a centre h₀, hence ‖h₀−1h − 1‖F ≤ R·2r = 1/4. Each centre therefore costs at most V₀ in group measure.

On the shell 2j ≤ ‖h‖F < 2j+1, its integral is at most V₀·2−Aj(1 + 32·4j)2n² ≤ V₀·332n²2−j. Summing over j ≥ 0 gives ∫‖h‖F−A dμ₀ ≤ exp(10n²)V₀. The continuous coefficient thus has an integrable majorant. The remaining factors exp(100m³)‖g₀‖F2L(3m)A/2exp(10n²) fit inside ℬ(1000) by the stated bound on g₀ and B ≥ 1.

A rank-four check. Put the block with rows (5/4, 3/4) and (3/4, 5/4) on one positive and one negative coordinate and use the identity on the other two. Since (5/4)² − (3/4)² = 1, this is signed unitary. Taking the positive coordinate in that block as z gives ℓ(g)² = 17/8. Here n = 3, A = 37 and L = 640. Of the 566 remaining powers the estimate uses 13, giving the factor (8/17)13/2 in its upper bound.

How is the size of the identity term used as the unit of comparison?

Diagonal normalization. The bounded-translate argument supplies an isometric embedding of the σ-model into the π-model, its adjoint P, and a choice of the bounded translate above with ℬ(100)‖Pv‖ ≥ 1. The branching eigenvector theorem, for the formal degree d′ > 0 of σ with respect to μ₀, supplies a smooth unit vector w and a scalar c₁ ≥ ‖Pv‖²/(2d′). For every vector a in σ the diagonal integral has the value

∫H conj(Φ(ι(h))) ⟨a,σ(h)w⟩ dμ₀(h) = c₁ ⟨a,w⟩.

In particular, setting a = w measures the positive diagonal scalar c₁ itself. The subgroup formal-degree estimate gives V₀ ≤ ℬ(11)/d′. Now 1 ≤ ℬ(100)²‖Pv‖² and 2 ≤ ℬ(1), so V₀ ≤ c₁ℬ(212). Multiplying by ℬ(1000) from the local integral gives c₁ℬ(1212). The simultaneous estimates also allow ℬ(600) for the derivative integral relative to V₀. A single exponent chosen for the full collection is C = 1000 + 600 + 11 + 201 = 1812. At scale b the construction takes B = b² and assumes log(8mb²) ≤ 6 log b. Thus ℬ(C) ≤ exp(q) with q = 6Cm⁴ log b.

Return to an arithmetic frame. At a real place τ choose an adapted frame Dτ that changes the arithmetic Gram matrix to signed coordinates, with Dτz equal to the distinguished coordinate vector. Then the distinguished column of DτgDτ−1 is exactly Dτgz. On the stabilizer the conjugate model reverses the coordinate order in both rows and columns. This is a continuous group isomorphism ρ with continuous inverse. The image of the arithmetic-coordinate Haar measure μτ is another Haar measure on the model group. Haar uniqueness gives ρ∗μτ = λτμ₀ for a finite λτ > 0. Consequently absolute integrability transfers in both directions. The integral of the norm gains λτ, as does the diagonal scalar after conjugating the smaller model's coefficient. Write cτ = λτc₁. If Iτ(g) is the absolute integral in these coordinates, then

Iτ(g) ‖Dτgz‖₂2m+5 ≤ exp(q) |cτ|,   2m + 5 = 2n + 7.

For example, multiplying μτ by 3 triples both Iτ and cτ. Their ratio still obeys the same bound. A. Sutherland, Number Theory I, Lecture 13, Theorem 13.3 (Haar uniqueness).

What measure and weight does a single rational class contribute?

Write f for the number of real places, and fix a Haar measure on the product of their stabilizers. In the chosen frames this measure is κ times the product of the local Haar measures, for one κ > 0. The translated coefficient is a product of local functions, each absolutely integrable by the preceding result. Fubini gives an absolute integral of κ∏τIτ. Applied to the diagonal pairing it gives c∞ = κ∏τcτ. The product of the bounds therefore has exponent Q = fq and diagonal factor |c∞|. Haar uniqueness, Theorem 13.3 · T. Tao, An Epsilon of Room, I, Theorem 1.1.23 (Fubini–Tonelli).

At finite places take a self-dual lattice Λ containing z and a nonzero level ideal 𝔪. Let K be the subgroup preserving Λ and acting trivially modulo 𝔪, and K′ its preimage in the finite stabilizer. Its finite-place Haar measure μf(K′) is positive and finite. The subgroup is open and contains the identity, and is compact because the stabilizer is closed and K is compact. For a translate g, the finite coefficient is the indicator of Sg = {hf : gfι(hf) ∈ K}. If h₀ belongs to this set, cancel gfι(h₀) inside K. Membership then says exactly h₀−1hf ∈ K′. Thus its measure is zero when it is empty and μf(K′) otherwise. For the identity term it equals μf(K′).

Given the adelic Haar measure, choose real and finite Haar factors whose product pushes forward to it. Indeed, multiplication identifies the two factors with the adelic group. Push forward any product of Haar measures and absorb the positive scalar from Haar uniqueness into the real factor. Let x be the chosen point in the stabilizer and γ a rational representative, set g = ι(x−1)γ, and write zγ = γz. The integral of the absolute coefficient factors as μf(Sg) times its real absolute integral. Denoting this adelic integral by Iγ(x), the estimate actually used in the sum is

Iγ(x) · (∏τ ‖Dτι(xτ−1)zγ,τ‖₂)2m+5 ≤ exp(Q) |c|,   c = μf(K′) c∞.

This c multiplies the value of the cusp form in the identity contribution. For every other class, multiplying the absolute coefficient integral by the supremum bound for the cusp form gives an upper bound on its weighted contribution. The congruence test and the lattice estimate for the sum then decide whether the identity term dominates. A simultaneous rescaling of the integrals and of the identity contribution preserves the comparison.
