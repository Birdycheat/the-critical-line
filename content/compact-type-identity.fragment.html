Which derivatives determine the lowest-type coefficient?

A matrix coefficient of an infinite-dimensional representation will be replaced by an integral on a compact group. The difficulty is to prove that the integral represents the same function. Fix G = U(p,s), m = p + s, q = min(p,s) and K = U(p) × U(s). Denote the diagonal signature matrix by J. Keep the coordinate order in which the Harish-Chandra parameters λ₁ > ⋯ > λm decrease, and record the sign of each coordinate in that order. Each λa − (m−1)/2 is integral. Suppose |λa − λb| ≥ h when the signs differ, with h ≥ 120mq3/2. When both signs occur, this gives an opposite-sign gap γ > 2m, for instance γ = h. When there is only one sign the gap condition is vacuous and any γ > 2m serves.

Let π be the unitary discrete series with these data. Denote its lowest K-type by S, with highest weight Λ = λ + ρn − ρc. Here ρn and ρc are the half-sums of the positive roots between opposite and equal signs respectively. The large-gap multiplicity-one theorem says that the sum of all copies of this lowest type is itself one finite-dimensional irreducible K-space. Write Π for the orthogonal projection onto S. We seek the operator x ↦ Ππ(g)x on S. The later branching projection in the identity term has a different target, the representation of a smaller unitary group.

Uniqueness from derivatives at the identity. Consider a smooth difference F(g) of two proposed operators on S. Suppose it transforms on both sides by K, so F(k₁gk₂) = π(k₁)F(g)π(k₂) on S, and F(1) = 0. One further set of first-order equations is required. Write Dab for differentiation along g exp(tX), extended complex-linearly to the matrix unit Eab. Arrays (vab) of vectors in S encode the noncompact first derivatives, with entries only in opposite-sign positions. Under the simultaneous compact action on the directions and the vectors, their lowering part is the sum of compact types of highest weight Λ + ea − eb with b < a of opposite signs. The first-order condition near the identity is

∑a,b Dab[F(g)vab] = 0   for every array in the lowering part.

Under these hypotheses every iterated derivative of F at 1 vanishes. The derivative of order zero is the value F(1) itself. For the coefficient below, averaging projections onto the compact translates of a highest-weight line gives I/dim S, so the normalized average and the compressed representation both start at the identity on S. Now assume all derivatives through order j−1 vanish. Commuting two adjacent derivatives replaces their difference by a bracket, a derivative of lower order. The order-j derivative is therefore symmetric in its j directions. Compact covariance removes any compact direction by differentiating the action on the input or output. The remaining derivative defines a compact-equivariant linear map T from arrays of j noncompact directions with coefficients in S to S. Applying the other j−1 derivatives to the displayed equation shows that T kills arrays obtained by inserting a lowering array in one slot with the other indices fixed.

Why must T vanish on the rest? Work in the orthogonal complement of its kernel. Permutations preserve T, so a vector f in that complement is symmetric. Orthogonality to the inserted lowering arrays says that every one-slot slice of f lies in the complementary raising part. Let CK be the compact Casimir and κ its scalar on S. The Casimir estimate on a raising slice gives a mixed term of at least (γ − 2m) times its squared norm. Summing over the slices recovers ‖f‖². There are j identical slot contributions by symmetry, each counted twice in the Casimir of a tensor product, and the Casimir on the direction indices is nonnegative. Consequently

Re ⟨f, CKf⟩ ≥ [κ + 2j(γ − 2m)] ‖f‖².

Equivariance gives T(CK − κ)f = 0, so pairing with f gives the left side exactly κ‖f‖². Since j ≥ 1 and γ > 2m, we get f = 0. This proves the induction from the assumed value F(1) = 0. The compact-type inputs here are the splitting of the one-slot array into raising and lowering types and the stated Casimir bound on each raising type.

An integral formula for the compressed operator

Choose a highest-weight unit vector w in S. Let d = dimℂ S, and give K Haar probability measure. Write Δa(Z) for the leading principal minor of size a, and put μa = λa + (m+1−2a)/2 for 1 ≤ a ≤ m, with μm+1 = 0. These are integers. Set Φ(Z) = ∏a=1m Δa(Z)−(μa−μa+1) wherever the minors are nonzero. Take a Hermitian X with XJ = −JX. Then exp X belongs to G and is positive definite, as is each compact conjugate. For arbitrary u, v ∈ S, the compression formula is

⟨v, π(exp X)u⟩ = d ∫K Φ(k−1 exp(X) k) ⟨v, π(k)w⟩ ⟨π(k)w, u⟩ dk.

Identify the value and derivatives. At X = 0, Φ(1) = 1. Averaging the projections onto the lines ℂπ(k)w gives an operator commuting with K. Its eigenspaces are invariant, so irreducibility makes it scalar. Its trace is 1, hence it equals I/d. This explains the multiplier d and makes the proposed operator agree with Ππ(1) on S.

For the comparison near 1 one uses the compact-equivariant extension from positive matrices by polar decomposition. Its local expression at a group element g uses Φ of the entrywise conjugate of k−1gk. On the positive ray the principal minors are real and this is exactly the displayed integral. Near 1 the minors are uniformly separated from zero for k in the compact group, so they may be differentiated under the integral. In the conjugated coordinates the derivative is a sum indexed by a row c and column b. Terms with b < c have zero scalar derivative coefficient by triangularity of the leading minors. For c ≤ b, the highest-weight component of a lowering array paired with the rotated vector w is zero. Thus the integral satisfies the lowering equation. The representation coefficient satisfies it as well. Indeed the map taking (vab) to ∑ dπ(Eab)vab intertwines the compact action. A nonzero image of a lowering component would give π a compact type with smaller value of ‖μ + 2ρc‖² than at μ = Λ, contradicting its minimality. The uniqueness result for derivatives now applies to the difference.

From the identity to the whole ray. Fix X and replace it by tX. On the integral side the minors depend analytically on t. At each real t their positive minimum over K permits integration of a locally convergent power series. On the representation side, the analytic-vector estimate for the lowest type gives constants B, C ≥ 0 with ‖dπ(X)ku‖ ≤ Ck(B+k)k‖u‖. Division by k! gives a Taylor series of positive radius, and unitarity gives the same derivative bound after translating along the ray. Hence the scalar difference is real analytic on ℝ. Each of its derivatives at 0 is a linear combination of the ordered derivatives already shown to vanish. The Taylor series is zero on a neighbourhood of 0. If this zero interval had a finite endpoint, continuity of every derivative and the convergent Taylor series there would extend it. The difference is therefore zero at t = 1, proving the formula.

A two-coordinate check. For U(1,1) take λ = (241/2, −241/2), so the gap 241 exceeds the required 240. The compact group U(1) × U(1) has one-dimensional types. With u = v = w, the two compact coefficients multiply to 1. Take X with zeros on the diagonal and ones off the diagonal. The leading minor of exp(tX) is cosh t and its determinant is 1, even after compact conjugation. The integral is (cosh t)−242. Its value at zero is 1, its first derivative is zero, and its second derivative is −242. At t = log 2 it equals (4/5)242. This example tests the compression formula in rank one. The subsequent period estimates impose their own dimension conditions.
