How does the local length give a coordinate for the torsion?

Suppose the count at the identity gives local length ℓ > Q. A field-valued point sees only the residue of a function. We need to recover the original linear coefficient from the term of degree Q in a Qth power. We first construct the ring in which to evaluate functions.

Local coordinate lemma. Let B be a finite-dimensional commutative local algebra over a field k, with maximal ideal 𝔪, residue field k via the structure map, and a one-dimensional tangent space. Choose x in 𝔪 with nonzero class in 𝔪/𝔪². If ℓ is the length of B over itself, then

k[X]/(Xℓ) ≅ B,   X ↦ x.

Proof. The dual-number description of tangent vectors identifies the cotangent space 𝔪/𝔪² with the dual of the tangent line. The class of x spans 𝔪/𝔪². Nakayama’s lemma, applied to the finite B-module 𝔪, gives 𝔪 = (x). The Stacks Project, Lemma 33.16.5 and Nakayama’s lemma.

Finite dimension makes B Artinian, so its maximal ideal is nilpotent. Let h be the first positive exponent with xh = 0. Write b as its residue plus xb₁ and repeat with b₁. After h steps this expresses b as a polynomial of degree less than h in x. These expressions are unique. In a nonzero polynomial relation of degree less than h, factor out the lowest power Xa. The remaining polynomial has nonzero constant term and so evaluates to a unit. This would give xa = 0 with a < h. Thus B ≅ k[X]/(Xh). Each successive quotient (xa)/(xa+1) for 0 ≤ a < h is a copy of the simple module k. This gives ℓ = h. The Stacks Project, finite-dimensional algebras and nilpotence in Artinian rings.

Now put T = k[t]/(tQ+1). Since ℓ ≥ Q + 1, sending x to t defines a surjection u ∶ B → T. In T, the class tQ still survives. For instance, a local coordinate of length nine gives a quotient of length four when Q = 3. This quotient is all the next calculation needs.

Where does the first derivative go under a Qth power?

Let k have prime characteristic q, let Q = qf with f ≥ 1, and write Δ = k[ε]/(ε²). The quotient u just constructed gives a point of the torsion group with values in T. Restricting it to Δ gives a tangent vector. The following two ring maps track the vector through Frobenius.

τ ∶ T → Δ,   t ↦ ε,  j ∶ Δ → T,   a + bε ↦ aQ + bQtQ.

The map τ fixes k. The map j acts on its coefficients by the fth iterate of Frobenius. It is additive because raising a sum to a qth power distributes in characteristic q. It respects multiplication because (tQ)² = 0 in T. Since 1 and tQ are linearly independent over k and a field has no nonzero nilpotents, j is injective. The Stacks Project, absolute Frobenius.

Write z = a + bt + t²c(t) in T. Its Qth power retains only the terms in degrees zero and Q. Thus

j(τ(z)) = zQ,   τ(j(a + bε)) = aQ.

For a coefficient example, take Q = 3 and k = 𝔽₃(ω), where ω² = −1. This field has nine elements since −1 is a nonsquare in 𝔽₃. If z = 1 + ωt + t² then τ(z) = 1 + ωε. Cubing gives j(τ(z)) = 1 − ωt³ because ω³ = −ω and t⁶ = 0. The quadratic coefficient has disappeared. The linear coefficient can still be recovered because Frobenius is injective on k.

The restriction τ ∘ u sends the local coordinate x to ε. Its derivative is therefore nonzero. The original T-point factors through the torsion group, so every element of 𝔴 kills it. These two properties and the identity j ∘ τ = Frobf give the lifted tangent used in the proof that the image tangent is constant. The strict threshold matters here. If ℓ = Q, every lift would satisfy u(x)Q = 0. The identity and injectivity of j would then force τ(u(x)) = 0.
