Which finite adelic coordinates represent the same lattice?

Removing the hyperbolic plane in the isotropic count leaves a nondegenerate Hermitian space W of dimension s = M − 2 over the CM extension E/F. Its isometries form a group H = U(W,h). Put Γ = H(F), the rational isometries. An adelic isometry has an archimedean coordinate in H∞ and a finite coordinate in Hf. We first show that, up to Γ, only finitely many integral lattice classes occur in the finite coordinate.

Fix coordinates, a reference lattice at the finite places, and its compact open stabilizer K ⊂ Hf. For y ∈ Hf, take the rational vectors in the translated local lattices and multiply them by one fixed nonzero integer to obtain L(y) ⊂ W. This is a finitely generated additive group stable under 𝒪E and spanning W. The finite lattice construction gives two identities

L(γfy) = γL(y),   L(y) = L(y′) ⇔ y−1y′ ∈ K.

The second identity uses recovery of the translated local lattices from their rational vectors by completion. Isometries preserve the pairing, so one integer suffices to make it integral on every translated lattice. The coordinates of the lattices themselves can have arbitrarily large denominators. Clearing denominators in the pairing and its inverse gives one bound D for all y. Explicitly, if L# = {w ∈ W ∣ h(w,L) ⊂ 𝒪E}, then L ⊂ L# and [L# ∶ L] ≤ D. Indeed, one positive integer b sends every such dual into its lattice. A full lattice of additive rank k then satisfies L ⊂ L# ⊂ b−1L and [b−1L ∶ L] = bk.

Finiteness of the lattice classes. In a fixed nondegenerate space, integral lattices with this dual index bound belong to finitely many rational isometry classes. Here is the reduction. Choose a positive Euclidean length that bounds the absolute Hermitian pairings at all archimedean places. When every nonzero lattice vector has length at least 1, lattice reduction with the bounded covolume gives bounded generators. Their Gram entries are algebraic integers bounded at every embedding, a finite set. The Gram entries of spanning generators determine an isometry taking one labelled set to another. If a vector has length less than 1, its Hermitian norm is an algebraic integer of absolute value less than 1 at every embedding. Its field norm forces it to vanish. Split off a hyperbolic plane through the resulting line. Reduction adapted to that line gives finitely many possibilities for the lattices in the two line coordinates and a bounded dual index in the smaller orthogonal complement. Induction gives finitely many models there. For each resulting full split lattice B, the adapted reduction puts the original lattice between B and n−1B for a fixed positive integer n. The finite quotient n−1B/B has only finitely many subgroups.

Choose a representative t for each class attained by L(y). If L(y) = γL(t), the two identities give y ∈ γftK. Thus the double classes Γf ∖ Hf / K are finite. Within the slice tK, exactly the following rational isometries can still identify two points

Γt = {γ ∈ Γ ∣ t−1γft ∈ K} = {γ ∈ Γ ∣ γL(t) = L(t)}.

Gluing the finite slices into a fundamental domain

Take one t for each double class. For now suppose the archimedean image of Γt has a fundamental domain Dt of finite Haar measure in H∞. The next step proves this for the lattice stabilizers above. Inside H∞ × Hf take

𝒟 = ⋃t Dt × tK.

Given a pair (x,y), move y into tK by a rational isometry. An element of Γt can now move the new archimedean coordinate into Dt while keeping the finite coordinate in tK. Conversely, a rational translate of one rectangle can meet another only in the same double class. In that class, the translating element belongs to Γt. Its archimedean action is faithful, and distinct translates of Dt are disjoint up to measure zero. These statements about coverage and overlap hold almost everywhere. Γ is countable, since its matrices have entries in a number field, so the union of the translated exceptional sets is still null.

Fix factor Haar measures μ∞ and μf. Their product is a Haar measure on the adelic group. For each rectangle its volume is μ∞(Dt) μf(K), because left translation by t preserves μf. This also shows why an archimedean exceptional null set stays null inside each slice. The finite sum gives

vol(𝒟) = μf(K) ∑t μ∞(Dt) < ∞.

Haar uniqueness says that all left Haar measures on a locally compact group differ by a positive factor. Thus the same construction gives finite volume for every choice of adelic Haar measure. A. Weil, Mesure de Haar, §§4–7.

Why the smaller unitary quotient has finite positive volume

It remains to verify the archimedean input. The stabilizer Γt is the integral subgroup of the rational algebraic group obtained from U(h) by restriction of scalars to ℚ, using the underlying integer lattice L(t). The arithmetic finite-volume theorem of Borel and Harish-Chandra says that a connected algebraic group over ℚ with trivial rational character group has an arithmetic quotient of finite invariant volume. Here the character condition can be checked directly. Over ℂ the group becomes a product of general linear groups, one for each real place of F. A character on each factor is an integral power of its determinant. Complex conjugation acts on each determinant by inversion, because the defining isometry equation identifies the conjugate matrix with a conjugate of its inverse transpose. A rational character would have every exponent equal to its negative, hence zero. The theorem gives the required finite archimedean covolume. A. Borel and Harish-Chandra, Arithmetic Subgroups of Algebraic Groups, theorem 9.4, p. 522.

One can also see where the volume bound enters reduction by short vectors. For an archimedean isometry g measure vectors with the positive length of g−1x. Where the shortest nonzero vector has length ≥ 1, bounded-basis reduction puts representatives in a compact set. Its finite measure bounds this portion of a fundamental domain. Where the shortest length is < 1, choose a shortest vector x. The norm argument above makes it isotropic. Shortest-vector reduction bounds [L ∩ Ex ∶ 𝒪Ex] by a constant depending on the field. Count all nonzero isotropic vectors satisfying that index bound and having length < 1. Their count is at least one on this thin part. The arithmetic unfolding theorem for bounded-index isotropic vectors gives a finite integral of that count over a fundamental domain. Its proof uses finitely many line orbits, parabolic coordinates and finite covolume for stabilizers in smaller dimension. The integral therefore bounds the volume of the thin part, as required.

For the same Haar measure μ, any two fundamental domains 𝒟 and 𝒟′ have the same mass. Partition 𝒟′ by its intersections with γ𝒟 and move each piece back by γ−1. The resulting pieces partition 𝒟 up to null sets. The common mass is positive because countably many translates of a null set could not cover a group with nonzero Haar measure. Thus replacing μ by cμ multiplies this mass by c. For s = M − 2 the isotropic shell estimate uses the smaller quotient volume as a fixed finite factor. Division by the volume of the ambient quotient takes place in the definition of the mean.

A one-dimensional complement. Take M = 3 over ℚ(i)/ℚ, with a hyperbolic plane and the line of norm zz̄. The smaller group at infinity is the circle. Choose K to be the norm-one integral units at the finite places. A norm-one finite idele determines a fractional ideal I with IĪ = (1). Gaussian integers admit Euclidean division, so I = (α). The positive rational number αᾱ generates the unit ideal and must equal 1. Dividing the idele by this rational isometry puts it in K. There is one double class. Its rational stabilizer consists of the four Gaussian units, so a quarter-circle gives an archimedean domain. With circle mass 1 and μf(K) = 1 the adelic covolume is 1/4. Tripling the adelic Haar measure gives 3/4.
