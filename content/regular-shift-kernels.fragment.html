How is the kernel defined before any representation exists?

The regular models are built from a function on G alone. At a shifted parameter no discrete series is given in advance, so the compression formula cannot be read as an identity between two known coefficients. Its right-hand side becomes a definition. Fix a parameter λ with the lattice condition, the integers μa = λa + (m+1−2a)/2 with μm+1 = 0, and the minor product Φ(Z) = ∏a Δa(Z)−(μa−μa+1). In other words μ = λ + ρ with ρa = (m+1−2a)/2. Let T be an irreducible unitary representation of K on a space of dimension d, with a unit vector w of highest weight Λ = λ + ρn − ρc, the lowest compact weight. Write P for orthogonal projection onto ℂw and give K Haar measure of mass one. Wherever every compact conjugate k−1xk has nonzero leading minors, set

A(x) = d ∫K Φ(k−1xk) T(k)PT(k)−1 dk.

Replacing k by ck in the integral gives A(cxc−1) = T(c)A(x)T(c)−1 for c ∈ K. This uses only invariance of the measure. At x = 1 every minor equals 1. The average of the projections commutes with T, hence is a scalar by Schur’s lemma, and its trace is 1 because each projection has trace 1. The factor d therefore gives A(1) = I.

From positive matrices to the group. Every g ∈ G factors as g = k exp X with k ∈ K and X Hermitian satisfying XJ = −JX, by the polar decomposition. The factors are unique. Indeed g*g = exp(2X), the exponential is injective on Hermitian matrices, and then k = g exp(−X). Define

Ψ(k exp X) = T(k) A(exp X).

For k1, k2 ∈ K the factorization of k1gk2 is (k1kk2) exp(k2−1Xk2), because k2−1Xk2 is again Hermitian and anticommutes with J. The conjugation rule for A then gives

Ψ(k1gk2) = T(k1) Ψ(g) T(k2),   Ψ(1) = I.

A symmetry and positivity on the noncompact part. The sign matrix J belongs to K and commutes with every element of K, so T(J) is a scalar. Since J exp(X) J = exp(−X), the conjugation rule gives A(exp(−X)) = A(exp X). Now exp X = exp(X/2)* exp(X/2) is positive definite, and so is each compact conjugate. Its leading principal submatrices are positive definite, hence every leading minor is a positive real number and Φ(k−1 exp(X) k) > 0. Moreover ⟨v,T(k)PT(k)−1v⟩ = |⟨T(k)w,v⟩|². Integrating, Ψ(exp X) is a positive semidefinite operator. This positivity is used when a shifted model is placed in the tensor product.

For example, take U(1,1) with signs (+,−), so K consists of the matrices diag(eiα,eiβ) and ρc = 0. Then Λ = μ = (λ1+1/2, λ2−1/2), d = 1 and T(diag(eiα,eiβ)) = ei(Λ1α+Λ2β). For X with zero diagonal and both off-diagonal entries t, every compact conjugate of exp X has first minor cosh t and determinant 1. Hence

Ψ(k1 exp(X) k2) = ei(Λ1(α1+α2)+Λ2(β1+β2)) (cosh t)−(λ1−λ2+1).

At λ = (3/2,−1/2) and t = log 2, the value at exp X is (4/5)³ = 64/125.

Why is the kernel smooth near every point of the group?

Differentiating Ψ through its polar factors would require the factorization to be smooth. The proof avoids this. It shows that near each point Ψ is a holomorphic function of g−1. The key is an identity for A at positive definite matrices, where the exponents of Φ and the weight of w must fit together. Write T(Y) also for the complex-linear differential of T on block diagonal matrices Y, those with Yab = 0 whenever a and b have opposite signs.

A Hermitian direction. Let x be positive definite and H block diagonal and Hermitian. The direction Hx + xH is the velocity of exp(tH) x exp(tH). Differentiate A under the integral. At a conjugate z = k−1xk put B = k−1Hk. Let L keep the entries of B below the diagonal, change the sign of those above it, and vanish on the diagonal. Then B + L is lower triangular, B − L is upper triangular, both have diagonal entries Baa, and

Bz + zB = (zL − Lz) + (B+L)z + z(B−L).

Left multiplication by a lower triangular matrix and right multiplication by an upper triangular one rescale each leading minor by the product of the leading diagonal entries. Hence each of the last two directions changes Φ at the rate −∑μaBaaΦ. The matrix L is block diagonal and skew-Hermitian, so the first direction is an infinitesimal compact conjugation. On the representation side the highest weight supplies a matching identity,

T(B)P + PT(B) = [T(L),P] + 2(∑ΛaBaa) P.

Indeed the part of B above the diagonal kills w, the diagonal part multiplies w by ∑ΛaBaa, and T(Y)* = T(Y*) moves the part below the diagonal to the other side of P.

The correction from a moving frame. The infinitesimal conjugation by L does not cancel by itself, because L depends on k. Write it as a combination of derivatives along the right translations k ↦ k exp(tEab), with a ≠ b of the same sign. Each such derivative of a continuous function on K integrates to zero, since the right translates of the integral do not depend on t. The coefficients Bab also move under these translations. Their derivatives contribute −4∑a ρc(a)Baa times the integrand. Here 2ρc(a) is the number of later minus the number of earlier positions with the sign of a. Collecting the scalar terms, the identity after integration holds exactly when μa = Λa + 2ρc(a) for every a. This is the relation between λ + ρ and λ + ρn − ρc, since ρ = ρn + ρc coordinatewise. We obtain

dA(x)(Hx + xH) = −(T(H)A(x) + A(x)T(H)).

One-sided identities. For a skew-Hermitian block diagonal Y, differentiating the conjugation rule gives dA(x)(xY − Yx) = A(x)T(Y) − T(Y)A(x). The Hermitian identity for H = iY, divided by i, gives the corresponding sum. Adding and subtracting, then extending complex-linearly to every block diagonal Y,

dA(x)(xY) = −T(Y)A(x),   dA(x)(Yx) = −A(x)T(Y).

These hold at positive definite x. For z close to such a matrix write z = Z1 + iZ2 with Z1, Z2 Hermitian. Along s ↦ Z1 + sZ2 the defect of each identity is holomorphic for |s| < 2. It vanishes for real s, where the matrix stays positive definite, and hence at s = i by uniqueness of analytic continuation. The identities therefore hold on an open neighbourhood U of the positive definite matrices.

The local formula. By the first identity, t ↦ T(exp tY)A(z exp tY) has derivative zero while the path stays in U. Fix g0 = k0 exp X0. For g = k exp X near g0, write k−1k0 = exp Y with Y small and skew-Hermitian. The polar factors depend continuously on g, so the path exp(−X) exp(tY), 0 ≤ t ≤ 1, stays in U. Together with A(exp(−X)) = A(exp X) this gives

Ψ(g) = T(k)A(exp X) = T(k0) A(g−1k0),   g−1k0 = exp(−X) exp(Y).

The right side is a holomorphic function of the matrix g on a neighbourhood of g0. Its restriction to G is therefore smooth. Only continuity of the polar factors was used.

A numerical check exercises the moving-frame correction, which is absent on U(1,1) because there ρc = 0. On U(2,1) with signs (+,+,−) and λ = (3,1,−2) one has ρc = (1/2,−1/2,0), Λ = (3,2,−3), μ = (4,1,−3) and d = 2. Quadrature over U(2) at a random positive definite matrix reproduces both one-sided identities to the accuracy of the finite differences. Replacing μ by Λ makes the defect as large as A itself.

Why do the Casimir operators act on the kernel through λ?

The realization theorem needs Ψ to be an eigenfunction of the Casimir operator, and the residue argument needs the whole central character. Both statements are proved first for holomorphic functions of a matrix and then transported to G. On matrices z with nonzero leading minors put LXF(z) = dF(z)(Xz), the derivative of F(etXz) at t = 0. Because the variable is multiplied on the left, [LX,LY] = LYX−XY.

The minor product is an extremal vector. Let c be lower triangular and c′ upper triangular, both with nonzero diagonal. The leading block of czc′ is the product of the three leading blocks, so Δa(czc′) = ∏l≤a cllc′ll Δa(z). The exponents of Φ telescope to Φ(czc′) = ∏l(cllc′ll)−μl Φ(z). Differentiating along c = 1 + tEab gives

LEabΦ = 0 for a > b,   LEaaΦ = −μaΦ.

Now compute LEabLEbaΦ. If a < b, the inner operator already kills Φ on a neighbourhood. If a = b, the result is μa²Φ. If a > b, exchange the two operators. The exchanged product vanishes, and the commutator is L applied to Ebb − Eaa, which multiplies Φ by μa − μb. Position a has a − 1 predecessors and m − a successors. Summing over all pairs therefore gives ∑μa² − ∑(m+1−2a)μa = |μ − ρ|² − |ρ|², that is,

∑a,b LEabLEbaΦ = (|λ|² − |ρ|²) Φ.

The compact average. For c ∈ K the function z ↦ Φ(c−1zc) satisfies the same equation. Indeed the tensor ∑Eab ⊗ Eba is unchanged when both factors are conjugated by the same invertible matrix. The set of z for which every compact conjugate has nonzero minors is open because K is compact, and it contains every positive definite matrix. On it, two differentiations under the integral show that A satisfies the equation as well. The projection P plays no role in this step.

Transport to the group. Write Eab also for the complex-linear extension of right differentiation on G. Every complex matrix Z splits as Z1 + iZ2 with Z1 = (Z − JZ*J)/2 and Z2 = (Z + JZ*J)/(2i) in the Lie algebra of G. Then E(Z) differentiates along Z1 and adds i times the derivative along Z2. By the local formula, Ψ(getX) = T(k0)A(e−tXg−1k0) near g0, so EabΨ(g) = −T(k0)(LEabA)(g−1k0). Applying this twice, the two signs cancel and the order of the factors is kept:

∑a,b EabEbaΨ = (|λ|² − |ρ|²) Ψ   on G.

No gap hypothesis has been used. Only the local formula needs μ = λ + ρ and Λ = λ + ρn − ρc.

All Gelfand elements. For k ≥ 0 let tr(Ek) be the sum of the products Ea1a2Ea2a3⋯Eaka1. The case k = 2 is the Casimir operator. Put lr = λr + (m−1)/2 and let γk(λ) be the coefficient of Xk+1 in 1 − ∏r(1 − X/(1 − lrX)). Then

tr(Ek)Ψ = γk(λ) Ψ,   tr((E∨)k)Ψ = γk(−λ) Ψ,

where E∨ab = −Em+1−b, m+1−a is the dual family reindexed by the order-reversing permutation. The proof has the same three steps. In the holomorphic step Φ is a highest-weight vector for two families satisfying the commutation relations of the Eab: the family −Em+1−a,m+1−b, with weight μ read backwards, and the transposed family, with weight −μ. On a highest-weight vector the diagonal entries of the matrix Ek act by scalars obeying a triangular recursion, whose sum is γk. Conjugating the family does not change tr(Ek), which takes care of the compact average. On the group each derivative contributes a sign, and the k signs cancel the sign (−1)k of the holomorphic eigenvalue.

The Gelfand elements commute with every Eab. Their scalar action therefore extends from the span of the coefficient functions fu(g) = ⟨w,Ψ(g)u⟩ to the module these functions generate under derivatives. Thus the generated module has infinitesimal character λ. This is the central-character input of the residue calculation. For λ = (3/2,−1/2) we get l = (2,0) and 1 − ∏ = 2X + X² + 2X³ + 4X⁴ + ⋯. Thus γ0 = 2 = m, γ1 = 1 = λ1 + λ2 and γ2 = 2 = |λ|² − |ρ|².

How fast does the kernel decay, and against which weights is it integrable?

Let h be the opposite-sign gap of λ and assume q ≥ 1. For X as above put t = ‖X‖F. Each conjugate k−1 exp(X) k equals exp(Y) with Y = k−1Xk. This matrix is again Hermitian, anticommutes with J and has Frobenius norm t. Its exponential has determinant 1, since Y has zero diagonal. The other exponents of Φ are μa − μa+1 = λa − λa+1 + 1, which are the weights of the minor-product bound. That bound therefore holds at every point of the average:

0 < Φ(k−1 exp(X) k) ≤ D(t) = exp(−(h/8) min{t², t/√(2q)}).

The kernel has its own proof of this bound. There the moments of the Cauchy–Binet weights come directly from determinants of rank-one and rank-two perturbations of the identity. The chain of inequalities is the one of the minor-product section. The operator A(exp X) averages the positive operators T(k)PT(k)−1 with positive weights at most D(t), and the same average with weight 1 is the identity. Hence 0 ≤ A(exp X) ≤ D(t)I as self-adjoint operators, and the norm is monotone on positive operators. Since T(k) is unitary,

‖Ψ(k exp X)‖ ≤ exp(−(h/8) min{t², t/√(2q)}).

No factor of d is lost. The kernel decays at the rate of its scalar factor.

Haar measure in polar coordinates. Parametrize the noncompact directions by the block B ∈ ℂp×q of entries between positive and negative positions. Let XB be the Hermitian matrix with that block. The polar map (k,X) ↦ k exp X is a bijection onto G. Transport dk ⊗ 𝒥(X)dX along it, where 𝒥(X) = ∏a<b sinh(ya−yb)/(ya−yb) over the eigenvalues y of X, each factor being 1 when the difference vanishes. The image is invariant under right translation. This reduces to the change of variables for the action of G on the cosets Kg, which the noncompact directions parametrize. It is also invariant under inversion, which sends (k,X) to (k−1,−kXk−1). Hence it is a Haar measure. Every Haar measure is a positive multiple of it. Each ratio sinh u/u is at most cosh u ≤ e|u|. The matrix X has rank at most 2q, and the squares of its eigenvalues add up to t². By Cauchy–Schwarz, ∑|ya| ≤ √(2q) t. Finally ∑a<b|ya−yb| ≤ (m−1)∑|ya|. Consequently, for every Haar measure ν there is a constant C such that every measurable f ≥ 0 satisfies

∫G f dν ≤ C ∫ℂp×q (∫K f(k exp XB) dk) em√(2q) ‖XB‖F dB.

Three thresholds. Put c = √(2q) and r = ‖X‖F. Expanding (r − 1/(2c))² ≥ 0 gives min{r², r/c} ≥ r/c − 1/(4c²), so ‖Ψ(k exp X)‖ ≤ eh/(32c²) e−hr/(8c). After paying the growth factor emcr, a function bounded by a constant times e−δr with δ > 0 is integrable over the finite-dimensional block space. This leaves three conditions.

  • The kernel is integrable when h/(8c) > mc, that is h > 16mq.
  • Its square decays at twice the rate, so the kernel is square integrable when h > 8mq.
  • The exponential series gives ‖k exp X‖F² = ‖exp X‖F² ≤ m e2r. For s ≥ 0 the function ‖Ψ(g)‖² ‖g‖Fs is therefore integrable when h/(4c) − s > mc, that is h > 8mq + 4s√(2q).

The construction of the regular models takes s = 2. It assumes h > max{2m, 16mq + 8√(2q)}, which gives all three conditions. These thresholds are not sharp. On U(1,1) the exact density is proportional to sinh(2t) dt dθ, so the kernel (cosh t)−(λ1−λ2+1) is integrable as soon as λ1 − λ2 + 1 > 2. The general argument asks for h > 32 there.

Weighted coefficients of the generated vectors. The signed column pairing assumes that ‖g‖F⟨y,R(g)x⟩ is square integrable for all vectors x, y of the module generated by the coefficient functions fu. Here R is right translation on L²(G). For two coefficient functions the realization theorem gives ⟨fu′,R(g)fu⟩ = ‖fw‖²⟨u′,Ψ(g)u⟩. The third threshold with s = 2 then makes these coefficients square integrable with the weight ‖g‖F. A growth bound for smooth vectors carries the property from the span of the fu to all their derivatives. That span is finite-dimensional, stable under the compact derivatives and an eigenspace of the Casimir operator. The bound integrates by parts against cutoffs and uses that every right derivative of log ‖g‖F² is at most twice the Frobenius norm of its direction. The substitution g ↦ g−1 exchanges the two vectors, because g−1 = Jg*J has the same Frobenius norm and Haar measure is invariant under inversion. Applying the growth bound in each slot covers every pair of generated vectors.

Why does the column differential have finitely many eigenvalues?

Let M be the module generated by the coefficient functions of the kernel, and consider columns f = (f1,…,fm) with entries in M. Write x[c] for the column whose only nonzero coordinate is x, in position c. On V* ⊗ π the matrix Eab acts by (Tabf)c = Eabfc − δcbfa. The column differential of the residue calculation is (Df)r = −∑s Ersfs. Expanding the tensor Casimir gives ∑TabTba = (Casimir on each entry) + m + 2D. The Casimir acts on M by the scalar |λ|² − |ρ|², so D commutes with every Tab. The space of columns is infinite-dimensional. Three facts nevertheless give a polynomial in D that vanishes on it.

A finite-dimensional stable piece. Let V be the span of the coefficient functions, a copy of the lowest compact type, and let Vm be the columns with entries in V. Each diagonal operator Tcc commutes with D. Hence the compact weights occurring in the spaces Dj(Vm) lie in a finite set, fixed by the minimal polynomials of the Tcc on Vm. Organize M by derivative layers. The zeroth layer is V, and the kth adds one noncompact derivative to the previous layer. The Casimir gap shows that a compact highest-weight vector of a given weight lies in a layer whose index is bounded by the compact Casimir difference divided by 2(h − m + 1). Choose a layer L containing every compact highest-weight vector of M whose weight is an allowed weight shifted by a unit vector. Suppose Dj(Vm) had a component orthogonal to Lm. Projecting away from Lm is compact-equivariant, so that component would contain a compact highest-weight vector. Its extreme nonzero coordinate would be a compact highest-weight vector of M of a shifted allowed weight. It would lie in L while being orthogonal to L, which is impossible. Thus the sum W of all Dj(Vm) is finite-dimensional and stable under D.

A positive invariant form. For columns f, f′ put Q(f,f′) = ∑∫G conj(φ(f)) φ(f′) dg, where φ runs over the coefficients of the column action against y ⊗ eξ, with y in a basis of V and ξ a coordinate. Each coefficient is a sum of products of a matrix entry of (g−1)ᵗ with ⟨y,R(g)fc⟩. That entry is at most ‖g‖F in absolute value, so the weighted bound of the preceding section makes Q finite. It is positive definite. If Q(f,f) = 0 every coefficient vanishes, and then f = 0 because M lies in the closed span of the translates of V. Right invariance of Haar measure makes each Tab adjoint to ± Tba, with the sign of the product of the two coordinate signs. The two signs multiply to 1 in the tensor Casimir, so D is symmetric for Q.

The nodal polynomial. A symmetric operator for a positive definite form on the finite-dimensional space W has real eigenvalues and no nilpotent part. Let N(X) = ∏b∈B(X − b) over its spectrum B on W. Then N(D) kills W, which contains Vm. The kernel of N(D) is stable under every Tab. A space of columns stable under every Tab and containing Vm contains all columns, because (Eabx)[c] = Tab(x[c]) + δac x[b]. Hence N(D) = 0 on every column. Lagrange interpolation at the points of B writes each column as a finite sum of eigenvectors of D, and every eigenvalue of D lies in B. Only the quadratic Casimir was used. Identifying which numbers occur is the work of the residue calculation, which uses the whole central character.

The partial traces of the powers. The partial trace of an operator S on columns is the operator x ↦ ∑c(S(x[c]))c on M. The operator D multiplies columns by the matrix (−Ers), whose entries are operators on M. Hence Dk multiplies them by the kth power of that matrix, with the factors kept in order. Taking the partial trace gives the trace of this power, (−1)ktr(Ek). The central character evaluates it on M as (−1)kγk(λ). Put X = −z in the series defining γk. Since 1 + z/(1 + lsz) = 1 + z/(1 − asz) with as = −λs − (m−1)/2, this proves the identity used in the residue calculation:

ptr(Dk) = coefficient of zk+1 in ∏s(1 + z/(1 − asz)) − 1.

A finite-dimensional model shows the mechanism without any analysis. Take m = 2 and replace M by ℂ² with Eab the matrix units, so λ = (3/2,−1/2). Then D(ej[i]) = −δij ∑r er[r]. Columns are 2 × 2 matrices here. The eigenvalue −2 occurs on the line spanned by the identity matrix and 0 on the traceless matrices. These are −λs − 1/2 for s = 1, 2. The partial traces of Dk for k = 0, 1, 2, 3 are 2, −1, 2, −4. The series (1 + z/(1+2z))(1 + z) − 1 = 2z − z² + 2z³ − 4z⁴ + ⋯ has the same coefficients.
