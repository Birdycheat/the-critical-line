Why does each marking have only finitely many symmetries?

Fix a marked rational pair (β, γ) with multiplier in ℚ× and positive eigenline of eigenvalue μ. We use the unitary group of the comparison, where the distinguished infinite place is the unique one with positive index one. Regularity means that the minimal polynomial Pγ over the centre F is separable and has degree equal to the matrix rank n. It may have several irreducible factors. Let Γ be the group of rational similitudes of β commuting with γ. A trivialization of the form away from p gives a map ι1 from Γ to the group there. At p choose a positive unramified degree r and local coordinates giving the second map ι2. Take the prescribed compact open level K1 away from p and a subgroup K2 commensurable with the standard integral level at p. For each pair of markings represented by y = (y1, y2), the following group is finite.

Sy = {w ∈ Γ ∣ ι1(w) ∈ y1K1y1−1,   ι2(w) ∈ y2K2y2−1}.

Put M = F[γ] ≃ F[T]/(Pγ). Over a splitting field, a matrix commuting with γ preserves its n distinct eigenlines. Interpolation on the eigenvalues expresses every diagonal operator in that basis as a polynomial in γ. The centralizer thus has dimension n after extending scalars. Its defining equations are linear, so it already equals the n-dimensional algebra M over F. Each w ∈ Γ is determined by a unit z of M. The adjoint involution c of β restricts to M because it sends γ to its multiplier times γ−1. In particular z c(z) = ν(w) ∈ ℚ× determines the multiplier as well.

Two properties of these arithmetic coordinates are needed. First, the positive-line marking and the signature imply that every complex embedding τ of a field factor of M, viewed as a map on M, satisfies τ(c(z)) = τ(z). In fact the eigenvalue λ of γ seen by τ satisfies |λ|2 = ν(γ) by the marked-pair eigenvalue theorem. The involution sends λ to ν(γ)/λ, which is its complex conjugate, and on the centre it acts by complex conjugation. Together these elements generate M. Second, the local level bounds both z and z−1 in every finite embedding. To see how the latter bound is obtained at a prime q ≠ p, all powers of w still lie in Sy. Their rational matrix coordinates at q stay in a bounded set by the compact open level condition. Evaluation of M at an embedding into an algebraic closure of ℚq is a linear functional on these coordinates. Hence |τ(z)|qj is bounded independently of j, which forces |τ(z)|q ≤ 1. Apply the same argument to w−1.

At p the integral-level estimate first gives the bound for embeddings above the chosen place of the imaginary quadratic field, together with the bound on the rational multiplier. If τ lies above the conjugate place, τ′ = τ ∘ c lies above the chosen one. The identity τ(z) = ν(w)τ′(z−1) gives the missing bound. This step explains why both the similitude relation and the local estimate on the multiplier enter the proof.

Factor Pγ over F. Its factors are distinct, so Bézout's identity and the Chinese remainder theorem express M as a finite product of number fields. In each factor z and its inverse are algebraic integers. Indeed the coefficients of their monic minimal polynomials are rational and integral at every finite prime, being symmetric functions of roots with absolute value at most one there. The same holds for c(z) by composing embeddings with c. The rational number ν(w) and its inverse are therefore integers. At a complex embedding ν(w) = |τ(z)|2 is positive, hence it equals 1.

All conjugates of each coordinate of z now have absolute value one. For a monic integer polynomial of degree d with such roots, the coefficient of degree d − j has absolute value at most the binomial coefficient d choose j. The degrees here are bounded by dimℚ M, so only finitely many polynomials and hence only finitely many roots occur. The finite product of these choices bounds Sy. The finite-prime bounds matter even for ℚ(i). The number (2 + i)/(2 − i) and all its powers have complex absolute value one, but the positive powers have distinct negative valuations at the prime (2 − i) above 5.

How does a vanishing orbital sum remove a marked pair?

Keep the marked pair and take r = 1. Its orbital term is defined to be zero if its component at p has no Frobenius norm representative. When there is one, let δ denote the representative and θ the Frobenius action. Write γ1 for the component of γ away from p, G1 and G2 for the two groups in the count, and K = K1 × K2, with K2 the integral subgroup at p. The ordinary centralizer of γ1 and the twisted centralizer {t ∣ tδ = δθ(t)} have product T. The norm comparison puts the image Γ′ of Γ inside T, and the joint map Γ → Γ′ is injective.

For the Hecke test g away from p and the indicator φ of K2, the value at y is F(y) = g(y1−1γ1y1)φ(y2−1δθ(y2)). It is constant on T y K. On the right this uses bi-invariance of g and θ(K2) ⊆ K2. Assume first that only finitely many classes in Γ′ ∖ (G1 × G2) / K give a nonzero value. Their contribution is weighted by the order of the stabilizer just proved finite.

Group these classes by their image T y K. Write C0 = T ∩ K and Hy = T ∩ y K y−1. The markings over this image correspond to Γ′ ∖ T / Hy via t ↦ t y. Indeed t y K = t′ y K precisely when t′−1t belongs to Hy. Denote by mass(H) the sum on Γ′ ∖ T / H in which the class of t contributes 1/|Γ′ ∩ t H t−1|. The level is commensurable with each conjugate, so C0 and Hy have intersection of finite index in both. Their masses are related by

volC0(H) = [H ∶ H ∩ C0] / [C0 ∶ H ∩ C0],   mass(H) volC0(H) = mass(C0).

For a refinement J ⊆ H, the stabilizer of a class acts on the [H ∶ J] cosets of J in H. Each orbit contributes its size divided by the order of the original stabilizer, by the orbit–stabilizer formula. The sum of these contributions is [H ∶ J] times the original weight. Refine both H and C0 to their intersection to obtain the displayed relation. For example, an index-three subgroup of C0 has relative volume 1/3 and contributes three times the reference mass.

Thus each contributing T y K carries F(y) divided by the relative volume of Hy, times the single reference mass. Since T and K are products, their double cosets split into the two coordinates. Both the test value and the relative volume factor, the latter because indices multiply in a direct product. Summing first over each coordinate gives the one-class counting formula

∑[y] ∈ Γ′ ∖ (G1 × G2) / K F(y)/|Sy|  = mass(C0) · O(γ1, g) · Oθ(δ, φ).

Each orbital sum on the right uses the intersection of its own centralizer with its own level as volume one. If the finite support is nonempty, a contributing fibre has positive finite mass. Changing its level as above gives the same reference mass in conjugate coordinates, so the calculation is independent of the representatives. For empty support, the product of the two test values vanishes on every pair of coordinates. At least one of the two tests therefore vanishes on its whole orbit. The convention for the point term also covers infinite support. A sum over classes is defined here to have value zero if its summand has infinite support. Each stabilizer is finite and contains the identity, so its reciprocal weight preserves the support. This last case supplies a value by definition and makes no assertion about convergence of an infinite series.

Now suppose Pγ is reducible over F and take the Hecke test with cuspidal slices on the chosen split matrix factor away from p. At that factor the matrix for γ has characteristic polynomial equal to the image of Pγ. Indeed the central algebra splits into matrices of size n, and scalar extension preserves the linear independence of 1, γ, …, γn−1. Its minimal polynomial still has degree n, hence agrees with its characteristic polynomial by Cayley–Hamilton. Changing the trivialization conjugates this matrix. The nonconstant monic factors of Pγ retain their degrees and separability persists. Regularity also makes the away-from-p centralizer commutative. The vanishing theorem for adelic slices now gives O(γ1, g) = 0. The displayed formula removes this marked pair regardless of the twisted orbital factor.

In the comparison of fixed points with definite pairs, the test also has support with separable characteristic polynomial at a specified auxiliary matrix place. This support condition is checked for the assembled Hecke test. In the vanishing argument for an individual marked pair, the separability used at the cuspidal place follows directly from its own regular polynomial.
