Which primes can test the two lifts?

The two lifts must be compared at enough places to determine a representation of the entire Galois group. We first specify the places using the field alone. Fix a CM field E, its maximal real subfield E⁺ and a positive integer d for which E contains an element x with x² = −d. For a finite place w of E, write q for the rational prime below it and c for complex conjugation. The set used in the comparison is

Qd = {w : cw ≠ w and −d is a square in ℚq}.

The first condition says that the prime below w in E⁺ has two distinct primes above it in E. The second concerns a square root in the rational completion. A prime of absolute residue degree one has residue field 𝔽q, or equivalently absolute norm q. We claim that every such w with q ∤ 2d belongs to Qd.

Indeed x is integral since it solves the monic equation X² + d = 0. Under any complex embedding its image is purely imaginary, so cx = −x. Choose an integer a reducing to the same element of 𝔽q as x. If cw = w, both x − a and −x − a lie in w. Their difference gives 2x ∈ w and multiplication by −x gives 2d ∈ w, contrary to the choice of q. Also a² + d ≡ 0 modulo q, with 2a nonzero modulo q. Hensel’s lemma lifts this simple root to ℤq. Both conditions follow. Sutherland, Lemma 9.15.

A split prime that fails the square test. Take E = ℚ(√5, i) and d = 1. The prime 3 is inert in ℚ(√5) since 5 is a nonsquare modulo 3. Its residue field is 𝔽₉, where (√5)² = 2 = −1. The two simple roots ±√5 of X² + 1 lift over that real field’s completion, giving two places in E. At each of them the first condition holds, while −1 has no square root in ℚ₃ since it has none in 𝔽₃. At 29 both 5 and −1 have simple square roots, 11 and 12 modulo 29. Every place above 29 has degree one. The first lifting step sends 12 to 41, with 41² + 1 = 2 · 29².

Why does a single automorphic vector give Satake data at almost every split place?

Fix the source representation U of the adelic unitary group and choose a nonzero smooth automorphic vector φ in it. Its stabilizer at the finite adeles is open. To see what openness gives, view a matrix g through the entries of both g and g−1. Around the identity, choose additive neighborhoods of the entries small enough that membership in all of them puts g in the stabilizer. There are only finitely many entries. Their neighborhoods contain a common neighborhood N of zero for the entry differences. Shrink once to a neighborhood N′ with N′ + N′ ⊆ N.

A basic neighborhood of zero in the finite adeles allows every integral value outside a finite set of coordinates. Thus an integral entry supported at just one place w belongs to N′ for all but finitely many w. Exclude also the conjugates of those exceptions. For a split pair w, cw, put Kw = GLn(𝒪Ew). A local matrix from Kw acts on the conjugate coordinate by the conjugate inverse transpose in the standard unitary model. Its entries and those of its inverse are integral at both places. Every entry of either matrix minus the identity is the sum of at most two integral entries supported at single places. It belongs to N′ + N′. Consequently every element of this local compact group fixes φ.

w, cw outside a finite set   ⟹   Kw φ = {φ}   at split w.

The automorphic representation has irreducible admissible local factors in its restricted tensor product. At a split place the local unitary factor is GLn(Ew). The local component theorem supplies an embedded irreducible smooth representation σ of this factor. Translations by the other factors commute with it, so they carry this copy to other copies of σ. Their span is invariant under the full group. Irreducibility of U makes the span all of U. In particular, φ lies in a finite sum of these copies. D. Flath, Theorem 2 on restricted products and J. Getz, Theorem 7.5 for the finite adèles.

To see why at least one copy contains a fixed vector, add the copies in order until their sum first contains φ. Write W for the preceding sum and V for the final copy. The local group preserves W ∩ V. Irreducibility of V makes this intersection zero, since V ⊆ W would leave φ outside their sum. The projection of φ from W ⊕ V onto V is therefore nonzero and fixed by Kw. Thus σ is spherical. The Satake isomorphism identifies its spherical Hecke character with an unordered multiset of n nonzero complex parameters. This supplies the source multiset at every split place outside a finite set depending on φ. Satake, Theorem 2 on spherical functions and Theorem 3 on the spherical transform.

Can these places realize any finite Galois test?

Let Γ = Gal(Ω/E) for an algebraic closure Ω. An open neighborhood of an element g in the Krull topology contains all automorphisms agreeing with g on some finite Galois extension L/E. Several simultaneous restrictions are one such test after taking the compositum of the fields involved. Write h for the restriction of g to L. Density amounts to producing an inverse Frobenius with restriction exactly h, even after an arbitrary finite set S of places of E has been discarded.

V(L,h) = {g′ ∈ Γ : g′|L = h}.

Apply Chebotarev to the conjugacy class of h−1 in Gal(L/E). The unramified places it gives have positive Dirichlet density. Those of absolute residue degree at least two have Dirichlet density zero. Indeed their sum of (Nw)−s is bounded for real s > 1 by [E∶ℚ] ∑q prime q−2, since Nw ≥ q² and at most [E∶ℚ] places lie over q. Dividing by log(1/(s−1)) and letting s decrease to 1 proves the assertion. We may therefore choose a degree-one place outside S and the finitely many places over 2dℓ. By the square-root argument this place belongs to Qd. Sutherland, Theorem 28.9 (Chebotarev).

At such a place w, changing the prime of L above w conjugates its arithmetic Frobenius. Choose it so that Frobenius is h−1 itself. Extend the prime to Ω and take a lift σ of the residue-field map z ↦ zNw in its decomposition group. Since L/E is unramified at w, all these lifts have the same restriction there. Now σ−1 lies in V(L,h). The freedom to choose the prime over w is what turns a conjugacy class into an element of the specified open set. Sutherland, Proposition 7.18 on the Frobenius class.

Two witnesses for one test. Continue with E = ℚ(√5, i) and take L = E(ζ₇), d = 1 and ℓ = 13. Ask for the inverse Frobenius that sends ζ₇ to ζ₇³. At 61 and 89 both 5 and −1 are nonzero squares modulo the prime. For 61 one may take the roots 26 and 11 respectively, and for 89 the roots 19 and 34. Thus both give places of norm the rational prime. In the residue field of L, arithmetic Frobenius raises ζ₇ to the 61st or 89th power, respectively. Both exponents are 5 modulo 7, whose inverse is 3. Reduction distinguishes seventh roots of unity away from 7 because the roots of X⁷ − 1 remain distinct. After excluding all places over 61, a place over 89 still realizes the same test.

Absolute degree one gives enough witnesses for the density proof. Some places of degree two also pass the good-place test. For example 5 is a nonsquare modulo 17 and 4² ≡ −1 modulo 17. A place above 17 has norm 17², splits over the real subfield and belongs to Q1. Its inverse exponent is 4 modulo 7, so it answers a different finite Galois test. Where do both lifts have the same source parameters?

Fix the two lifts, their common twist κ and the finite lists of auxiliary Hecke characters ψ1,i and ψ2,i. The odd-rank attachment theorem supplies a continuous semisimple representation ρj for each lift. For all but finitely many places in Qd, each ρj already matches the normalized Satake parameters of its lift whenever those parameters exist. This compatibility is an input from the odd-rank construction. We now show that all the hypotheses for applying it to both lifts hold together on a dense set of inverse Frobenius elements.

Each of the finitely many characters is unramified outside a finite set, as is κ. Here is the reason for finiteness. A neighborhood of 1 in the idele group contains every single-place integral unit outside finitely many places, along with every power of that unit. By continuity we can choose the neighborhood so that all its character values lie at distance less than ½ from 1. A complex number with all of its integral powers in this disk must be 1. Its modulus must first be 1 by taking positive and negative powers. For a different point of the unit circle the averages of its first N powers tend to zero by the geometric sum formula, whereas numbers in the disk have real part greater than ½. Thus the character is trivial on the entire local unit group at every remaining place.

Collect in one finite set the places above ℓ, the conductors of all these characters, the exceptions for both odd-rank matches, and the finite exceptional set for sphericity of the source. Outside this union each w ∈ Qd supplies a source Satake multiset Bw. The two lift identities use that very same multiset. Write Bwalg for the result of multiplying each entry by (Nw)(n−1)/2, and Sw for the result of multiplying each entry by κ(w)−1. On applying the fixed coefficient identification, the eigenvalue multiset of ρj at inverse Frobenius is

Sw ⊎ {ψj,1(w), …, ψj,t(w)},   j = 1, 2.

The density result allows this whole finite union to be discarded at once. Adding the other list to each side therefore gives equal polynomials on a dense set. The character-transfer argument extends equality to Γ and isolates the auxiliary lines. The exclusions here serve the density argument. At a particular target place, including one above ℓ, the stronger local conclusion uses the separately prescribed data there and the final untwisting step.
