How can the error shrink with the exceptional main term?

Write d = log m, L = log U, and φ = φ(m). When an exceptional pair exists, set η = min{1, (1−β)d} and s = χ(a) ∈ {−1,1}. In its absence take η = 1, s = 0 and β = 1. The difficulty occurs at s = 1. As β approaches one, both the expected number of primes and the permissible error in this class shrink. We need the same factor η in two estimates.

Lower bound for the corrected main term. Suppose U ≥ 16 and L ≥ d. Then B ≥ P, where

P = Uη/(2φL).

Indeed β ≥ ½ and Uβ ≥ 2. If s ≤ 0, integrating 1/log t from U/2 to U already gives φB ≥ U/(2L). If s = 1, integrate from Uβ to U. Throughout this interval 1/log t ≥ 1/L. Its length is U[1−exp(−(1−β)L)] ≥ Uη/2. Here (1−β)L ≥ η and 1−exp(−η) ≥ η/2 for 0 ≤ η ≤ 1, by concavity and the endpoint values. This gives the bound for both signs.

The corresponding saving in the error. Let Ψ(t) sum log p for each prime power pk ≤ t congruent to a modulo m. Its model is F(t) = (t − s tβ/β)/φ. The analytic input, obtained by summing the errors for Dirichlet characters and applying their orthogonality, gives constants A, c > 0 such that

|Ψ(t) − F(t)| ≤ A tη[exp(−c log t/d) + exp(−c√(log t))]/φ.

This holds for 8 ∣ m, gcd(a,m) = 1 and log t ≥ C d log(d+2) with an absolute C large enough. To see where η enters for large moduli, recall that the contribution from a zero ρ, after dividing the weighted explicit formula by t, contains tRe ρ−1. A very close exceptional zero forces the remaining zeros farther to the left by the Deuring–Heilbronn theorem. The density estimate bounds how many such zeros must be counted, with their multiplicities. After the exceptional zero has been set aside, these two inputs bound the relevant weighted zero sum by a constant times η dN exp(−c′ log t/d) for fixed N and c′ > 0. The remaining power of d can be paid for explicitly. Once log t ≥ (2N/c′)d log(d+2), we have dN exp(−c′ log t/(2d)) ≤ 1. Half the exponential decay and the whole factor η survive. For d at most a fixed small multiple of √(log t), the character estimate instead supplies the square-root exponential. The explicit formula, zero-density and repulsion estimates are the deeper analytic inputs to this step. A published zero-density theorem incorporating repulsion is given by Thorner and Zaman, Refinements to the prime number theorem for arithmetic progressions, Theorem 2.1.

How does a weighted error give a relative error for the number of primes?

Enlarge the range constant so that the weighted estimate holds throughout √U ≤ t ≤ U and L ≥ 16d. There log t ≥ L/2. Put c₃ = min(c/2, c²/2), R = exp(−c₃L/d) + exp(−√(c₃L)), and D = AηR/φ. We now have |Ψ(t) − F(t)| ≤ Dt on the whole upper interval.

Partial summation removes the logarithmic weights. The endpoint error is at most DU/L. The integral of the error over the upper interval is at most ∫√UU D dt/(log t)² ≤ 4DU/L². Applying the same integration to F gives the corrected logarithmic integrals, since F′(t) = (1−s tβ−1)/φ. On the lower interval [2,√U], Chebyshev’s bound gives 0 ≤ Ψ(t) ≤ 6t. The model satisfies |F(t)| ≤ 3t, because φ ≥ 1, |s| ≤ 1, tβ ≤ t and 1/β ≤ 2. Since log t ≥ log 2 > 1/2, the absolute value of the integrand (Ψ(t) − F(t))/(t(log t)²) is at most 36. The lower integral therefore costs at most 36(√U − 2). Its two boundary corrections are F(2)/log 2 and s li(2β)/φ. The first has absolute value at most 12. For the second, √2 ≤ 2β ≤ 2, and 1/log t ≤ 2/log 2 < 4 on [√2,2], an interval of length less than one. The total cost of the lower integral and both corrections is thus at most 40√U. The resulting sum still counts pk with weight 1/k. For k ≥ 2 the base prime is at most √U and there are at most L/log 2 possible exponents, so their contribution is at most 4√UL. Combining these bounds gives

|π(U;m,a) − B| ≤ 5DU/L + 44√UL.

The first term is 10A R P. To compare the second with P, the exceptional-zero bound at exponent one gives η ≥ c₁/m for some c₁ > 0, and φ ≤ m. Thus 44√UL/P ≤ (88/c₁)L² exp(2d−L/2). Now 2d ≤ L/8 and L² ≤ 64 exp(L/4), so the last bound is at most (5632/c₁)exp(−L/8). The inequality √(c₃L) ≤ 2c₃ + L/8 makes this at most (5632 exp(2c₃)/c₁)R. Since P ≤ B, both errors are now bounded by a fixed constant times R B. This is the relative estimate required for the candidate count.

Check the size of the subtracting interval

To test the inequality numerically, take m = 16, U = 1616 and a trial value 1−β = 1/(128 log 16). These values illustrate the integral bound without requiring an exceptional zero at that modulus. Then η = 1/128, (1−β)L = 1/8, and (U−Uβ)/U = 1−exp(−1/8) ≈ 0.1175. The proof retains η/2 = 1/256 of U, enough to compare with an error that also contains η.
