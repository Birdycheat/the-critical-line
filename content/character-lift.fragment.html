Which character takes values in the cyclotomic integers?

The Jacobi identity multiplies two algebraic integers. A sum computed only modulo p would lose the ideal it is supposed to measure. Let K = ℚ(ζ), where ζ has order M ≥ 1, and choose a prime ideal of norm p with p ∤ M. Its quotient has p elements. Identify it with 𝔽p and call the resulting reduction π. The algebraic integer ζ satisfies the cyclotomic polynomial ΦM. Its reduction satisfies the same polynomial modulo p. As p ∤ M, the roots of XM − 1 are simple, and the cyclotomic factors for the divisors of M have disjoint roots. A root of ΦM therefore has exact order M. In particular πζ has that order and M divides p − 1. The prime-norm quotient · Reducing a primitive root · The reduction chosen in the construction

Put h = (p − 1)/M. For each nonzero x in 𝔽p, the element xh is an Mth root of unity. The M powers of πζ are distinct and list every such root. Find the exponent 0 ≤ a < M with xh = (πζ)a and set χ(x) = ζa. Set χ(0) = 0. Multiplying two residues adds their exponents modulo M, so χ is multiplicative. Its order is exactly M. Indeed, if χk = 1, a generator g of 𝔽p× satisfies ghk = 1 after reduction. Hence Mh divides hk, so M divides k. Conversely all nonzero values have Mth power one. This explains why the nontriviality checks on the Jacobi characters reduce to exponent checks. Lift the residue 2 at level five

Take M = 5 and p = 11, with πζ = 3. The powers of 3 starting at exponent zero are 1, 3, 9, 5, 4 and then 1. Since h = 2, square the input 2 to obtain 4. The required lift is χ(2) = ζ⁴. Squaring this value gives χ(4) = ζ³. Checking the other way, 4² ≡ 5 ≡ 3³ modulo 11. For the other reduction π′ζ = 9, the input 2 still gives 2² = 4. Since 9² ≡ 4 modulo 11, its lift is now χ′(2) = ζ². The prime ideal is part of the construction.

Why every prime above p occurs only once

For each invertible residue t modulo M let σt(ζ) = ζt and put Pt = ker(π ∘ σt). This inverse-image convention is what sets the indices in the signed divisor. Every reduction is onto, since ordinary integers already give all of 𝔽p. Its kernel is therefore a maximal ideal of norm p. If two kernels agreed, subtract from any algebraic integer the ordinary integer representing its first reduction. The difference would also belong to the second kernel, forcing both reductions to agree on that element. Applying this to ζ gives (πζ)s = (πζ)t, hence s = t. These φ(M) distinct maximal ideals are pairwise coprime, and each divides (p). Their product divides (p) and already has norm pφ(M). This equals the norm of (p), because [K : ℚ] = φ(M). The leftover integral ideal has norm one and is the unit ideal. Thus (p) = ∏tPt. For a Jacobi sum J dividing p, this gives ordPₜ(J) ∈ {0,1}. Testing π(σtJ) = 0 tells exactly which exponent is one. In the example at 11 the four reductions send ζ to 3, 9, 5 and 4. A repeated prime would exhaust too much of the norm.
