How much of the torsion lies at each geometric point?

Let A be the abelian variety at a geometric point of the unitary moduli problem in characteristic q. Write k for its algebraically closed field. Take the algebra degree m = 1, the rank parameter n ≥ 1, and a principal level N ≥ 3 prime to q. Choose a coefficient map 𝒪F → R of the base and compose with the map R → k of the point. Call the resulting character χ, its kernel 𝔴, and the size of its residue field Q = |𝒪F/𝔴| = qf, with f ≥ 1. This calculation works at every such point, including those where the ordinary count is still in question.

Local length formula. Put K = A[𝔴]. For every k-scheme T, its T-points are the points of A(T) killed by all elements of 𝔴. Let M = #K(k) and let ℓ be the length of the local ring B = 𝒪K,0 at the identity. Then

Qn = Mℓ,   M = Qj,   ℓ = Qn−j   (0 ≤ j ≤ n).

Proof. The arithmetic input is the ideal-torsion degree theorem for this unitary datum. It gives a finite flat subgroup A[𝔴] of rank (Norm 𝔴)mn, here Qn. Its proof chooses an auxiliary ideal 𝔟 prime to q and coprime to 𝔴 with 𝔴𝔟 = (g). The two coprime factors split the kernel of [g] into its 𝔴-torsion and 𝔟-torsion. Dividing the degree of [g] by the known prime-to-q degree for 𝔟 gives the stated rank. We use these two isogeny degrees in this step.

The coordinate ring of K is a finite-dimensional k-algebra. It is Artinian, hence a product of its local rings. Every residue field is a finite extension of the algebraically closed field k, hence equals k. Each factor therefore accounts for one point of K(k). Translation by that point identifies the identity with it and gives an isomorphism of the two local rings. Each factor has length ℓ and also dimension ℓ over k, since every simple quotient in a composition series is its residue field. Adding the dimensions of the M factors gives Qn = Mℓ. The Stacks Project, Lemma 10.53.6 on Artinian rings.

Finally 𝒪F acts on the abelian group K(k), and all elements of 𝔴 act by zero. Thus K(k) is a finite vector space over the field 𝒪F/𝔴. If its dimension is j, its cardinality is Qj. Since ℓ ≥ 1, we have j ≤ n, and division gives the last equality. To determine which exponents can occur, we now look inside the identity factor.

Why does the tangent at the identity force a dichotomy?

Tangent and point count. Under the hypotheses of the local length formula, the tangent space of K at its identity has dimension one over k. Thus ℓ > 1. Either ℓ = Q and M = Qn−1, or ℓ ≥ Q2. The first case is precisely ordinarity at 𝔴.

Proof. By the defining property of A[𝔴], its tangent vectors are those v ∈ Lie(A) killed by every coefficient in 𝔴. We identify this space using the Lie conditions of the moduli problem. Write λj for the coefficient characters at our point, with χ = λi. The datum has a quadratic element ωF whose polynomial splits modulo q. Its two roots in 𝔽q are distinct because its discriminant is prime to q. All λj send ωF to the same chosen root r. The first Lie condition says that every vector on which ωF acts by r is a sum of vectors vj on which all coefficients act by λj. The second says that each of these simultaneous eigenspaces has dimension m, here one.

Take a vector v killed by 𝔴. If z is an integer lifting r, then ωF − z ∈ 𝔴. Consequently ωFv = rv, and the first Lie condition gives v = ∑j vj. The coefficient characters have pairwise distinct maximal kernels by the separation condition on the base. The Chinese remainder theorem therefore gives a coefficient e which is 1 modulo 𝔴 and 0 modulo every other labelled kernel. Applying e to the sum leaves exactly vi. Since e − 1 ∈ 𝔴, the same operation fixes v. Conversely, on the χ-eigenspace every coefficient in 𝔴 acts by zero. Thus the entire tangent space is exactly the χ-eigenspace. Its prescribed dimension also supplies a nonzero tangent vector. The Stacks Project, Chinese remainder theorem.

In the de Rham description used in the deformation argument, the dual H of primitive first de Rham cohomology maps onto Lie(A) with kernel Fil. This map commutes with the coefficient action. A generator of the χ-line therefore lifts to some h ∈ H, and eh still lifts that generator, so eh lies outside Fil. This identifies the nonzero tangent direction in the Hodge quotient. Berthelot, Breen and Messing, Théorie de Dieudonné cristalline II, §2.5 and Proposition 5.1.10.

To read this direction in the local ring, let c ∶ B → k be the residue map and 𝔪 = ker c. A tangent vector is a point over k[ε]/(ε²) reducing to the identity. Its map on functions factors through B and has the form b ↦ c(b) + εδ(b). Multiplicativity gives δ(ab) = c(a)δ(b) + c(b)δ(a), so δ kills 𝔪². Conversely any linear functional on 𝔪/𝔪² extends by zero on k to such a δ. Thus 𝔪/𝔪² is dual to this tangent line. If ℓ were one, B would equal its residue field and every δ would vanish. We conclude ℓ > 1. The Stacks Project, maps from a local scheme and tangent spaces and dual numbers.

Now ℓ = Qn−j with positive exponent. If ℓ ≤ Q, the exponent must be one, giving the ordinary count. If ℓ > Q, the exponent is at least two. This extra length is the hypothesis for the construction of a lifted tangent.

Two groups of rank nine. Over an algebraically closed field of characteristic 3, μ3 × (ℤ/3ℤ)k has three points, one for each element of the constant factor. At each point the multiplicative coordinate u = t − 1 satisfies u3 = t3 − 1 = 0, so the local ring is k[u]/(u3). The product formula reads 9 = 3 · 3. For α9, the kernel of x ↦ x9 on the additive group, the only geometric point is 0 and its local ring is k[x]/(x9). Here 9 = 1 · 9. In each ring the maximal ideal modulo its square is generated by the class of the coordinate. These finite group schemes illustrate both numerical outcomes at Q = 3 and n = 2. Realization of a particular group in the unitary moduli problem is a separate geometric question.
