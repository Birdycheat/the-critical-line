Which compact types can occur in one derivative?

The uniqueness argument for the derivatives separates two kinds of noncompact directions. The distinction is visible in one derivative before we symmetrize several of them. Keep the coordinates in decreasing parameter order, and let εa be the sign of coordinate a. Put m = p + s, K = U(p) × U(s), and let S be the irreducible finite-dimensional unitary K-space of highest weight Λ. Write ea for the ath coordinate weight. The complexified noncompact directions are the matrix units Eab for which εa ≠ εb. A vector in their tensor product with S is an array ∑ Eab ⊗ vab. Set its equal-sign entries to zero.

Let this array be a nonzero highest-weight vector of weight μ. Choose a nonzero coefficient vab with maximal a − b. The diagonal action on Eab has weight ea − eb, so the coefficient has weight μ − ea + eb. To find its weight inside S, apply any compact raising operator Ecd, with c < d and equal signs. Its action on the full array vanishes. In position (a,b) that action is

Ecdvab + δac vdb − δbd vac = 0.

When a = c, the second term has index difference d − b > a − b. When b = d, the third has difference a − c > a − b. Both vanish by maximality. Thus every compact raising operator kills the selected coefficient. In the irreducible space S it has highest weight Λ. We conclude μ = Λ + ea − eb.

Finite-dimensional unitary representations split into irreducibles by successively taking invariant orthogonal complements. Group together all copies of each highest weight. The preceding calculation puts every occurring weight in one of two lists. The case a < b gives the raising part, b < a the lowering part. Distinct ordered opposite-sign pairs give distinct shifts. The two parts are consequently orthogonal and exhaust the array space. A particular candidate weight can have no summand. If one sign is absent, the noncompact direction space is zero and both lists are empty. P. Etingof, Lie Groups and Lie Algebras, proposition 25.5 and corollaries 25.11 and 35.2 on highest weights and complete reducibility.

The Casimir on one noncompact direction

For compact matrix units write Dcd for the infinitesimal action in a representation. Fix the normalization C = ∑εc=εd DcdDdc, summing over ordered pairs, including the diagonal. On an irreducible space of highest weight η this Casimir acts by

κ(η) = ‖η + ρc‖² − ‖ρc‖².

Here ρc is half the sum of the roots ec − ed with c < d and equal signs. On a highest vector the diagonal terms give ∑ ηc². For each such pair the raising operator kills that vector, so the two orders together contribute the commutator Dcc − Ddd, with eigenvalue ηc − ηd. The sum is κ(η). This scalar holds on the entire irreducible space because C commutes with the compact action. Indeed ∑ Ecd ⊗ Edc pairs a basis of each matrix block with its dual for the conjugation-invariant trace pairing, so the sum and its product are invariant.

On the array the generator is Pcd + Dcd. The first operator acts on the matrix position by commutator and the second acts on the coefficient in S. They commute. Expanding the Casimir and exchanging c,d in one cross term gives Ctotal = Cposition + Ccoefficient + 2Ω, where Ω = ∑εc=εd PcdDdc.

To compute the position term, apply the double commutator [Ecd, [Edc, Eab]] to an opposite-sign direction. Its two middle matrix products vanish because they would require the row and column of Eab to lie in the same sign block. The outer products give EccEab and EabEdd. In the sum the first contributes once for each index in the block of a, the second once for each index in the block of b. The blocks have total size m. Thus Cposition acts by m, and Ccoefficient acts by κ(Λ). On a summand of highest weight μ the mixed operator Ω has eigenvalue

cab = (κ(μ) − m − κ(Λ))/2,   μ = Λ + ea − eb.

How much of the weight gap survives?

For the lowest compact type, Λ = λ + ρn − ρc, where ρn is half the sum of the positive opposite-sign roots. Expanding the square in κ after adding ea − eb adds twice the difference of the two coordinates of Λ + ρc, plus 2. Hence

cab = λa − λb + (ρn)a − (ρn)b + 1 − m/2.

For a < b of opposite signs, assume λa − λb ≥ γ. Twice each coordinate of ρn counts opposite-sign indices after it minus those before it. Each coordinate lies between −m/2 and m/2. This gives cab ≥ γ + 1 − 3m/2 ≥ γ − 2m. For a general raising array, write it as the orthogonal sum of its isotypic components. The real part of its inner product with Ω applied to it is the sum of the corresponding eigenvalues times the squared component norms. It is therefore at least (γ − 2m) times its squared norm.

A check with interleaved signs. In dimension three take (+,−,+) and λ = (721,360,0). Then both half-sums equal (1/2,0,−1/2), so Λ = λ. The direction E23 tensored with a highest vector gives a highest vector of weight μ = (721,361,−1) because the only compact raising operator E13 commutes with E23. The change in the sum of coordinate squares is 722, and the change in the compact-root term η₁ − η₃ is 1. Thus κ(μ) − κ(Λ) = 723, and Ω acts by (723 − 3)/2 = 360. The two opposite-sign gaps are 361 and 360, so the uniform lower bound for γ = 360 is 354.

In a symmetric derivative of order j, fixing the other j − 1 positions gives a one-position slice. Once the lowering components have been removed, the preceding inequality applies to every such slice. Their squared norms sum to the squared norm of the whole array. By symmetry each of the j positions contributes the same lower bound, and the Casimir expansion counts each mixed term twice. This supplies the gain 2j(γ − 2m) in the uniqueness proof.
