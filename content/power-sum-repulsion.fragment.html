How does a real zero push other zeros away from 1?

Let χ₁ be a nonprincipal quadratic character modulo m with a real zero β. Let ρ be a zero belonging to a character χ of the same modulus. Write λ = 1 − β, Δ = 1 − Re ρ, γ = Im ρ and H = log(mT). Assume m ≥ 3, T ≥ 2 and |γ| ≤ T. The pair (χ,ρ) must differ from (χ₁,β). In particular this includes another zero of χ₁ itself. There are fixed positive constants c₀, c₁, c₂ such that if 0 < λ < c₁/H and Δ ≤ c₀, then

ΔH ≥ c₂K,   K = log(c₁/(λH³)).

Taking T = T₀ + 3 gives the strip coordinate v = ΔH used above. The exponent N in the strip bound can therefore be taken to be 3. The cube specifies when the logarithm helps. It is positive precisely when λH³ < c₁. At fixed height and modulus, decreasing λ by a factor of e⁴ raises the asserted lower bound for Δ by 4c₂/H. The ordinary zero-free region already gives ΔH ≥ cZ/2 for a distinct pair. We use that bound when K is bounded. For large K, the additional information comes from the derivatives of four logarithmic derivatives of L-functions.

The same primes control two heights. Write Dψ = −L′(s,ψ)/L(s,ψ). Evaluate the pair for the principal character and χ₁ at s₀ = 1 + Δ, and the pair for χ and χχ₁ at s₁ = 1 + Δ + iγ. In the sum of their signed derivatives of order j − 1, the real contribution of an integer n coprime to m contains the factor (1 + χ₁(n))(1 + Re(χ(n)n−iγ)). Both brackets are nonnegative. The other weights are Λ(n)(log n)j−1n−1−Δ/(j − 1)!, where Λ(pk) = log p for prime powers and is zero elsewhere. Integers sharing a factor with m contribute zero. The triangle inequality also bounds the absolute value of the shifted derivative pair by the nonnegative unshifted one, denoted Pj−1 after division by the factorial and multiplication by (−1)j−1.

Near s₁, list the zeros of the two shifted factors within distance 4/25 of 26/25 + iγ, counting multiplicity. Write wa = s₁ − ρa. Remove one copy of β if its character is χ₁ and it occurs in the disk. The target zero remains and contributes wa = 2Δ. Every listed zero has real part below 1, so Re wa ≥ Δ. The disk factorization and Cauchy’s estimate for the logarithmic derivative, after separating the zeros and the possible principal pole, expresses its normalized derivative as a pole term minus a sum of inverse powers of these distances, with error bounded by CH25j. A pole at 1 paired with a zero at β contributes at most jλ/Δj+1, by integrating the derivative of (s − x)−j from x = β to 1. If β falls outside the shifted disk, the unpaired shifted pole is at distance at least ⅛ and its cost fits in the error. At the real point the other first inverse powers have nonnegative real part and can be dropped from the upper estimate. Together these give

Re ∑a 1/wa ≤ 2λ/Δ² + 25CFH,
|∑a wa−j| ≤ Pj−1 + 2jλ/Δj+1 + CFH25j   (j ≥ 1).
Where the three powers of the logarithm enter

Set ℓ = log(3m). The prime estimate at 1 + Δ uses the nonnegative divisor coefficients ∑b∣n χ₁(b). For 1 ≤ y ≤ x, their sum with weights 1/n on (y,x] is at most |L(1,χ₁)| log(x/y) + Cm(1 + log y)/√y. Using |L(1,χ₁)| ≤ Cλℓ² and the prime moment estimate gives

Pj−1 ≤ CPλℓ²(j + 1)²/Δj+1 + (CPℓ)j/(j − 1)! + CP(j + 1)ℓ²/(mΔj).

Multiply through by Δj. The first term now contains λℓ²/Δ. Since ℓ ≤ 2H and 1/Δ ≤ 2H/cZ, this costs a constant times λH³. For the last term use the exceptional-zero lower bound λ ≥ cS/√m and ℓ² ≤ 32√m. This gives ℓ²/m ≤ 32λ/cS. The middle term will decay once the order j is large enough compared with Δℓ.

Why must one of the power sums be large?

Set ua = Δ/wa. One value equals ½. Also |ua|² ≤ Re ua, since Re wa ≥ Δ. By the first-moment estimate the sum S of these real parts is at most 2λ/Δ + 25CFΔH. Suppose, seeking a contradiction, that ΔH < c₂K. Choosing c₁ small relative to the ordinary gap ensures 2λ/Δ ≤ 1. We can then make S ≤ 1 + κK for a fixed small κ by choosing c₂ small enough.

Keep the n indices with |ua| ≥ ε, where r = 16e² and ε = 1/r. The index with value ½ survives and nε² ≤ S. At order j ≥ 2 the discarded terms have total absolute value at most εj−2S. Thus even a large list of tiny terms has a controlled contribution. Divide the retained values by their largest modulus, which is at least ½, to obtain n numbers za of modulus at most one with maximum modulus one.

Turán’s power-sum bound. For any such list and any integer h ≥ 0, some integer j between h + 1 and h + n satisfies |∑ zaj| ≥ [n/(1600(h + n))]n. For instance, (1 + i)/2 and (1 − i)/2 both satisfy the disk inequality for u. Their squares sum to zero and their fourth powers sum to −½. The modulus and the freedom to choose the order both matter.

Take α = 1/(2 log r) and h = ⌈αK⌉. Since nε² ≤ 1 + κK, choosing κ small gives αK < j ≤ 2αK + j₀ and n log 1600 ≤ j + a fixed constant. In Turán’s bound, (1 + h/n)n ≤ eh. Restoring the largest modulus costs at most a factor 2j. With h ≤ j, the resulting lower bound for the retained sum is at least (2e²)−j/E₀ for a fixed E₀. Add back the discarded tail to obtain

(8/r)j/E₀ ≤ |∑a uaj| + εj−2S.

The upper bound for the same expression decays faster. The terms involving the real zero are at most C(j + 1)²λH³. For the factorial term, j! ≥ (j/e)j, and Δℓ ≤ 2c₂K allows us to arrange eCPΔℓ/j ≤ 1/r. The Cauchy remainder is (25CFΔH)(25Δ)j−1. Its first factor is at most κK and the fixed strip width c₀ ensures 25Δ ≤ 1/r. The discarded tail obeys the same geometric estimate because S ≤ 1 + κK. Finally λH³ = c₁e−K ≤ c₁rj₀r−j by the upper bound for j. Hence the right side is at most G(j + 1)²r−j for a fixed G. Comparing gives 8j ≤ E₀G(j + 1)², impossible for large K since j > αK. The ordinary gap covers the bounded values of K after decreasing c₂. This proves the logarithmic repulsion used in the zero-strip sum.
