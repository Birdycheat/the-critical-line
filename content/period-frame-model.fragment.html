How much can we divide out in two dimensions?

Take the two factorizations over the polynomial ring ℤ[t]. Here t plays the role of the period parameter. Use initial height N = 4, target height H = 1 and filtration degrees −1 and 1. The exponents of the first matrix are 3 and 5. Those of its companion occur in reverse order. Choose an integral change of columns, the upper triangular matrix P with diagonal entries 1 and upper right entry 1. Choose the other change of basis to be the identity.

A = diag(t³, t⁵) P−1,   A′ = P diag(t⁵, t³),   AA′ = t⁸I.

The rows of A are (t³, −t³) and (0, t⁵). Those of A′ are (t⁵, t³) and (0, t³). Each contains an entry of order exactly three. We can therefore remove t³ from each matrix. The two quotients have rows (1, −1), (0, t²) and (t², 1), (0, 1), respectively. Their product is t²I. For example, its upper right entry is 1 − 1. A fourth division by t would introduce a pole in both matrices. This example attains the guaranteed power N − H in the matrix lemma.

Why inspect both matrices? Keep the same change of basis and replace the degrees by (1,1). Now every entry of A is divisible by t⁵. The companion has an entry of order three. Dividing both matrices by the largest power found in the first would give an entry t−2 in the second.

A genuine period frame also carries a Galois action and a Frobenius equation. For an example satisfying both, take the trivial representation, basis matrix T = I and correction ε(g) = I for every g. At height b ≥ 0 put A = A′ = EbI. Frobenius fixes I, the product is E2bI, and the constant matrix β = I passes the integral de Rham test at every precision with exponent zero. The crystalline test also holds because ε(g) − I vanishes. All filtration degrees of the trivial representation are zero. Dividing both matrices by Eb returns the frame to height zero with the same period basis.
