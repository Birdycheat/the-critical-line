How does the intersection acquire a basis over the power-series ring?

The limit columns give a matrix V invertible over 𝒪ℰ. Keep the same Breuil–Kisin datum over the finite extension K/ℚℓ, its primitive period τ, the common height H and the target rank d. Write A = Ainf and W = W(ℂℓ♭). Identify A with its image in W and denote evaluation in the two rings by e and eW, respectively. The matrix M = τHT∗ is invertible over W. The preceding construction gives an integral matrix Y1 = MV. Its columns belong to the intersection

L = Ad ∩ M𝒪ℰd = { y ∈ Ad ∣ M−1y ∈ 𝒪ℰd }.

Integral basis theorem. There are columns y₁, …, yd in L such that each element of L has a unique expression ∑j e(sj)yj with sj in 𝔖 = W(k)[[u]]. The matrix Y of these columns is invertible over W, and M−1Y is invertible over 𝒪ℰ. The original limit columns give the lower bound on the rank. The new columns must also generate every integral vector in the intersection.

Reduce Witt vectors modulo ℓ by taking their first component, denoted by a bar. Put D = k[[u]], F = ℂℓ♭ and let b ∈ 𝒪F be the image of u under reduction of evaluation. It is nonzero and has positive valuation. To see that D embeds into 𝒪F, observe that a nonzero series uah with h(0) ≠ 0 maps to ba times a unit, giving zero kernel. We identify D with this image. The definition of 𝒪ℰ at precision one places its reduction in k((u)).

The intersection L is closed under addition and multiplication by 𝔖. It is also ℓ-saturated in Ad, meaning that if ℓy belongs to L then y does too. To check this, it suffices to prove the same cancellation for 𝒪ℰ inside W. Suppose ℓz belongs to 𝒪ℰ. At precision n+1 its definition gives vaℓz ≡ eW(s) modulo ℓn+1, with v = eW(u). Reduction modulo ℓ shows that the image of s in D is zero by the injection just proved. Write s = ℓs′, divide in the ℓ-torsion-free Witt ring, and obtain the required approximation for z at precision n. Apply this entrywise to M−1y.

To compute L/ℓL, choose c ≥ 0 large enough that all entries of P = bc (M̄)−1 have nonnegative valuation in F. Finitely many entries and the positive valuation of b make one exponent possible. The matrix P remains invertible over F. For y ∈ L, the entries of Pȳ lie in 𝒪F and also in k((u)), because M−1y has entries in 𝒪ℰ. The intersection of these two rings in F is D, since the valuation of uah for a unit h of D has the sign of a. Thus we have a map to Dd.

L → Dd,   y ↦ Pȳ,   kernel = ℓL.

Indeed Pȳ = 0 forces ȳ = 0, so y = ℓz for an integral column z. Saturation places z in L. The reverse inclusion holds by reduction. Since 𝔖 → D is surjective, the image is a D-submodule. The ring D is a Noetherian principal ideal domain. Every nonzero ideal is generated by a series of least order in it, since such a series is a power of u times a unit. The image is therefore finite free, of some rank s ≤ d. Choose a basis of the image and lift its columns to y₁, …, ys in L. Stacks Project, Lemma 15.126.8(1).

Write Y for the matrix of these s lifts. Given y(0) ∈ L, express its image in the chosen residual basis and lift those coefficients to a column a0 over 𝔖. The kernel calculation gives y(0) − Y e(a0) = ℓy(1) with y(1) in L. Repeating the step m times gives

y(0) = Y e(a0 + ℓa1 + ⋯ + ℓm−1am−1) + ℓmy(m).

The coefficient sums converge ℓ-adically in 𝔖, since W(k) is ℓ-adically complete and one can take the limit of each power-series coefficient. Their evaluations give the required expression in A, where an element divisible by every ℓm has every Witt component zero. This proves generation before the rank of the lifted system has been determined. Brinon and Conrad, CMI Summer School Notes on p-adic Hodge Theory, §4.2.

The d columns of Y1 also belong to L, so Y1 = Y e(C) for a matrix C with s rows. The left side has full rank over the fraction field of W because it is MV. Its factorization forces s ≥ d, hence s = d. The residual basis columns Pȳj are now d independent columns of Dd. Their determinant stays nonzero under the injection D ↪ F. It follows that det Ȳ is nonzero in F and det Y is a unit in W. A relation among the columns with coefficients in 𝔖 must therefore vanish after evaluation. Evaluation is injective because successive reductions modulo ℓ using D ↪ F show that any element of its kernel is divisible by every ℓm. This proves uniqueness of the coefficients.

Finally, put U = M−1Y. Its entries lie in 𝒪ℰ by the definition of L. Cancelling M in MV = Y e(C) gives V = U eW(C). Thus eW(C)V−1 is a right inverse to U with entries in 𝒪ℰ. For square matrices over this commutative ring, the determinant and adjugate give a two-sided inverse. This identifies the resulting change of basis on the étale lattice. In particular T = T∗U is a new target basis and Y = τHT. The next two-column calculation illustrates the change of lattice. The comparison at a chosen level uses this equality for Y.

How can two columns form a basis and still miss an integral vector?

Take 𝔖 = ℤ₃[[u]] and let 𝒪 be the 3-adic completion of 𝔖[1/u]. Use A = 𝔖 and take 𝒪 itself as the common overring. With M = I the full intersection is L = 𝔖². Consider the columns of V = diag(ua, 1), where a ≥ 0. In 𝒪 we may divide the first coordinate by ua, so these two columns form a basis. Inside L they generate only ua𝔖 × 𝔖. For a = 1, evaluating the first coordinate at u = 0 gives the quotient ℤ₃. This smaller module is already 3-saturated: if 3 times the constant coefficient vanishes in ℤ₃, the coefficient itself vanishes. Yet the vector (1, 0) is missing.

For general a, the missing information consists of the terms of degrees 0 through a − 1 of the first coordinate. For example, at a = 2 the vector (u, 0) passes the constant-term test. Its coefficient in the first column would be 1/u, so it still lies outside the integral span. The full intersection has the elementary basis (1, 0), (0, 1).

L/V𝔖² ≃ 𝔖/(ua),  (s₁, s₂) ↦ s₁ mod ua.

The second test in the experiment asks whether a matrix can serve as a right inverse up to a power of 3. Suppose a > 0 and we try to solve VG ≡ 3rI modulo 3n with G over 𝔖. At u = 0 its upper-left entry would give 0 ≡ 3r modulo 3n. Whenever n > r, this is impossible. If n ≤ r, the zero matrix suffices. When a = 0, V is the identity and G = 3rI works at every precision. This example isolates what the coefficient rings alone can guarantee. For the arithmetic tower the uniform right inverse will also use the period identity and the distinguished polynomial.
