How many faces can an arithmetic group have up to symmetry?

In the homology inequality, A bounds the number of face orbits and s bounds the order of the subgroup fixing any one face. A face in this chain model is an ordered tuple of vertices, with repetitions allowed. Two such tuples represent one orbit if the same group element moves every entry of the first tuple to the corresponding entry of the second. Here the group is G = GLN(𝒪F), with F a totally complex number field, n = [F : ℚ], D = |disc F|, N ≥ 1 and d = nN. Thus d is the integer rank of the vector lattice 𝒪FN. We will count orbits in each face degree p ≥ 0. The two inputs to the homology theorem.

Find a frame shared by the whole face

For each infinite place of F take a Hermitian form on ℂN. Their values on the images of a lattice vector y add to a real number qh(y). Impose qh(y) ≥ 1 for every nonzero y. A vector is minimal for h when equality holds. A vertex is a feasible family h uniquely determined by its equalities. A degree-p face in the complex is an ordered list of p + 1 such vertices at which one supporting linear functional attains its minimum. That functional is strictly positive on nonzero positive semidefinite families. The definition allows repeated vertices. The common minimal vectors of a face span FN. To see why, average the vertices. At the average a lattice inequality is tight exactly when it is tight at every vertex. If the common minimal vectors missed a direction, a nonzero F-linear functional λ would vanish on their span. The family of rank-one forms obtained from |λ|² at all embeddings is positive semidefinite and nonzero. A sufficiently small subtraction of this family from the average preserves the tight constraints. Local finiteness of the inequalities keeps the others feasible. The supporting functional would strictly decrease below its minimum. Thus we can choose N independent common minimal columns and place them in a matrix A. They form a basis over F. Its integral determinant can be a nonunit. The determinant estimate gives

r = |NormF/ℚ(det A)| ≤ J,   J = DNdd.

For this last inequality pull back each local form by A. The pulled-back determinants have product at most one by the diagonal bounds and Hadamard’s inequality. Their product is r times the product of the original determinants, for which the geometric lower bound is 1/J. One congruence count for the frame

Fix its positive integer r. Over F put B = rA−1. Why does this have integral entries? The principal ideal (det A) has norm r and contains the integer r. Hence r/det A lies in 𝒪F, and multiplying the integral adjugate of A by that element gives B. Reduce its entries modulo r𝒪F. Suppose a second frame A′ has the same norm and its matrix B′ has the same residue list. Write B − B′ = rX with X integral. Over the field this gives

A′A−1 = 1 + A′X,   AA′−1 = 1 − AX.

Both matrices are integral and they are inverses. The first therefore belongs to G and takes A to A′ by left multiplication. An integral basis identifies 𝒪F/r𝒪F as an additive group with (ℤ/rℤ)n. The list of N² entries has at most rnN² = rdN values. Choosing one frame for each attained list covers every orbit. Summing the bound for r = 1,…,J costs at most J1+dN. Example. In ℤ[i] take the one-column matrices A = 1 + i and A′ = 1 − i. Both norms are 2. The scaled inverses are B = 1 − i and B′ = 1 + i, so X = −i. The formula gives g = −i, and multiplication by this Gaussian unit sends 1 + i to 1 − i. This tests the congruence argument for frames of bounded determinant norm.

How many vertices can share a chosen frame?

Fix a representative A. A nearby vector is one that can be minimal for some feasible form making every column of A minimal. The coordinate estimate gives |σ((By)j)| ≤ R = NNJ² at every embedding. Since AB = r·1, the vector By determines y. Each of its coordinates has at most (2R + 1)n possibilities. Indeed, if two such algebraic integers differ by (2R + 1)x, every conjugate of x has absolute value at most 2R/(2R + 1) < 1. A nonzero algebraic integer has nonzero integer norm, ruling this out. The residue count thus bounds the number of nearby vectors by M = (2R + 1)d. The coordinate estimate on the scaled inverse, separation by conjugate size, counting all coordinates. The column replacement argument below derives the coordinate bound at each embedding.

The equations qh(y) = 1 are linear in the unknown coefficients of h. At a vertex, at most dN independent equations suffice to determine it. Their vectors lie in the nearby set. A set of at most dN vectors drawn from M choices can be recorded as a list of length dN by sorting it and padding with a blank. Hence this frame admits at most (M + 1)dN vertices. Each entry of an ordered tuple can use any of these in an upper bound. Together with the frame count this gives the orbit bound

Ap = J1+dN(M + 1)dN(p+1) ≤ (2Dd)12(p+1)d³N.

This count allows lists that fail the common-support condition, which only enlarges the bound. Formally choose a frame and a translated tuple for each orbit. Why reduction modulo three counts stabilizers

An element fixing an ordered tuple fixes its first vertex. The first vertex has finitely many minimal vectors, and these include N independent ones. The images of those basis vectors under an element fixing the vertex are again minimal and determine the element. Thus the stabilizer is finite. Now view it as a subgroup of the units of the matrix ring T = MN(𝒪F). Distinct elements have distinct residues in T/3T. Otherwise their quotient would be a finite-order unit congruent to 1 modulo 3. Finiteness from minimal vectors, modulo-three separation.

Here is the divisibility argument behind this separation. If such a unit were nontrivial, one of its powers would have prime order l and still be congruent to 1. Write that power as 1 + 3kY with k ≥ 1 maximal and Y nonzero modulo 3. Maximality follows by looking at any nonzero integer coordinate in the free abelian group T. For l ≠ 3 its lth power differs from 1 by l3kY modulo 3k+1, which is nonzero. For l = 3 the leading term is 3k+1Y modulo 3k+2. The other two terms in the cube are divisible by 32k+1 and 33k, both at least the required power. This contradicts prime order in both cases. Induction on the order gives the same separation. The power divisible by three, the prime-to-three power.

The additive rank of T is nN² = dN, so the stabilizer bound is s = 3dN, uniform in the face degree. In rank one over ℤ[i] the units 1, −1, i, −i already have four distinct residues among the nine available. In homology degree m, Am bounds every face degree p ≤ m. Substituting it and s into the homological torsion inequality gives the estimate used in degree two.
