Why do all the matrix entries lie in one finite extension?

The trace construction gives a continuous representation r of a compact group Γ. For each group element g, the matrix r(g) has entries in an algebraic closure Ω of ℚℓ. Our next task is to compare integral matrices to a specified precision. We first need a single finite extension of ℚℓ in which to write every entry of r, including those of all the inverse matrices.

Put F = ℚℓ. An entry x is algebraic over F. There is a number β algebraic over ℚ such that F(x) ⊆ F(β). To see this, take the monic minimal polynomial of x over F and approximate its coefficients by rational numbers. Its monic approximation q can be chosen to make |q(x)|ℓ arbitrarily small. This value is the product of the distances from x to the roots of q. Thus one such root β can be made closer to x than any distinct F-conjugate of x. An automorphism fixing β preserves these distances and must fix x. Indeed, the ultrametric triangle inequality puts the image of x closer to x than any distinct conjugate. The fixed-field criterion gives the inclusion. For x already in F, the inclusion is immediate. J. S. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, Proposition 7.60 (Krasner’s lemma) and Corollary 7.62.

Only countably many elements of Ω are algebraic over ℚ, since there are countably many rational polynomials, each with finitely many roots. The fields obtained by adjoining finitely many such elements to F therefore form a countable family. Each is finite over F, and every finite collection of entries in Ω belongs to some member of the family.

In the compact image group H = r(Γ), take the set of matrices whose entries and inverse entries all lie in one such field L. These matrices form a subgroup. This subgroup is closed, since finite extensions of the complete field F are complete and hence closed inside Ω, and the matrix entries are continuous. These closed subgroups, one for each field, cover H. By the Baire category theorem on the compact Hausdorff group H, one of them has nonempty interior. Translating its interior to the identity shows that this subgroup of the image is open. Its cosets cover the compact image, so finitely many suffice. The Stacks Project, Lemma 5.13.3, Baire category theorem.

Enlarge L by the finitely many entries of coset representatives. Every group matrix is a product of a representative and a matrix over L, so the resulting finite extension E contains them all. We can include any previously prescribed finite field by taking the compositum. This reasoning applies separately to every auxiliary representation. Later, each comparison involves only finitely many of them, even though the family varies with the required precision.

Which lattice records an integral combination of group matrices?

Let a be the dimension of the representation and E the finite extension of ℚℓ containing its coefficients. Write R = ℤℓ and let 𝒪E be the elements of E of ℓ-adic absolute value at most one. We use two modules. The first lives in the vector space Ea on which the group acts. The second, which enters the approximation theorem, lives in the space of a by a matrices.

Start with the 𝒪E-span Λ of the vectors r(g)v, for all g ∈ Γ and all v ∈ 𝒪Ea. The identity shows that Λ contains 𝒪Ea. The group image is compact, so all its entries have a common bound. Thus Λ is contained in ℓ−t𝒪Ea for some integer t. It is finitely generated and free of rank a, since the ring of integers of a local field is a discrete valuation ring. Left multiplication by a group matrix permutes the lattices used to generate Λ. In a basis of Λ the group acts by invertible integral matrices. In particular, traces of integral combinations of their products belong to 𝒪E. J. S. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, Theorem 7.38 and Corollary 7.42, on finite extensions and their valuation rings.

Now form the R-span M of the matrices themselves. An element of M has the form

X = ∑h ∈ Γ fh r(h),   fh ∈ R, with only finitely many nonzero coefficients.

The matrices of M preserve Λ, so M is an R-submodule of End𝒪E(Λ). The latter is finite free over R because 𝒪E is finite free over R. Submodules of a finite free module over the principal ideal domain R are finite free. Choose an R-basis of M and denote its rank by d. Expressing each basis element as a finite sum of group matrices, then gathering those finitely many supports, gives a finite set of group matrices generating M. These generators need not themselves be a basis. The Stacks Project, Lemma 15.22.11 on finite torsion-free modules.

The product rule r(g)r(h) = r(gh) makes M an algebra containing the identity I. Left multiplication X ↦ r(g)X acts on the d basis coordinates of M by an invertible integral matrix. The inverse is multiplication by r(g−1). Each coordinate of r(g)X is continuous in g, because it is a linear coordinate on the finite-dimensional F-space spanned by M. The vector of coordinates of the identity is distinguished. Applying this action to it reads off the coordinates of r(g) itself.

For example, a trivial two-dimensional representation has r(g) = I for every g. Then Λ has rank two over 𝒪E and M = R I has rank one over R. In what follows, the assertion X ∈ ℓnM says that every coordinate of X in a basis of M is divisible by ℓn. Dividing the entries of a matrix tests a weaker condition, as the two-dimensional sign representation will show.

How much trace precision forces divisibility in the matrix lattice?

The approximation gives scalar traces. To construct maps of lattices we need to recognize a matrix that becomes zero modulo ℓnM. Knowing tr(X) alone cannot usually do this. For example, any nonzero traceless matrix would give the same answer as zero. The group supplies further measurements, the traces of X after multiplication by each r(g).

Trace-divisibility lemma. Assume the representation is semisimple over Ω, meaning a direct sum of irreducible representations there. For its matrix lattice M there is an integer c ≥ 0, independent of X and n, such that for all X ∈ M and n ≥ 0,

tr(Xr(g)) ∈ ℓn+c𝒪E for every g ∈ Γ   ⟹   X ∈ ℓnM.

Why these measurements detect zero. Over Ω decompose the representation into distinct irreducible spaces Sj with positive multiplicities mj. The density theorem for representations says that the group algebra acts through the full product of the matrix algebras End(Sj). Thus, for each block, a linear combination of group matrices can act as any prescribed matrix unit there and as zero on the other blocks. The total trace of XY is ∑j mj tr(XjYj). If the entry in row u, column v of Xj is nonzero, take Yj with a single 1 in row v, column u. The trace is that entry times mj, which is nonzero in characteristic zero. Vanishing of every translated trace would contradict this calculation. P. Etingof et al., Introduction to Representation Theory, Theorem 3.2.2(ii).

Semisimplicity matters. Let the additive group ℤℓ act by r(x) = I + xN, where N is the two-by-two matrix whose sole nonzero entry is a 1 above the diagonal. Since N² = 0, this is a continuous representation. Its lattice contains N = r(1) − I and has basis I, N. Yet tr(Nr(x)) = tr(N) = 0 for every x, while N is a basis element and is indivisible by ℓ in this lattice.

Why one loss suffices for every precision. Take the finite generating list r(g₁),…,r(gt) for M. Its tests suffice because the other group matrices are integral combinations of this list. Each test is 𝒪E-valued on M. Express its value in an R-basis of 𝒪E and express X in an R-basis of M. The resulting rectangular matrix B over R takes a coordinate vector x of length d to all the trace coordinates. By the preceding argument its kernel over F is zero. In particular one can select d rows forming a square matrix of nonzero determinant Δ.

If all the measurements belong to ℓn+c𝒪E, all coordinates of Bx belong to ℓn+cR. Multiplying the selected rows by the adjugate matrix shows that every coordinate of Δx is divisible by ℓn+c. Taking c to be the ℓ-adic valuation of Δ proves the lemma. In a ramified field, if a uniformizer ϖ has valuation one and ℓ has valuation e, the precision required in the trace field is the ideal (ϖe(n+c)). The conclusion divides the matrix by ℓn in its original ℤℓ-lattice. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, Remark 7.43.

How does the same proof look in terms of norms?

Map a tuple of coefficients on the finite generating list to the corresponding tuple of translated traces. This is a linear map of finite-dimensional ℚℓ-spaces onto its range. On that range it has a continuous linear right inverse, obtained by lifting a basis and taking linear combinations of the lifts. Consequently sufficiently small trace tuples have lifts with all coefficients in ℤℓ. Choose c so a tuple of norm at most ℓ−c lies in this neighbourhood. Dividing all the original measurements by ℓn puts them in the neighbourhood. An integral lift now gives a matrix Y ∈ M with the same translated traces as X/ℓn. Their difference has zero translated traces, so the detection argument gives X = ℓnY.

Which loss is the smallest possible?

Reduce B by invertible row and column operations over R. One can first bring an entry of minimal valuation to the upper left. It divides all entries, allowing one to clear its row and column by subtracting integral multiples. Repeating on the remaining rectangle gives nonzero diagonal entries ℓs₁,…,ℓsd up to units, where 0 ≤ s₁ ≤ ⋯ ≤ sd. These operations preserve divisibility of a vector by a power of ℓ. In the resulting coordinates, testing xj means testing ℓsjxj. Hence sd suffices. If sd > 0, take x equal to ℓn−1 times the last basis vector, with n ≥ 1. The measurement passes precision n + sd − 1, although x is indivisible by ℓn. This proves optimality. For the zero lattice one may take c = 0.

The coordinate ring matters even in dimension one. Over ℚ₂ let a generator of the cyclic group of order three act by a primitive cube root ζ. The polynomial X² + X + 1 is irreducible modulo 2, so the matrix lattice has ℤ₂-basis 1, ζ. Its two-by-two trace matrix over ℚ₂(ζ) has rows (1,ζ) and (ζ,ζ²), whose determinant vanishes. The test against 1 nevertheless returns the full field element a + bζ. In fact, if either coefficient is odd, the norm a² − ab + b² is odd. Factoring out the largest common power of two therefore reads the divisibility of this field element directly from its two coefficients. This is why B uses all coordinates of each trace.

Why does one extra binary digit determine membership in the lattice?

Let Γ be the two-element group acting on ℚ₂² by the identity I and the sign matrix D = diag(1,−1). Its matrix lattice has basis I, D over ℤ₂. Write a typical matrix as X = aI + bD = diag(a + b, a − b). To recover a and b from a diagonal pair (u,v), take half its sum and half its difference. Thus M consists exactly of the diagonal pairs whose entries have the same parity.

There are just two translated traces. Since D² = I and tr(D) = 0, they give

tr(X) = 2a,   tr(XD) = 2b.

For n ≥ 1, requiring both answers to vanish modulo 2n+1 is equivalent to requiring both coefficients a, b to vanish modulo 2n. That is precisely X ∈ 2nM. One fewer digit can fail at every depth. Take X = 2n−1I. The two traces are 2n and zero, while the first lattice coefficient is only 2n−1. The coordinate matrix of the trace tests has diagonal (2,2). Its determinant gives a sufficient loss of two digits. Its two diagonal entries show directly why one digit is enough.

At depth one take a = b = 1. The matrix is diag(2,0), whose entries are even. Dividing by 2 gives diag(1,0), whose entries have opposite parities. The quotient is outside M. In the experiment, also try a = 0 and b = 1. Then X = D has trace zero at every precision. The second measurement, tr(XD) = 2, detects the missing coefficient.

In the auxiliary representations there will rarely be a basis this simple. Translating a relation before taking traces supplies the same two ingredients as in this example, enough measurements to see every coordinate and enough precision to divide inside the prescribed lattice.
