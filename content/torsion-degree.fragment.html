How elliptic torsion puts a unit coefficient within degree p²

Why must the reduction of e(T) = [p](T) have a nonzero term by degree p²? Fix the good elliptic model above, with p ≥ 5. Its formal group law gives the integral series e by adding a point to itself p times. Its constant term is zero. To bound the degree, represent the curve by the corresponding complex torus ℂ/Λ. Its p-torsion is (p−1Λ)/Λ, with p choices in each of the two lattice directions. Take one representative P of each of its p² − 1 nonzero points. Write xP for its Weierstrass ℘-value transported into ℂp by the chosen field isomorphism. The representatives P and −P each contribute a factor. Why is the extra power series an integral unit?

Let R be the valuation ring and φ the correcting series of the good curve model, with φ(0) = 0. Write qₘ = φ([m](T)) for multiplication by an integer m in the formal group. For series with zero constant term write a ≡ b if exp(a − b) has all coefficients in R. Its constant term is one, so its inverse also has coefficients in R. Consequently these congruences can be added, subtracted and multiplied by integers.

The two curve identities used here say that the addition defect of φ is biadditive modulo this congruence and that q−1 ≡ φ. The second uses that 2 is a unit in R. Denote the defect on (T,T) by B = q₂ − 2φ. Applying biadditivity to (T,[m](T)) gives qm+1 ≡ φ + qₘ + mB. At m = −1 this reads 0 ≡ 2φ − B, because q₀ = 0. Thus successive differences satisfy qm+1 − qₘ ≡ (2m + 1)φ. Summation gives qₚ ≡ p²φ.

After the change to the formal parameter, the sigma identity reads e² exp(−2qₚ) T2(p²−1) = G T2p² exp(−2p²φ). Cancel the common power of T. The remaining factor is U = exp(2(qₚ − p²φ)), the square of the integral unit given by the congruence. In particular multiplying by U preserves the first nonzero degree after reduction.

For each factor, the maximum coefficient norm is max(1, |xP|p). It is first attained in degree 0 if |xP|p ≤ 1, and degree 2 otherwise. In a product the first degrees attaining the maxima add. At their sum the product of the first maximal coefficients is the unique summand of largest norm. Thus G attains its maximum coefficient norm M = |p²|p ∏P max(1, |xP|p) by degree 2(p² − 1). What forces that maximum to equal one? Choose a nonzero half-period and let h be its transported ℘-value. It solves 4h³ − g₂h − g₃ = 0. Good reduction makes h integral and 3h² − g₂/4 a unit, since the reduced cubic has distinct roots. The torsion product identity at the half-period gives |p²|p ∏P|h − xP|p = 1. Each factor is bounded by max(1, |xP|p), so 1 ≤ M. Now reduce the squared multiplication formula modulo the maximal ideal. Multiplication by U preserves the first nonzero degree, and T² adds two. Hence the first nonzero degree of the reduced e² is at most 2(p² − 1) + 2 = 2p². Squaring doubles the first degree of a nonzero series over a field, giving the claimed bound. The two descents at an inert CM prime separately force that first degree to be a positive multiple of p². Together the arguments identify it exactly. A good curve whose first unit occurs before degree 25

At p = 5 take y² = x³ − x. Its discriminant 64 is a unit. Write x = u−2 and y = −u−3√(1 − u⁴), taking the square root with constant term 1. Then T = u + u⁵/2 + O(u⁹) and the invariant differential gives dλ = dx/(2y) = (1 + u⁴/2 + O(u⁸)) du. Integration and substitution give λ(T) = T − 2T⁵/5 + O(T⁹). Write [5](T) = 5T + aT⁵ + O(T⁹). Comparing degree five in λ([5](T)) = 5λ(T) gives a = 2 · 5⁴ − 2 = 1248 ≡ 3 modulo 5. Here the first unit occurs at degree 5. The inert curve at the same prime reaches degree 25.
