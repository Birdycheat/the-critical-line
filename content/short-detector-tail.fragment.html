How accurately does a finite character sum approximate the value at a zero?

Let χ be a nonprincipal Dirichlet character modulo q. Write A(n) = ∑1≤k≤n χ(k) and PN(s) = ∑1≤n≤N χ(n)n−s. A complete period of χ sums to zero. Deleting whole periods bounds |A(n)| uniformly, and we may take B = q+1. For any complex s with σ = Re s > 0, put f(x) = x−s on the positive real axis. The point of using the partial sums A(n) is that the differences of f are absolutely summable throughout this half-plane.

Indeed, integrate f′(x) = −s x−s−1 from n to n+1, and take absolute values inside the integral. This gives |f(n)−f(n+1)| ≤ (|s|/σ)(n−σ−(n+1)−σ). Moreover, on any compact subset with σ ≥ σ₁ > 0 and |s| ≤ K, the difference is at most K n−σ₁−1. Thus G(s) = ∑n≥1 A(n)(f(n)−f(n+1)) converges locally uniformly and defines a holomorphic function there. For finite N, replacing χ(n) by A(n)−A(n−1) gives

PN(s) = ∑1≤n≤N A(n)(f(n)−f(n+1)) + A(N)f(N+1).

The last term tends to zero. For σ > 1, the limit on the left is the absolutely convergent Dirichlet series for L(s,χ). By uniqueness of analytic continuation G therefore gives L throughout σ > 0. Subtracting the displayed identity from G leaves the sum of differences beginning at N+1, followed by minus A(N)f(N+1). In the bound for the sum, the consecutive differences of n−σ telescope to (N+1)−σ. The boundary term costs B(N+1)−σ. Hence for all integers N ≥ 1,

|L(s,χ)−PN(s)| ≤ B(1+|s|/σ)(N+1)−σ ≤ 2B(1+|s|/σ)N−σ.

The second, deliberately weaker bound is the one used in the detector. It applies at a zero even on the left of the line of absolute convergence. One can see the boundary term already with the quadratic character modulo 4, s = 1 and N = 2. Here A(1) = A(2) = 1, so the first two differences give (1−1/2)+(1/2−1/3) = 2/3. The boundary term is +1/3 in the finite identity, giving P₂(1) = 1. Kiran Kedlaya, Notes on analytic number theory, Lemma 3.2, Theorem 3.5 and equation (3.2.3).

Why are there exactly J possible blocks?

Fix Q ≥ 20 and a primitive character of conductor 1 < q ≤ Q. It is nonprincipal because the principal character is induced from conductor one. Take a zero ρ with σ = Re ρ > 399/400 and |Im ρ| ≤ Q. For this step the lower bound on σ suffices. Put X = ⌈Q⌉, Y = X⁴, J = 8(⌊log₂ X⌋+1) and N = 2J. The number 2⌊log₂ X⌋+1 is strictly larger than X, including when X is a power of two. Its eighth power is N, so N ≥ X⁸ ≥ Y.

At ρ the remainder bounds |PN(ρ)|. Since |ρ| ≤ σ+Q and σ > 1/2, we have 1+|ρ|/σ ≤ 2+2Q ≤ 3X. Also q+1 ≤ 2X. The weaker inequality σ ≥ 15/16 already gives Nσ ≥ X15/2 = X⁷√X ≥ 4X⁷. The other factor in the detector is MY(ρ) = ∑1≤d≤Y μ(d)χ(d)d−ρ. Each summand has modulus at most one, so |MY(ρ)| ≤ Y. All four estimates hold simultaneously for every allowed character and zero. Multiplying them gives

|PN(ρ)MY(ρ)| ≤ 2(2X)(3X) X⁴/(4X⁷) = 3/X ≤ 1/2.

By the finite Möbius product identity, the same product is 1+D, where D sums the coefficients a(n)χ(n)n−ρ over Y < n ≤ YN. The reverse triangle inequality gives |D| ≥ 1/2. Partition these integers with endpoints Y, 2Y, 4Y, …, Y2J = YN. There are J+1 endpoints and exactly J intervals. For index j = 0,…,J−1, write Bⱼ(ρ) for the sum on (Y2j, Y2j+1]. The first integer included is Y+1. Each internal endpoint belongs to the earlier interval, and the last interval includes YN. Thus

1/2 ≤ |D| ≤ ∑j=0J−1|Bⱼ(ρ)| ≤ J max0≤j<J|Bⱼ(ρ)|.

For the small product example Y = 3, N = 4 = 2², the two intervals are (3,6] and (6,12]. The coefficient a(6) = −2 belongs entirely to the first block, and a(12) = −1 belongs to the second. In the zero count, each character supplies at least one block of modulus ≥ 1/(2J), with equality allowed. Choosing one such index per character produces the families in the block mean-square estimate. That estimate also needs Re ρ < 1. The Euler product excludes zeros with Re ρ > 1, and the classical nonvanishing theorem excludes the whole line Re ρ = 1 for a nonprincipal character, including the real point ρ = 1. Every chosen zero of conductor greater than one therefore lies strictly to the left of that line. Kedlaya, equation (3.2.1) and Theorems 3.8, 3.10 and 3.11.
