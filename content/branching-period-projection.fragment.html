Where can the smaller representation be seen inside the larger one?

Keep the signed unitary groups H ⊂ G, the positive unit vector z fixed by H, and the real branching assumptions. Write m = n + 1 for the larger dimension and X = 8mB. Fix a lowest compact-type unit vector v₀ for π on G and a lowest compact-type unit vector w₀ for σ on H. The signed interlacing theorem and its coefficient projection supply an isometric intertwiner i from the space of σ to that of π. Set P = i∗. Then P takes values in the smaller space, while iP is the orthogonal projection onto its copy inside the larger space.

The task is to choose a translate π(g₀)v₀ whose projection has a quantitative lower bound. Consider the nonnegative function F(g) = |⟨iw₀,π(g)v₀⟩|² and the squared column length Y(g) = ‖g−1z‖₂². For Haar measures μ on G and ν on H let dπ, dσ be the corresponding formal degrees. Schur orthogonality says that the squared matrix coefficient of two vectors integrates to the product of their squared norms divided by the formal degree. For our unit vectors this gives ∫F dμ = 1/dπ. The model uses its fixed ambient Haar measure for the estimates below. M. Duflo and C. C. Moore, orthogonality relations for square-integrable representations.

Mixed coefficient moment. The branching construction gives the following bound on the average of Y with density dπF.

∫G Y(g)F(g) dμ(g) ≤ X8m/dπ.

Here is how a column of g−1 enters. Tensor π with the dual of the defining representation. Acting on a tensor supported in coordinate l and reading its z-coordinate inserts the matrix entry (g−1)l,z. The discrete-series decomposition of this tensor on smooth vectors writes the resulting coefficient as ∑s al,s(g) over at most m summands, each with one parameter lowered by 1. In each summand, Schur orthogonality gives ∫|al,s|² ≤ X6m/dπ. Three bounds multiply here. The scalar obtained by applying the tensor projection and taking the distinguished coordinate costs at most Xm, the squared projected input with its form normalization costs X4m, and the reciprocal shifted formal degree costs at most Xm/dπ. These are the tensor decomposition, projection and formal-degree estimates used at this step.

For each l, the square of the sum costs at most the number of summands times ∑s|al,s|² by Cauchy–Schwarz. Summing over l recovers the whole squared column. The three factors from Cauchy–Schwarz, the sum over s and the sum over l are each ≤ m ≤ X. Thus the exponent is at most 6m + 3 ≤ 8m. This moment controls the mass with a large distinguished column even when left multiplication by the stabilizer makes the full matrix arbitrarily large.

How does a bound on one column locate a translate of bounded matrix norm?

Bounded translate. There exists g₀ ∈ G such that

‖g₀‖F ≤ X10m,   ‖Pπ(g₀)v₀‖ ≥ X−100m³.

Take Y₁ = 2X8m and S = {g : Y(g) ≤ Y₁}. The moment bound gives ∫G∖SF ≤ 1/(2dπ), so at least half the mass lies in S. Put U = {h ∈ H : ‖h − 1‖F ≤ 1}, and let C be the ambient ball ‖g‖F² ≤ 64m(m + Y₁)². The radius here is specified by the squared Frobenius norm.

For g ∈ S, choose a signed-unitary matrix b taking z to g−1z with ‖b‖F² ≤ 16(m + Y₁)². Such a matrix is obtained by rotations in the positive and negative subspaces and a two-dimensional signed-unitary block, as in the column decomposition. Then gb fixes z, so gb = ι(h₀) for some h₀ ∈ H. If h = h₀u with u ∈ U, then ι(h)−1g = ι(u)−1b−1 belongs to C. Indeed ‖ι(u)‖F² ≤ 4m, and inverse signed-unitary matrices have the same Frobenius norm. Each g ∈ S therefore contributes at least ν(U) to the integral of the indicator of C over these h. Multiply by F(g), integrate and change variables by left translation. Tonelli's theorem gives

ν(U) ∫S F dμ ≤ ∫C ∫H F(ι(h)k) dν(h) dμ(k).

Equivariance of i and Schur orthogonality on H make the inner integral exactly ‖Pπ(k)v₀‖²/dσ. Two quantitative comparisons fix the scale. Integrating the decaying subgroup coefficient of w₀ gives 1/dσ ≤ Dν(U), with D = X67m². Covering the ambient ball by translates of the identity neighbourhood of radius (100m(B + 1))−2 gives dπμ(C) ≤ K, with K = 4X24m³. The formal degree times the measure of that small neighbourhood is at most 4. T. Tao, An Epsilon of Room, I, Theorem 1.1.23 (Tonelli).

If ‖Pπ(k)v₀‖² were smaller than 1/(4DK) throughout C, multiplying the displayed double integral by dπ would give at most ν(U)/4 on the right and at least ν(U)/2 on the left. Since 0 < ν(U) < ∞, one g₀ in C has projected squared norm at least 1/(4DK). The parameter gap implies X ≥ 16. The numerical inequalities 64m(m + 2X8m)² ≤ X20m and 4DK = 16X67m²+24m³ ≤ X200m³ give the asserted bounds after taking square roots. For the first inequality use m + 2X8m ≤ 3X8m and 576m ≤ X4m. In particular ℬ(100)‖Pπ(g₀)v₀‖ ≥ 1 for ℬ(100) = X100m⁴, the common scale of the period estimates.

For m = 4 the moment calculation costs 27 powers of X and allows 32. The coefficient before the squared projection is 16X2608, since 67·16 + 24·64 = 2608. The bound 16 ≤ X then gives 4DK ≤ X2609, within the allowed X12800.

Why does the projected vector yield a positive diagonal period?

Set v = π(g₀)v₀, p = Pv ≠ 0 and ρ = π restricted to H. Inner products are linear in their second argument. The preceding absolute-integral estimate at the identity makes h ↦ ⟨v,ρ(h)v⟩ integrable. On the smaller Hilbert space define the coefficient operator

⟨a,Tb⟩ = ∫H conj(⟨v,ρ(h)v⟩) ⟨a,σ(h)b⟩ dν(h).

Inversion of Haar measure on this unimodular group gives T = T∗. The coefficient of v is bounded and integrable, hence square-integrable. In an orthonormal basis of the smaller space, Schur orthogonality makes the matrix coefficients of σ an orthonormal family of functions after multiplication by √dσ. For every finite part of this family, subtract the orthogonal projection of the coefficient onto its span. The squared norm of the remainder is nonnegative. This gives Bessel’s inequality and bounds the sum of squares of all entries of T by the squared L² norm of the coefficient divided by dσ. Keeping finitely many of these entries gives a finite-rank operator. By Cauchy–Schwarz the squared operator norm of the discarded part is at most the sum of its squared entries, which tends to zero. Thus T is compact.

The lower estimate is Re⟨a,Ta⟩ ≥ |⟨p,a⟩|²/dσ. One way to see why the projection appears is to average ρ against the conjugate coefficient of w₀, multiplied by dσ. This is an orthogonal projection by Schur convolution, and fixes iw₀. Its quadratic form therefore dominates |⟨·,iw₀⟩|². For a in the span of translates of w₀, a change of variables in the two coefficient integrals transfers this inequality to T. That span is dense by irreducibility, so continuity gives the lower estimate for every a.

In an eigenbasis of the compact self-adjoint T, the quadratic form at p is the sum of the eigenvalues weighted by the squared coordinates of p. If all eigenvalues were smaller than ‖p‖²/(2dσ), this sum would be at most ‖p‖⁴/(2dσ). The preceding lower estimate gives at least ‖p‖⁴/dσ. Thus there is a unit eigenvector w with eigenvalue c₁ ≥ ‖p‖²/(2dσ) > 0. Inserting b = w into the defining integral gives c₁⟨a,w⟩, the diagonal term of the period. MIT 18.102, Lecture 22, Theorem 232 (compact self-adjoint spectral theorem).

The construction also gives a smooth w. The form ∫⟨y,ρ(h)v⟩⟨σ(h)w,a⟩ dν represents ⟨Ay,a⟩ for a bounded operator from the larger space to the smaller one. Its norm bound is ‖w‖(∫|⟨v,ρ(h)v⟩| dν/dσ)1/2 by the coefficient Cauchy–Schwarz inequality and Schur orthogonality. Left invariance makes A equivariant. At y = v the eigenvector identity, conjugated, says Av = c₁w. Dividing the intertwiner by c₁ maps the smooth vector v to w, which is therefore smooth.

Check on a circle. To test this last operator identity, take normalized Haar measure on the unit circle, σ trivial, ρ(t) = diag(1,t) and v = (3/5,4/5). Embed σ as the first coordinate. Then Pv = 3/5 and dσ = 1. The coefficient of v is 9/25 + (16/25)t. Its conjugate integrates to 9/25, so the eigenvalue is 9/25 and the guaranteed lower bound is 9/50. This example checks the normalization of the diagonal integral independently of the large-gap estimates.
