How many rational classes can share a height label?

The convergence estimate groups rational Siegel cosets by a tuple of nonnegative integers. Write n for the source rank (called a above), d for the number of real places of the totally real field F, and B = 4d. Fix a label μ = (μ₁,…,μn). Two counts enter. First one covers the unipotent matrix coordinate by finitely many centres at scales set by μ. Then one bounds how many cosets can use any one centre. Both bounds are uniform over μ. Which rows form a basis, and why is each label nonnegative?

Over the CM field E, the module of integral rows Λ has underlying ℤ-rank n[E : ℚ]. The real-place reduction selects n rows in Λ independent over E. They form the rows of Δ and a basis of En. Their span over the ring of integers contains MΛ for a fixed positive integer M, and is contained in Λ. To see why the index can be controlled, multiply the selected rows by an integral basis of E to obtain n[E : ℚ] rows. Their Gram determinant is the determinant for an integral lattice basis times the square of the sublattice index. Comparing this identity with the product bound on the selected row lengths controls the index and keeps the dyadically rescaled row matrix away from singularity. At infinity the row lengths are scaled by powers of 2. Passing to the idele s reverses the sign of these exponents. Count the centres for a fixed label. At scalar depth k, subtract a rational element from an adele of F to put it in a fixed compact set. Bound its real components in absolute value by a nonnegative integer R. At each real place use the grid 2−kℤ between −R and R. Its integer numerators have exactly 2·2kR + 1 possibilities. Rounding leaves an error of size at most 2−k. The finite component stays in a fixed compact set. Why a centre has bounded multiplicity. Subtract the rational unipotent term and put the compact error into a matrix w(q). In the Cayley coordinates the proof obtains a rational invertible matrix Z(q) = z(μ,y)w(q), with w(q) in a fixed compact set. The map q ↦ Z(q) is injective. In fact, equality of two such matrices makes the original rational representatives differ on the left by an element stabilizing the diagonal subspace. They represent the same Siegel coset. Fix μ and a centre y. For a nonempty finite collection of cosets using them, choose one member q₀. Multiplying by Z(q₀)−1 cancels the common left factor z(μ,y). The relative matrices w(q₀)−1w(q) are rational and belong to a fixed compact set, independent of μ, y and q₀. Each matrix entry belongs to a finite set of rational points, so there are at most N possible relative matrices. Left multiplication preserves injectivity. Each chosen centre can therefore account for at most N cosets. Summing over all available centres gives an upper bound even when some centres are unused and others occur for multiple cosets.
