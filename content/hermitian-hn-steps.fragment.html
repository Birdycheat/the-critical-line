Why do the two steps exist with these sizes?

The size properties of the two steps follow from the classical Harder–Narasimhan argument once sizes are read as slopes. Let f be the number of real places of the base field and d = |disc E|1/(2f) the root discriminant of the CM field E. The frames at x realize a sublattice N ⊆ Λ of rank rk N over 𝒪E as a ℤ-lattice ρN of rank 2f rk N in ℂfn, with the majorant metric at every place. Put deg N = −log covol(ρN) and μ(N) = deg N/(2f rk N). Since 𝒪E has covolume (d/2)f in ℂf, the normalized size is

size(N) = ((2/d)f rk N covol(ρN))1/(2f rk N) = (2/d)1/2 e−μ(N).

Small sizes are large slopes. The largest size of N is therefore (2/d)1/2e−μmin(N), where μmin(N) is the least slope of a nonzero quotient of N by a saturated sublattice. Similarly τ = (2/d)1/2e−μmax(Λ), where μmax(Λ) is the greatest slope of a nonzero sublattice. Fix t > 0 and put θ = log((2/d)1/2/t). Then F≤t is the sum Λθ of all nonzero N ⊆ Λ with μmin(N) ≥ θ, and F<t is the sum of those with μmin(N) > θ.

The step at a threshold. Write degθ N = deg N − 2fθ rk N. The condition μmin(M) ≥ θ says that degθ S ≤ degθ M for every S ⊆ M. The degree is supermodular, deg A + deg B ≤ deg(A+B) + deg(A∩B), and ranks add in the same way, so degθ is supermodular too. Consequently, if M satisfies the condition, then degθ T ≤ degθ(T+M) for every T ⊆ Λ. Indeed degθ T + degθ M ≤ degθ(T+M) + degθ(T∩M), and degθ(T∩M) ≤ degθ M. Four facts follow.

  • The sum is finite. Λ is a noetherian 𝒪E-module, so each step is the sum of finitely many of its generators.
  • Lower bound on quotient slopes. If M1 and M2 satisfy the condition, so does M1+M2. For S ⊆ M1+M2, the inequality above, applied with M1 and then with M2, gives degθ S ≤ degθ(S+M1) ≤ degθ(S+M1+M2) = degθ(M1+M2). Hence μmin(Λθ) ≥ θ when Λθ ≠ 0.
  • Saturation. Let N′ be the saturation of Λθ. For S ⊆ N′, degθ S ≤ degθ(S+Λθ) ≤ degθ N′, the second inequality because S+Λθ has finite index in N′. So N′ satisfies the condition and lies in Λθ.
  • Strictness. Every N with Λθ ⊊ N ⊆ Λ satisfies deg N − deg Λθ < 2fθ(rk N − rk Λθ). Otherwise choose a saturated N0 ⊋ Λθ maximizing the ratio (deg N0 − deg Λθ)/(rk N0 − rk Λθ), which is then at least 2fθ. For S ⊆ N0, first degθ S ≤ degθ(S+Λθ). Maximality of the ratio and the bound rk(S+Λθ) ≤ rk N0 then give degθ(S+Λθ) ≤ degθ N0. Thus N0 satisfies the condition and lies in Λθ, a contradiction. The ratio attains a maximum by finiteness at bounded covolume.
Why is the largest slope attained?

Saturating a sublattice preserves its rank and can only lower its covolume. For each possible rank, a lower bound on the degree difference in the ratio gives an upper bound B on the covolume. Only finitely many saturated sublattices meet that bound.

Indeed consider the underlying ℤ-lattice. In a rank-k sublattice of covolume at most B, Minkowski’s convex body theorem gives a nonzero vector of length at most √k B1/k. Its line has a primitive generator u in the ambient lattice. Saturation puts u in the sublattice, and the length bound leaves finitely many choices. For each fixed u, projection off its line leaves a saturated sublattice of rank k − 1 and covolume at most B/‖u‖ in the quotient lattice. Induction gives finitely many projected sublattices, and each has a unique preimage containing ℤu. This argument applies also to the 𝒪E-sublattices, since their saturated quotients are torsion-free over ℤ.

Why the degree is supermodular

Let A′ be the saturation of A in A+B, and C′ the saturation of A∩B in B. Covolumes multiply along saturated sublattices, the quotients carrying the quotient metric: covol(A+B) = covol(A′) covol((A+B)/A′) and covol(B) = covol(C′) covol(B/C′). Since C′ ⊆ A′ and C′ ∩ A = A∩B, the group C′/(A∩B) embeds in A′/A, so covol(A∩B)/covol(C′) ≤ covol(A)/covol(A′). The orthogonal projection away from the span of A maps B/C′ onto (A+B)/A′. It is injective, because an element of B with zero projection lies in A′, so one of its multiples lies in A∩B. It does not increase lengths, so covol((A+B)/A′) ≤ covol(B/C′). Multiplying these relations gives covol(A+B) covol(A∩B) ≤ covol(A) covol(B).

Translating back into sizes gives the stated properties. The open step equals Λθ′ for every θ′ slightly above θ, because its finitely many generators all have μmin > θ. Both steps are therefore saturated. A nonzero closed step has largest size at most t. For the open step the quotient-slope bound holds at θ′ > θ, so its largest size is strictly below t. Every nonzero sublattice of Λ/F≤t has the form N/F≤t. Strictness at θ gives it size greater than t. Applying strictness for θ′ decreasing to θ gives size at least t for every nonzero sublattice of Λ/F<t. Finally Λθ = 0 exactly when μmax(Λ) < θ, that is, when t < τ, and the open step vanishes exactly when t ≤ τ. In the Gaussian hyperbolic plane above, both non-strict inequalities are equalities: F≤¼ is a line of size ¼, and Λ/F<4 has size 4. At t = 4 the closed step is already the whole plane. Its two slope pieces have slopes log 4 and −log 4.

Why are the perpendicular steps dual, with reciprocal sizes?

Write ht(N) = (2/d)f rk N covol(ρN), so that size(N) = ht(N)1/(2f rk N), and normalize quotients in the same way. Heights then multiply along saturated sublattices. The reciprocal formula rests on one identity: for a saturated M ⊆ Λ,

rk M⊥Λ + rk M = n,   ht(M⊥Λ) · ht(Λ) = ht(M).

The general dual lattice. Let A be a lattice spanning V, let A∨ ⊆ Hom(V,E) be its 𝒪E-dual, realized with the dual frames, and let N0 be the annihilator of a saturated N ⊆ A. After identifying the two ambient spaces, the real inner product of ρx with 2ρ∨φ is TrE/ℚ φ(x). Through this pairing, the ℤ-dual of the quotient lattice A/N is the realized trace dual of A intersected with N0, scaled by 2. A lattice and its ℤ-dual have reciprocal covolumes, and covolumes multiply along ρN ⊆ ρA. Finally A∨ is the different times the trace dual, and the norm of the different is |disc E| = d2f. Write k = rk A − rk N. Scaling the trace dual by 2 multiplies its covolume by 22fk, while multiplication by the different multiplies it by d2fk. Hence the unnormalized dual annihilator has covolume (d/2)2fk covol(ρN)/covol(ρA). Applying the height normalization in ranks k, rk A and rk N gives ht(A∨ ∩ N0) · ht(A) = ht(N).

The Hermitian case. Self-duality says that y ↦ h(y,·) maps Λ onto Λ∨. This map sends M⊥Λ onto Λ∨ ∩ M0, and it is an isometry for the dual frames. Indeed each frame satisfies Xw*JXw = diag(±1), and conjugation in E is complex conjugation at every place. The general formula with A = Λ and N = M therefore gives the identity. For M = 0 it reads ht(Λ)² = ht(0) = 1, so ht(Λ) = 1 and ht(M⊥Λ) = ht(M). By the rank formula, (M⊥Λ)⊥Λ is a saturated lattice of the same rank containing M, hence equal to M. Thus ⊥ is an inclusion-reversing involution of the saturated sublattices. By multiplicativity, ht(M⊥Λ/M′⊥Λ) = ht(M′/M)−1 for saturated M ⊆ M′. For rk M < n this gives size(Λ/M) = 1/size(M⊥Λ), so the largest size of Λ is 1/τ.

Set C = F≤t and P = C⊥Λ. Every positive-rank quotient of P by a saturated sublattice is dual to a sublattice beyond C, of size greater than t. Its own size is therefore strictly below 1/t. In the other direction, sublattices of Λ/P have size at least 1/t: saturating can only decrease their size, and each saturated one is dual to a quotient of C of size at most t. These two bounds force P to be the open step at 1/t.

Why do these bounds determine the open step?

If P is nonzero, its largest quotient size is attained and is below 1/t, so P belongs to the sum defining the open step. The inclusion is automatic if P = 0. Conversely, project any generator L of that step to Λ/P. If the image is nonzero, the quotient L/(L ∩ P) has size below 1/t. Orthogonal projection onto the quotient by P is a contraction, so the image has no greater covolume than this quotient in their common rank. This contradicts the lower bound on sublattices of Λ/P. Thus every generator lies in P.

We have proved (F≤t)⊥Λ = F<1/t. Replacing t by 1/t and taking perpendiculars gives the second cutoff identity.

In the Gaussian plane above, h(e, ze + wb) = w, so (ℤ[i]e)⊥Λ = ℤ[i]e, in agreement with (F≤¼)⊥Λ = F<4. The non-isotropic line M = ℤ[i](e + b) has M⊥Λ = ℤ[i](e − b). Here f = 1 and d = 2, so heights are covolumes, and ht(M) = ht(M⊥Λ) = 1/16 + 16 = 257/16. The class of b generates Λ/M. Its squared length after projection off e + b is 16 − 16²/(257/16) = 16/257. Thus the quotient has size 4/√257, while the perpendicular line has size √257/4.
