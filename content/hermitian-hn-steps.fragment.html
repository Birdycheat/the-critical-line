Why do the two steps exist with these sizes?

The size properties of the two steps follow from the classical Harder–Narasimhan argument once sizes are read as slopes. Let f be the number of real places of the base field and d = |disc E|1/(2f) the root discriminant of the CM field E. The frames at x realize a sublattice N ⊆ Λ of rank rk N over 𝒪E as a ℤ-lattice ρN of rank 2f rk N in ℂfn, with the majorant metric at every place. Put deg N = −log covol(ρN) and μ(N) = deg N/(2f rk N). Since 𝒪E has covolume (d/2)f in ℂf, the normalized size is

size(N) = ((2/d)f rk N covol(ρN))1/(2f rk N) = (2/d)1/2 e−μ(N).

Small sizes are large slopes. The largest size of N is therefore (2/d)1/2e−μmin(N), where μmin(N) is the least slope of a nonzero quotient of N by a saturated sublattice. Similarly τ = (2/d)1/2e−μmax(Λ), where μmax(Λ) is the greatest slope of a nonzero sublattice. Fix t > 0 and put θ = log((2/d)1/2/t). Then F≤t is the sum Λθ of all nonzero N ⊆ Λ with μmin(N) ≥ θ, and F<t is the sum of those with μmin(N) > θ.

The step at a threshold. Write degθ N = deg N − 2fθ rk N. The condition μmin(M) ≥ θ says that degθ S ≤ degθ M for every S ⊆ M. The degree is supermodular, deg A + deg B ≤ deg(A+B) + deg(A∩B), and ranks add in the same way, so degθ is supermodular too. Consequently, if M satisfies the condition, then degθ T ≤ degθ(T+M) for every T ⊆ Λ. Indeed degθ T + degθ M ≤ degθ(T+M) + degθ(T∩M), and degθ(T∩M) ≤ degθ M. Four facts follow.

  • The sum is finite. Λ is a noetherian 𝒪E-module, so each step is the sum of finitely many of its generators.
  • Semistability. If M1 and M2 satisfy the condition, so does M1+M2. For S ⊆ M1+M2, the inequality above, applied with M1 and then with M2, gives degθ S ≤ degθ(S+M1) ≤ degθ(S+M1+M2) = degθ(M1+M2). Hence μmin(Λθ) ≥ θ when Λθ ≠ 0.
  • Saturation. Let N′ be the saturation of Λθ. For S ⊆ N′, degθ S ≤ degθ(S+Λθ) ≤ degθ N′, the second inequality because S+Λθ has finite index in N′. So N′ satisfies the condition and lies in Λθ.
  • Strictness. Every N with Λθ ⊊ N ⊆ Λ satisfies deg N − deg Λθ < 2fθ(rk N − rk Λθ). Otherwise choose a saturated N0 ⊋ Λθ maximizing the ratio (deg N0 − deg Λθ)/(rk N0 − rk Λθ), which is then at least 2fθ. For S ⊆ N0, first degθ S ≤ degθ(S+Λθ). Maximality of the ratio and the bound rk(S+Λθ) ≤ rk N0 then give degθ(S+Λθ) ≤ degθ N0. Thus N0 satisfies the condition and lies in Λθ, a contradiction. A maximizer exists because saturating does not decrease the ratio, a lower bound on the ratio bounds the covolume, and only finitely many saturated sublattices have bounded covolume. That finiteness follows from Minkowski’s theorem by induction on the rank. A lattice of rank k contains a nonzero vector of length at most √k covol1/k, there are finitely many such vectors in ρΛ, and the induction proceeds in the quotient by the saturated line each one spans.
Why the degree is supermodular

Let A′ be the saturation of A in A+B, and C′ the saturation of A∩B in B. Covolumes multiply along saturated sublattices, the quotients carrying the quotient metric: covol(A+B) = covol(A′) covol((A+B)/A′) and covol(B) = covol(C′) covol(B/C′). Since C′ ⊆ A′ and C′ ∩ A = A∩B, the group C′/(A∩B) embeds in A′/A, so covol(A∩B)/covol(C′) ≤ covol(A)/covol(A′). The orthogonal projection away from the span of A maps B/C′ onto (A+B)/A′. It is injective, because an element of B with zero projection lies in A′, so one of its multiples lies in A∩B. It does not increase lengths, so covol((A+B)/A′) ≤ covol(B/C′). Multiplying these relations gives covol(A+B) covol(A∩B) ≤ covol(A) covol(B).

Translating back into sizes gives the stated properties. The open step equals Λθ′ for every θ′ slightly above θ, because its finitely many generators all have μmin > θ. Both steps are therefore saturated. Semistability bounds the largest size of a nonzero closed step by t, and that of a nonzero open step strictly below t. Strictness at θ gives every nonzero quotient beyond the closed step a size greater than t. Strictness at every θ′ slightly above θ gives those beyond the open step a size at least t. Finally Λθ = 0 exactly when μmax(Λ) < θ, that is, when t < τ, and the open step vanishes exactly when t ≤ τ. In the Gaussian hyperbolic plane above, both non-strict inequalities are equalities: F≤¼ is a line of size ¼, and Λ/F<4 has size 4.

Why are the perpendicular steps dual, with reciprocal sizes?

Write ht(N) = (2/d)f rk N covol(ρN), so that size(N) = ht(N)1/(2f rk N), and normalize quotients in the same way. Heights then multiply along saturated sublattices. The reciprocal formula rests on one identity: for a saturated M ⊆ Λ,

rk M⊥Λ + rk M = n,   ht(M⊥Λ) · ht(Λ) = ht(M).

The general dual lattice. Let A be a lattice spanning V, let A∨ ⊆ Hom(V,E) be its 𝒪E-dual, realized with the dual frames, and let N0 be the annihilator of a saturated N ⊆ A. After identifying the two ambient spaces, the real inner product of ρx with 2ρ∨φ is TrE/ℚ φ(x). Through this pairing, the ℤ-dual of the quotient lattice A/N is the realized trace dual of A intersected with N0, scaled by 2. A lattice and its ℤ-dual have reciprocal covolumes, and covolumes multiply along ρN ⊆ ρA. Finally A∨ is the different times the trace dual, and the different has norm |disc E| = d2f. The normalization absorbs the factors of 2 and of the discriminant exactly, and ht(A∨ ∩ N0) · ht(A) = ht(N).

The Hermitian case. Self-duality says that y ↦ h(y,·) maps Λ onto Λ∨. This map sends M⊥Λ onto Λ∨ ∩ M0, and it is an isometry for the dual frames. Indeed each frame satisfies Xw*JXw = diag(±1), and conjugation in E is complex conjugation at every place. The general formula with A = Λ and N = M therefore gives the identity. For M = 0 it reads ht(Λ)² = ht(0) = 1, so ht(Λ) = 1 and ht(M⊥Λ) = ht(M). By the rank formula, (M⊥Λ)⊥Λ is a saturated lattice of the same rank containing M, hence equal to M. Thus ⊥ is an inclusion-reversing involution of the saturated sublattices. By multiplicativity, ht(M⊥Λ/M′⊥Λ) = ht(M′/M)−1 for saturated M ⊆ M′. In particular size(Λ/M) = 1/size(M⊥Λ), so the largest size of Λ is 1/τ. Transporting the size properties of the steps through ⊥ gives the reciprocal cutoffs, with the closed and open conventions exchanged.

In the Gaussian plane above, h(e, ze + wb) = w, so (ℤ[i]e)⊥Λ = ℤ[i]e, in agreement with (F≤¼)⊥Λ = F<4. The non-isotropic line M = ℤ[i](e + b) has M⊥Λ = ℤ[i](e − b). Here f = 1 and d = 2, so heights are covolumes, and ht(M) = ht(M⊥Λ) = 1/16 + 16 = 257/16, as the identity predicts.
