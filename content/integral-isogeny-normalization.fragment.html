How do the two ends of an elementary isogeny acquire integral models?

A point of the elementary Hecke correspondence records an abelian variety, its quotient by a prescribed subgroup and the markings on both. Forgetting the quotient gives the source projection. Keeping the quotient gives the target projection. To construct the integral correspondence we need both maps on the same scheme, including its special fibre.

Let R be a discrete valuation ring of characteristic zero and residue characteristic q. Let X be the smooth quasi-projective marked moduli scheme over R, at level N ≥ 3 prime to q. We work with the multiplicity-one unitary datum. Fix the maximal ideal 𝔴 containing q and ω − r, which selects the prescribed root r of the coefficient action. Fix an elementary index 0 ≤ j ≤ n and a multiplier c = qe, with e > 0 if j > 0. The starting moduli theorem supplies the correspondence Y₀ over U = X[1/q], its two finite étale maps s₀, t₀ to U and the marked isogeny between the two pulled-back abelian schemes.

The source projection. Normalize X in Y₀ using s₀. On an affine open of X this takes the integral closure of its ring in the functions on the inverse image in Y₀. These rings glue to a scheme Y with structural map s to X. Over U the given cover is finite and normal, so the same construction recovers Y₀ there. To see why s is finite, take a normal Noetherian affine domain D on one component of the smooth base. Each component of the generic cover gives a finite separable extension of its function field. Clear denominators in a field basis to obtain a vector-space basis consisting of integral elements. If z is integral over D, its trace pairing with each basis element belongs to D, since it is integral and lies in the fraction field of D. Nondegeneracy of the trace pairing puts all these z in the finite free module spanned by the dual basis. Its submodule of integral elements is finite because D is Noetherian. The Stacks Project, Lemma 10.161.8. The model is locally noetherian and flat over R. Its local rings are integrally closed domains. We may work one component at a time. The Stacks Project, Relative normalization.

The target projection. Fix y ∈ Y. Test a possible value for t(y) along a discrete valuation ring V. Its closed point maps to y, and its generic point lifts to a point x of Y₀. Two properties of the elementary correspondence enter here. First, t₀(x) extends to a point over V. Second, as the test varies through y, the possible target specializations form a finite set. Quasi-projectivity puts that set in one affine open of X. Its coordinate functions are then integral on every such test. The valuative characterization of the normal local domain 𝒪Y,y puts these rational functions in that local ring. This gives the map at y. Finite presentation spreads it to a neighbourhood, and separatedness makes the neighbourhood maps agree on overlaps.

To prove that the resulting target map t is finite, apply the valuative lifting result in the opposite direction, with the target specialization prescribed. This gives properness. The specialization bound with the two projections reversed, together with finiteness of s, gives finite fibres for t. A proper morphism with finite fibres is finite. The two valuative lifting results and the bounds on specializations are inputs about this moduli problem. The Stacks Project, Lemma 37.44.1.

X ←s Y →t X,   s|Y₀ = s₀,   t|Y₀ = t₀.

Here both restrictions are read as maps to X by the inclusion of U. Both projections commute with the maps to Spec R. Their special-fibre maps are finite by base change, and their restrictions to U are finite étale.

Why do the isogeny and its reverse companion extend?

Pull the universal abelian scheme over X back along the two integral projections. Call the resulting abelian schemes A and B over Y. Their restrictions to Y₀ are the marked source and quotient. The moduli datum includes homomorphisms ψ₀ ∶ A₀ → B₀ and ψ₀′ ∶ B₀ → A₀ whose composites in both orders are multiplication by c. The companion lets us certify the extended map on every fibre.

Extension of homomorphisms. For semiabelian schemes over a locally noetherian normal base, a homomorphism over a dense open extends uniquely over the base. A semiabelian scheme has smooth group fibres which are extensions of abelian varieties by tori. Abelian schemes are the case of trivial tori. One can apply the theorem at the local rings of Y. Each local ring is a normal domain whose generic point lies in Y₀. The local extension theorem for semiabelian homomorphisms supplies the map there. Uniqueness on the dense open identifies the local extensions on overlaps. Faltings and Chai, Degeneration of Abelian Varieties, I, Proposition 2.7.

Apply this theorem first to ψ₀, obtaining ψ ∶ A → B. Apply it again with A and B exchanged, obtaining ψ′. Each composite and the corresponding multiplication map extend the same generic homomorphism. Uniqueness gives

ψ′ ∘ ψ = [c]A,   ψ ∘ ψ′ = [c]B.

On each geometric fibre, ψ has finite kernel because it is a closed subgroup of A[c]. It is also surjective because its image contains the image of [c] on B, which is all of B. Thus ψ is an isogeny on every geometric fibre. Milne, Abelian Varieties, Proposition 7.1 and Theorem 7.2. The compatibility of ψ with the coefficient action follows by the same comparison of two homomorphisms. For every lattice label x in the level marking, the sections satisfy η′(x) = ψ(η(x)) on the dense open. They agree on all of the reduced scheme Y since B is separated. The remaining compatibility concerns the polarizing bundles.

How can the polarizing bundles be compared on the special fibre?

The marked objects already carry rigidified polarizing line bundles P on A ×Y A and P′ on B ×Y B. Rigidification gives, in particular, a trivialization at the pair of zero sections. An isogeny with multiplier c must identify P⊗c with the pullback of P′ by ψ × ψ. An isomorphism exists over Y₀. To prove existence over Y, form the difference of the two line-bundle classes in the Picard group, where tensor product is addition.

δ = (ψ × ψ)∗[P′] − c[P]   in   Pic(A ×Y A).

Two restrictions determine the answer. Suppose f ∶ Z → Y is smooth with geometrically connected fibres over a locally noetherian normal base and has a section s. A line-bundle class on Z is zero if it vanishes over a dense open of Y and its pullback along s is zero. To see the role of the section, consider any line bundle M on Y. The bundle f∗M can be trivial over the open even though M is nontrivial. Its pullback along s recovers M, since f ∘ s is the identity.

Here is the local argument for the criterion. Choose a generator of the bundle over the dense open and a generator along the section. Multiply the first generator by a unit from the open in the base so the two agree along the section. On a normal scheme one can check regularity of a rational section and of its inverse in codimension one. A vertical divisor maps to a divisor of the base by the dimension formula for smooth morphisms. The fibre at the generic point of this base divisor is smooth and geometrically connected, hence geometrically integral. Over the corresponding height-one point of the base, its uniformizer has order one along the fibre. The order of the normalized generator there is its order along that fibre, which its value on the section fixes at zero. Thus the local section and its inverse both extend and the local generators glue. The Stacks Project, the height-one criterion for reflexive modules and the flat local dimension formula.

Apply the criterion to Z = A ×Y A with section (0,0). The generic compatibility gives the first vanishing. The image of (0,0) under ψ × ψ is (0,0) on B ×Y B. Both bundles restrict there to the trivial bundle by rigidification, giving the second vanishing. Hence δ = 0 and an integral isomorphism of the two polarizing bundles exists. Together with the two companion identities this gives the required polarized isogeny over Y. The moduli theorem for the initial elementary correspondence and its valuative specialization theorems remain inputs to this construction.
