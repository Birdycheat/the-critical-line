What do the projector and its annihilators look like above 2?

We can see both the place projector and the two annihilator identities in an integer ring with just sixteen classes modulo 2. Put θ = √2, β = (1 + √−7)/2 and F = ℚ(θ,β). The defining relations are θ² = 2 and β(1 − β) = 2. The real field ℚ(θ) has degree two, and adjoining the nonreal number β doubles the degree again.

The two places. Write C for the ring of integers of F. Its integral basis is 1, θ, β, θβ, as verified below. Reducing this basis and its multiplication modulo 2 gives θ² = 0 and β² = β. Evaluating β first at 1 and then at 0 gives the two components

C/(2) ≅ S × S,   S = 𝔽₂[η]/(η²),   θ ↦ (η,η),   β ↦ (1,0).

Indeed a pair (u,v) of polynomials in η has inverse image βu(θ) + (1 − β)v(θ). The two residue maps to 𝔽₂ have kernels 𝔴 = (θ,1 − β) and 𝔳 = (θ,β) upstairs. To calculate 𝔴², all three generators θ², θ(1 − β), (1 − β)² are multiples of 1 − β. Conversely 1 − β = (1 − β)² + 2 belongs to 𝔴². Thus 𝔴² = (1 − β). Likewise 𝔳² = (β) since β = β² + 2. Multiplying gives (2) = 𝔴²𝔳². At either prime the ramification index is 2 while the residue degree is 1. The nilpotent η records the second layer at each place.

Before and after reduction. Select 𝔴 and take e = β, a = βθ, d = β²θ, c = β³, c′ = 1 and s = θ. The equations a(1 − e) = 2θ and ad = 2β³ hold in the integer ring. Also e² − e = −2. Modulo 2 the operators simplify to e = (1,0), a = d = (η,0) and c = (1,0). Thus e is the identity on the selected component, multiplication by η² kills that component, and c′ce = e. Here the index 2 gives d = ea. Modulo 2 the projector fixes a = (η,0), so the two residues agree. In the general construction their local orders are 1 and r − 1 before truncation.

Take x = (η,1 + η). Then ex = (η,0) = a(1,0) and ax = dx = 0. To write ex = du, the constant term of the first coordinate of u must be 1. Its η-coefficient and its entire other coordinate are free, giving eight choices. To write x = au + (1 − e)v, keep those eight choices for u, prescribe the second coordinate of v to be 1 + η, and choose its first coordinate freely. There are 32 pairs. For x = (1,1 + η), the first coordinate has a nonzero constant term, so neither representation is possible. The experiment lets you test every class and see which free choices disappear when the hypotheses fail.

Checking the integral basis

The quadratic subfields have integer rings ℤ[θ] and ℤ[β]. For squarefree h, write an algebraic integer z of ℚ(√h) as (t + v√h)/2. Its trace t and norm are integers, so h v² = t² − 4 Nm(z) is integral. A denominator in the rational number v would have its square dividing the squarefree integer h, hence v is integral. The congruence t² ≡ h v² modulo 4 forces both t, v even when h = 2, and gives them the same parity when h = −7. The displayed generators are integral by their monic quadratics. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, Remark 2.12 on quadratic integer rings.

Put U = ℤ[θ,β]. For an integer z of F, write z = u + vθ with u, v ∈ ℚ(β). The relative traces of z and θz are 2u and 4v. Both are algebraic integers in ℚ(β), being sums of conjugate integers. Hence 4z ∈ U. Write also z = h + jβ over ℚ(θ). The traces of z and βz are 2h + j and h − 3j, in ℤ[θ]. The determinant of these two equations is −7, so 7z ∈ U. Now z = 2(4z) − 7z also lies in U. Reduction by the two monic quadratic relations shows the four displayed elements span U, and degree four gives their linear independence.
