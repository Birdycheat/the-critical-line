Why do isogeny kernels form a finite étale family?

Fix a prime q, a Harris–Taylor datum of multiplicity one and rank n, and a Noetherian PEL base ring on which q is invertible. Let N ≥ 3 be prime to q. The datum fixes an integral quadratic element ω, its trace Tr(ω) = ω + ω̄ and norm Nm(ω) = ωω̄, with q prime to Tr(ω)² − 4Nm(ω). For an integer r put I = (q, ω − r) in the ring of integers 𝒪 of the CM field. Fix a maximal ideal 𝔴 containing I, an integer 0 ≤ j ≤ n, and c = qe with e ≥ 0 and e > 0 whenever j > 0. Let X be any fine marked model for these data.

Generic correspondence theorem. Marked polarized isogenies of multiplier c and the specified local type are classified by a scheme Y with two finite étale maps s, t to X, recording source and target. Every geometric source has an outgoing isogeny. We first construct the parameter space of possible kernels. We will then impose the markings, prove existence on each geometric fibre and identify the target projection.

Let S be locally Noetherian and let A be an abelian scheme over S. Fix a positive integer c which is invertible on S. We seek the outgoing isogenies f ∶ A → B for which multiplication by c factors through f. After pulling back to any test scheme over S, such an arrow has kernel contained in A[c]. The target can be recovered from this subgroup.

Because c is a unit, the group scheme A[c] is finite étale. A finite locally free subgroup of this torsion is étale as well. Its map to the base is unramified because it is a closed subscheme of the torsion, and finite local freeness gives flatness and finite presentation. Étale locally its points form a constant finite group G. Make one sheet of the parameter space for every subgroup of G. Relabelling the torsion sends subgroups to subgroups, so these local sheets glue to a finite étale scheme Z → S. On the sheet for H take all the points of H. These subsets glue as well, giving the universal subgroup in A[c] over Z. This construction also works when the torsion points cannot be labelled globally. Finite étale covers and monodromy.

By the quotient theorem for abelian schemes, form B = A/H and write f for the quotient map. Because [c] kills H, the quotient property gives a unique g ∶ B → A such that gf = [c]A. Now fgf = [c]Bf. The quotient map is faithfully flat, and equality of morphisms may be tested after this cover, so fg = [c]B. Conversely an isogeny with gf = [c]A gives a subgroup of A[c] and identifies its target with the quotient by that subgroup. Thus an arrow from an identified source determines a unique map to Z. Distinct subgroups of the same fibre can give distinct classifying maps. For a complex elliptic curve and c = 2 the five sheets before marking correspond to the zero subgroup, the three lines in (ℤ/2)² and the whole group. Invertible torsion on abelian schemes.

Where is invertibility of the torsion order needed?

The subgroup sheets were obtained by labelling the geometric points of a finite étale group. In residue characteristic q, consider the group αq given on a k-algebra R by the elements x ∈ R with xq = 0, under addition. Let k be algebraically closed of characteristic q. For each a ∈ k the graph y = ax in αq × αq is a subgroup of rank q, since its coordinate ring is k[x]/(xq). These graphs all have the same single point over k. They are distinct as subgroup schemes. Over R = k[ε]/(εq), the point (ε, aε) lies on the graph for b precisely when a = b. This is why the later special-fibre argument needs a new finiteness theorem for the permitted marked specializations.

Which part of an isogeny kernel determines its type?

Over the generic fibre, the elementary correspondence classifies outgoing isogenies with a prescribed local type. Let Y be a scheme over the generic PEL base. Suppose f ∶ A → B and g ∶ B → A are homomorphisms of abelian schemes over Y, with both composites equal to multiplication by c = qe, where q is the residue prime and e ≥ 0. The action on A contains the integers 𝒪 of the CM field. Fix I = (q, ω − r) and a maximal ideal 𝔴 containing I. Its norm N𝔴 is the cardinality of 𝒪/𝔴.

At a geometric point y, write H for the kernel of f on geometric points and H[𝔞] for those elements of H killed by every element of the ideal 𝔞. Write H[I∞] for the union of H[Ia] over a ≥ 1. In the unitary case of multiplicity one, the type j condition is the conjunction

H[I∞] ⊆ H[𝔴],   #H[𝔴] = (N𝔴)j.

Lemma. The points satisfying these two tests form an open-and-closed subscheme of Y. A map from any test scheme to Y factors through this subscheme exactly when the tests hold at all its geometric points. For the elementary correspondence one takes 0 ≤ j ≤ n, with e > 0 if j > 0. At general multiplicity m, the definition uses the exponent mj in the count.

Proof. From g ∘ f = [c] we have H ⊂ A[c]. The integer c is a unit on the base, so the c-torsion of each abelian scheme is finite étale. Étale locally A[c] and B[c] are constant finite sets. For a fixed ideal 𝔞 the tests f(x) = 0 and 𝔞x = 0 are equalities between entries of these finite sets. It suffices to use a finite set of generators of 𝔞. Their simultaneous solutions form a finite étale subscheme, with fibre H[𝔞]. Its locally constant degree counts the geometric points in the fibre. Taking 𝔞 = 𝔴 gives the second test. Invertible torsion Loci of fixed degree

For the first test there appear to be infinitely many exponents. In the finite ring 𝒪/(c), however, the descending sequence of images of Ia is eventually constant. Take E ≥ 1 at which it stabilizes. Every x in A[c] is killed by c, so the annihilator of x contains (c). It follows that some power of I kills x exactly when IE does. Now the first test just asks whether H[IE] ⊆ H[𝔴]. On each étale trivialization this is an inclusion of locally constant finite subsets. Its truth is locally constant and independent of their labelling. Intersecting the two loci proves the lemma, including the test after base change.

Two modules with the same order and the same 𝔴-count

Take 𝒪 = ℤ[i], q = 5 and r = −2, so I = 𝔴 = (2 + i) and N𝔴 = 5. Both finite modules below are killed by 25. In H₁ = 𝒪/(5), the factorization 5 = (2 + i)(2 − i) identifies the module with 𝔽₅ × 𝔽₅. The element 2 + i acts as zero on the first factor and as a unit on the second. Hence H₁[I∞] = H₁[𝔴] has 5 elements, although H₁ has 25. For H₂ = 𝒪/𝔴², the subgroup killed by 𝔴 is 𝔴/𝔴² and again has 5 elements. Now 𝔴² kills all 25 elements. Both modules give the count for j = 1, and only the first satisfies the inclusion. This example illustrates the two finite-module tests before imposing the polarization and the level markings.

The marked parameter space will impose this condition together with order stability and the polarization test.

Which quotients pass the polarization test?

Keep the quotient pair f ∶ A → B, g ∶ B → A with both composites equal to [c]. The source marking includes a bundle P on A × A, rigidified along the two axes. Pull it back by g × g. Evaluating along B × {x} gives a degree-zero bundle on B and thus a homomorphism h ∶ B → B∨. The zero of the dual represents the trivial rigidified bundle. The required test on the finite étale group B[c³] is

h(x) = 0   ⇔   [c]x = 0,   x ∈ B[c³].

Both arrows take this torsion group into finite étale torsion groups. Trivialize the three groups étale locally. On the finite sets of labels we can check both inclusions of kernels. Where an inclusion fails, the offending label persists on a whole sheet. Its image in the base is open and closed because the sheet sits in a finite étale cover. Intersecting the two good loci gives the required open-and-closed condition, compatible with every base change. On this locus the root construction gives a target bundle P′ satisfying (f × f)*P′ ≅ P⊗c.

Why must both inclusions be tested?

Take a principally polarized complex elliptic curve A and c = 2. If the subgroup H ⊆ A[2] has order d, then deg f = d and deg g = 4/d, since gf = [2]. Under the canonical identifications with the dual elliptic curves, the pullback induces g∨g = [deg g]. On B[8] all three possibilities for deg g divide 8. For d = 1 the pullback kernel is B[4], strictly containing B[2]. For d = 4 its kernel is zero, strictly contained in B[2]. For each of the three lines of order 2 the kernels agree. The isotropy test uses the mixed pairing on the two axes. Its square is identically one on the 2-torsion and would incorrectly admit all four points. This calculation isolates the polarization condition before imposing the order action and the specified local type. The dual isogeny of an elliptic curve.

How does the kernel test produce the target bundle?

Continue on the open-and-closed locus where the kernel test holds. Write λA ∶ A → A∨ for the homomorphism representing the source bundle P on A × A. In this description a bundle trivialized on both axes is obtained from the universal Poincaré bundle by the identity on the first factor and a homomorphism to the dual on the second. Pulling back along g in both coordinates gives h = g∨λAg. The kernel test gives B[c] ⊆ ker h, since every c-torsion point is already c³-torsion. This single inclusion suffices at this step.

Multiplication by c is a faithfully flat quotient by B[c]. Two lifts of a section through this quotient differ by an element killed by h. Hence h descends uniquely to a homomorphism λ′ ∶ B → B∨ with h = λ′[c] = cλ′. Pulling the Poincaré bundle back along (id, λ′) gives a bundle P′ trivialized along both axes. Additivity of that bundle in the dual coordinate identifies (g × g)*P with (P′)⊗c.

It remains to obtain the marking in the other direction. Since gf = [c]A,

c f∨λ′f = (gf)∨λA(gf) = c²λA.

Cancellation here takes place among homomorphisms of abelian schemes. If cD = 0 for a homomorphism D from A, then D[c]A = 0 and the faithfully flat map [c]A detects equality. It follows that f∨λ′f = cλA. The corresponding bundles, with both axes trivialized, therefore satisfy (f × f)*P′ ≅ P⊗c. The marked quotient uses these identities to establish positivity and the prime-to-residue-characteristic condition.

Which scheme classifies the marked quotients?

Inside the finite étale scheme Z of subgroups, impose three conditions on the universal subgroup H. It must be stable under the coefficient order, pass the two-sided polarization test, and have the prescribed local type. For order stability it suffices to test a finite set of additive generators of the order on the finite torsion labels. Like the other two tests, equality or inclusion of these finite labelled subsets is locally constant. Their intersection Y is open and closed in Z, so s ∶ Y → X is finite étale.

Over Y, each coefficient endomorphism of A descends to B = A/H by stability. The quotient map f is faithfully flat, so equality after precomposition with f proves the ring identities for the descended action. Its differential identifies the two Lie modules: the differential of the companion is inverse after division by the unit c. Hence the order action on tangent vectors and the ranks of its eigenspaces agree with those on the source.

The normalized Poincaré bundle used to define the target bundle supplies its two axial rigidifications. The two pullback identities for its homomorphism give symmetry and the Rosati adjoint relation. For instance the equality for an endomorphism and its adjoint can be checked after composition with the quotient and its dual, cancelling multiplication by c. The cth power of the target diagonal bundle is the pullback of the ample source diagonal bundle along the finite companion. Finite pullback preserves ampleness, and a line bundle is relatively ample if a positive power is. Thus the target diagonal bundle is relatively ample. Ampleness under finite surjections. Why does the source annihilator still kill the polarization kernel?

Let f ∶ A → B be the quotient, g its companion and c = qe their common composite multiplier. The source polarization λA has kernel killed by an integer m prime to q. For the descended polarization λ′ we know g∨λAg = cλ′. We show that the same m kills its kernel. This is where the reverse inclusion in the test on B[c³] is needed.

Take a section x of ker λ′ over an arbitrary test scheme. Composing the displayed identity with f∨ on the left gives cλA(gx) = 0 because gf = [c]. Thus m c g x = 0. Applying f and using fg = [c] gives c²m x = 0. After the faithfully flat base change obtained by pulling back multiplication by c, choose y with cy = mx. Then

c³y = c²mx = 0,   (g∨λAg)y = λ′(cy) = mλ′(x) = 0.

The reverse inclusion on B[c³] gives cy = 0. Hence mx = 0 on the cover and therefore on the original test scheme. The factor c³ enters because the lift of a c²-torsion section was taken through multiplication by c.

The primitive roots in the prime-to-q Tate marking

At a geometric point of the generic PEL base, fix any positive integer m prime to q. The two isogenies have composites [c], where c is a power of q. On m-torsion, choose d with dc ≡ 1 modulo m. Then d g is inverse to f on torsion. Applying f to the source marking gives bijections at every such level that respect the order action and commute with passage between levels. In particular the new level-N marking is fη.

There is also a pairing to preserve. The pullback of the target polarizing bundle along (f,f) is the cth tensor power of the source bundle. Functoriality and multiplicativity of the Weil pairing give

eλ′,m(fa,fb) = eλA,m(a,b)c.

Choose as the target primitive root the cth power of the source primitive root. It is still primitive because m is prime to c. These choices commute with the power maps, giving a single compatible marking of the tower. This explains why a level isomorphism of the underlying torsion groups alone would not supply the polarized marking.

To verify the universal property, start with a marked arrow over any test scheme T → X and identify its source with the prescribed pullback. Forgetting the marking gives a unique map to Z. Order stability and the type are part of the given arrow. For the polarization test, put h = g∨λAg. The marked pullback relation f∨λBf = cλA gives h = cλB after composing with the companion and cancelling c. If x ∈ B[c³] and h(x) = 0, then cx is killed both by c² and by the prime-to-q annihilator of ker λB. Thus cx = 0. The converse follows directly from h = λB[c]. This proves that the family factors through Y.

The identification with the universal quotient respects the descended order by uniqueness and respects the marked sections because their values are fη. The two target polarization homomorphisms have the same composite with f and its dual; composing with the companion lets us cancel c to see they coincide. The rigidified bundles therefore agree. Uniqueness of the original map to Z and the inclusion Y ↪ Z give uniqueness of the classifying map to Y, also for a nonreduced test scheme. The marked universal target now gives t ∶ Y → X by the fine moduli property.

Does every marked source admit an outgoing isogeny?

Let x be any point of X. Pull the universal abelian variety A back to an algebraic closure k of its residue field. The PEL base inverts every prime other than q, and we have also inverted q. A positive characteristic of k would therefore send a unit to zero. Thus the characteristic-zero torsion count applies to this fibre.

The polarization gives a perfect pairing on A[c](k) with adjoint involution complex conjugation on 𝒪. Put J = (q, ω − (Tr(ω) − r)), the conjugate of I. Reduction at 𝔴 makes r a root of the quadratic equation modulo q. The other root Tr(ω) − r is distinct because q is prime to the discriminant. The generators ω − r and ω − (Tr(ω) − r) thus have product zero and differ by a unit modulo q. Consequently IJ ⊆ (q) and I + J = 𝒪. Their primary parts give a direct sum in A[c]. Each part is isotropic: by the Chinese remainder theorem choose a scalar acting as one on this part and zero on the other. Its conjugate acts as zero on the first part, so adjointness makes the pairing trivial there. Nondegeneracy now pairs the two parts perfectly. If j > 0 then e ≥ 1 and A[𝔴] ⊆ A[c]. The characteristic-zero ideal-torsion count gives #A[𝔴](k) = (N𝔴)n at multiplicity one. Choose a j-dimensional 𝒪/𝔴-subspace V there. For j = 0 take V = 0 directly, including when c = 1. Let W be the subgroup in the J-summand orthogonal to V. Then V ⊕ W is its own orthogonal complement. Indeed, the J-coordinate of an element orthogonal to this subgroup lies in W by definition, and its I-coordinate lies in the double orthogonal of V, equal to V by perfect duality of finite abelian groups. The I-primary part is exactly V, killed by 𝔴 and of order (N𝔴)j.

The subgroup is stable under 𝒪 by adjointness and hence under the full order, since at multiplicity one the map from 𝒪 to the order is surjective. On the quotient B = A/H, isotropy descends the polarizing bundle and self-annihilation gives a prime-to-q annihilator of its kernel. The marked quotient construction then supplies the prescribed isogeny. The same kernel and type pull back to every extension of the algebraically closed geometric field. The outgoing classifying property now gives Spec k → Y above Spec k → X. Its image lies over x, which proves surjectivity of s. The finite étale source projection therefore has a point in every geometric fibre.

Descending the mixed pairing and its kernel on the geometric fibre

Write P for the source bundle on A × A, normalized on both axes, and f for the quotient by H. The restriction of P⊗c to either slice through a point h ∈ H is trivial because ch = 0. Translation in either coordinate thus preserves the bundle. Normalize the translation isomorphisms at the origin on the corresponding axis. The isomorphisms compose in each coordinate by this normalization; the commutator between the two coordinates is precisely the mixed pairing on H. Isotropy makes it one. The two actions therefore define an action of H × H on the bundle, so faithfully flat descent along f × f gives a bundle PB on B × B. Normalize it on the axes by removing its axial restrictions. Its pullback is still P⊗c. Restriction to the diagonal gives an ample pullback, so ampleness descends along the finite surjection f.

Let d, prime to q, annihilate the source polarization kernel. For x ∈ ker λB, lift x locally to y on A. The identity f∨λBf = cλA gives cd y = 0. Hence dy lies in A[c]. At a geometric point the mixed commutator of dy and h ∈ H is pulled back from the target. There, translation by fdy preserves the target bundle since λB(fdy) = 0. The other translation is by fh = 0, hence the commutator is one. Self-annihilation gives dy ∈ H and therefore dx = 0 at that point. Already cdx = 0 on the whole test scheme. Since B[c] is finite étale, the equalizer of the sections dx and zero is open and closed; equality at every geometric point gives equality on the entire test scheme. Thus the original integer d works for the target.

Why is the target projection finite étale?

The outgoing classifying scheme provides the finite étale map s. This alone gives no finiteness assertion for t. For an arrow ψ ∶ A → B take its companion g ∶ B → A, so gψ = [c] on A and ψg = [c] on B. The companion has type j at the conjugate ideal 𝔳 of 𝔴. The root parameter changes from r to Tr(ω) − r.

Why do the two kernels have the same local count?

Work at a geometric point with algebraically closed field k of characteristic zero. Write H and H′ for the kernels of ψ and g on k-points. For x ∈ H′, the target bundle restricted to {x} × B represents λB(x). Its pullback along ψ is trivial: ψ∨λB(x) = λA(gx) = 0. Here the adjoint identity follows from the polarization identity by cancellation along the surjective ψ. Translation by y ∈ H acts on a trivialization by a scalar E(x, y). Composition of translations and biadditivity of the polarizing bundle give a character in each variable. If the character of x is trivial, descent of the trivialization puts x in the kernel of λB. That kernel is killed by an integer prime to q, while cx = 0. Hence x = 0. Applying the same construction to the companion gives a character injection in the other direction. By Rosati adjointness, E(ax, y) = E(x, ay) for a ∈ 𝒪, where the bar denotes complex conjugation.

The two-primary decomposition applies to both kernels. Here J = (q, ω − (Tr(ω) − r)) is the conjugate of I, and 𝔳 is the conjugate of 𝔴. Their two distinct residue roots ensure that I + J = 𝒪 and IJ ⊆ (q).

For x ∈ H′J and y ∈ HJ, choose d so that Jd kills both, and choose a ∈ Id congruent to 1 modulo Jd. Then ay = y and ax = 0, so E(x, y) = 1. The character of x is determined on HI = H[𝔴], the equality following from the type condition and I ⊆ 𝔴. For b ∈ 𝔳, its conjugate kills H[𝔴], so the character of bx is trivial. Injectivity gives bx = 0. Hence H′J = H′[𝔳]. The reverse character injection gives the opposite bound by applying the same orthogonality argument with the two summands exchanged. Each of H[𝔴] and H′[𝔳] embeds in the character group of the other. A finite abelian group of order d has exactly d characters over k, as one sees on its cyclic factors by counting roots of unity. The two orders agree. Conjugation identifies 𝒪/𝔴 and 𝒪/𝔳, giving #H′[𝔳] = (N𝔳)j.

The two classifying maps. For a marked object A let SaA denote the same object with marking x ↦ ηA(qax). Write Y′ for the outgoing scheme of the conjugate type. Since ψ sends ηA to ηB, its companion sends ηB(x) to ηA(qex). It commutes with the order action, and pulling the polarizing bundle of A back along g × g gives the c-th power of that of B. Thus g ∶ B → SeA defines a classifying map F ∶ Y → Y′. Its source on Y′ is the original target B, with its original marking. Hence s′ ∘ F = t.

Choose h ≥ 0 with qe+h ≡ 1 mod N, possible because q has finite order in (ℤ/Nℤ)×. For a reverse arrow χ ∶ C → D, use its companion χ′ with the marking on its source corrected. The arrow χ′ ∶ ShD → C preserves markings since χ′ηD(qhx) = ηC(qe+hx) = ηC(x). This defines G ∶ Y′ → Y. A companion is unique because one can cancel the original, surjective isogeny in its defining equation. Taking the companion twice recovers the underlying arrow. Thus GF classifies ψ ∶ Se+hA → B and FG classifies χ ∶ C → Se+hD. In both cases Se+h fixes the level marking. The compatible squares of universal families and uniqueness in the two classifying properties give both identities as maps of schemes. Now t = s′ ∘ F is finite étale, since F is an isomorphism and s′ is finite étale.

For instance, with N = 7, q = 2 and e = 1, the first companion multiplies the level coordinate by 2. Its correction multiplies by 4. Their composite multiplies by 8, which fixes every level-seven marking.
