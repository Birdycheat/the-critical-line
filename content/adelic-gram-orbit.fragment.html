Why can the same columns be used at every place?

Fix a CM extension E/F with conjugation and an invertible Hermitian matrix J of size M. Let X be an ordered tuple of n rational columns in EM. When their Gram matrix is nonsingular, the columns are independent and form a frame for their span, which may be smaller than the ambient space. Stack them as a matrix and write T = X∗JX, where ∗ means conjugate transpose. Assume det T ≠ 0. Given an adelic tuple Y with this same matrix of pairings at every place, we seek one adelic isometry carrying X to Y. The first change of basis takes place among the n columns. It can be chosen over E before taking any completions.

Consider the pairing h on the space of column coefficients, with matrix T. For n > 0 there is a vector w of nonzero length h(w,w). To see this, suppose every length vanished. Expanding the length of u+v gives h(u,v)+h(v,u) = 0. Choose a nonzero θ with conjugate −θ by taking the difference of an element of E outside F and its conjugate. Expanding the length of u+θv then gives θ(h(u,v)−h(v,u)) = 0. Together the two identities force every pairing to vanish, contradicting nonsingularity. The nonzero length β is fixed by conjugation and therefore belongs to F. For every z, subtract w h(w,z)/β to obtain a vector orthogonal to w. The orthogonal complement is nonsingular because a vector in its radical would pair to zero with the whole space. Repeating the argument gives an invertible rational matrix S of new columns. The identity serves for the empty tuple. Thus

S∗TS = diag(β₁,…,βₙ),   βⱼ ∈ F×.

For example, over ℚ(i) take two columns e, f of lengths 0 and 2 with h(e,f) = 1+i. The Gram determinant is −2. Choose f first and then e−f(1−i)/2. Their lengths are 2 and −1, and their pairing is zero. The division by 2 is valid in every completion. At places above 2 its inverse lies outside the valuation ring, which matters when we ask whether a local isometry preserves an integral lattice.

The tuples XS and YS have the same diagonal Gram matrix. A unitary map g acts on the left, so g(XS) = YS implies gX = Y on multiplying both sides on the right by S−1. The same rational S and its inverse have integral entries outside finitely many places. We can therefore use this one diagonal model in the local tests that follow.

Which local inverses form an adelic inverse?

A finite adèle of F is a tuple (aᵥ) in the completions Fᵥ with aᵥ in the valuation ring 𝒪ᵥ outside a finite set. Its inverse is an adèle precisely when every aᵥ is nonzero and almost every aᵥ is a unit of 𝒪ᵥ. Indeed the only possible inverse has components 1/aᵥ. At a place where aᵥ is integral, its inverse is integral exactly when its valuation is zero. At the finitely many archimedean places one just needs a nonzero component. As an example over ℚ, take aₚ = p at every finite prime and a∞ = 1. This is an adèle with every component nonzero. Its componentwise inverse has negative valuation at every prime. In contrast, a fixed nonzero rational number and its inverse have a nonzero valuation at only finitely many primes. Sutherland, The idele group, Definition 26.2.

Apply this criterion to a denominator in a unitary change of frame. At a finite place put Aᵥ = Fᵥ ⊗F E. This is a quadratic field or a product of two copies of Fᵥ. In both cases the norm of δ is N(δ) = δδ. A nonzero norm gives the explicit inverse δ/N(δ). In a split algebra the norm tests both components. For example, (0,1) is a nonzero element with zero norm. Each of the four candidate phases gives a scalar norm fⱼ. Why do they generate the unit ideal over the full ring of base adèles? For t = a+bθ, their two differences are 4βa and 4β(Pa−Qbd). The scalars 4β and Qd are nonzero elements of F and therefore have adelic inverses. Hence the ideal generated by the four norms contains a and b. For the phase +1 we have f₁ = (β−a)²−db², so β²−f₁ belongs to the ideal generated by a and b. Adding f₁ shows that the ideal of the four norms contains β². Dividing by β² shows that a linear combination of the norms with adelic coefficients equals one. Evaluating this identity at an exceptional place produces at least one nonzero fⱼ. This suffices in the local field even at a ramified or dyadic place. For instance, over ℚ₂(i) take β = t = 1 and the phases ±1, ±i. Their denominators are 0, 2 and 1±i. None is an integral unit, yet 2 is invertible in the field. Where all the coefficients and norms are integral, at least one fⱼ must be an integral unit, since otherwise the sum would lie in the maximal ideal.

Select an admissible index at each place. Both the numerator δ and the inverse of the selected norm are then integral at almost every finite place. Consequently the entries of the elementary unitary corrections, and of their inverses, are adèles in the chosen coordinates. Different places may use different indices. The four indicator functions of the chosen sets take values in {0,1}, hence are integral everywhere. Multiplying each candidate by its indicator and adding realizes the local choices as a single adelic coefficient. This construction applies at each of the finitely many column steps. Each new correction fixes the already matched columns because they are orthogonal to both vectors used in that correction.

How does a pointwise lift give a single compact family?

Write G for the adelic unitary group preserving J. By the column construction and the rational change of basis, every adelic tuple Y of Gram matrix T equals gX for some g ∈ G. Conversely, an isometry preserves all pairings, so the orbit of X is exactly the fibre Z of the Gram map at T. Without the nonsingularity hypothesis, equality of Gram matrices can leave several orbits. In a hyperbolic plane the zero vector and a nonzero isotropic vector both have Gram matrix (0). Every invertible linear map fixes zero.

For the shell bound we need one compact family of such g as Y varies in a given compact set Ω. We first check the topology needed to pass from points to compact sets. In any basis of E over F, an adelic coefficient is a pair of base adèles. Conjugation is linear in these coordinates and multiplication is polynomial. The tuple space is therefore locally compact and Hausdorff, and the entries of the Gram matrix are continuous. The fibre Z is closed. Give an invertible matrix the topology of the pair (g,g−1). In the product of the two matrix spaces, the equations gh = hg = I and g∗Jg = J define a closed set. This gives G local compactness, the Hausdorff property and a countable basis, inherited from the finite powers of the adèle ring. The action is continuous by matrix multiplication.

Why the adelic orbit map is open. Given any neighborhood W of the identity in G, choose a compact neighborhood K with K−1K ⊂ W. The translates of the interior of K cover G, and second countability gives a countable subcover. Transitivity then covers Z by countably many translates of the compact, hence closed, set KX. By the Baire category theorem at least one of these closed translates has nonempty interior in Z. Translating back, choose kX in the interior of KX with k ∈ K. Then k−1 int(KX) is a neighborhood of X inside K−1KX ⊂ WX. Translating this inclusion to each group element proves that g ↦ gX is open onto Z. The Stacks Project, Lemma 5.13.3, Baire category theorem.

Take a relatively compact open neighborhood of each group element. Their images under this open map cover Z, so finitely many images cover Ω ∩ Z. The union of the closures of those finitely many neighborhoods is a compact family of adelic isometries giving all the required lifts.

This compactness controls all places together. To see its force, consider the open sets of matrices for which both the matrix and its inverse have integral coordinates outside a prescribed finite set of places. They cover G as the finite set varies. A compact set of representatives meets the requirement for one common finite set, by taking the union from a finite subcover. In coordinates one can enlarge that set to include the finitely many primes in the multiplication table of the chosen rational basis. Applied to the hyperbolic pair, the compact family transports the fixed pair to each of the pairs used in the shell comparison.
