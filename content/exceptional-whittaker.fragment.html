What replaces the good-place formula at an exceptional finite place?

The good-place count assumes a particular test function, the indicator of the full matrix lattice. A test supported on just one residue class can change the answer even at an unramified prime. The local argument works at every finite place and gives an entire function of the complex parameter z. Its value at s₀ = (M−n)/2 is the density of the chosen test at the target Gram matrix. Fix the local quadratic algebra Ev = Fv ⊗F E, the local component ψv of the adelic additive character, a nondegenerate Hermitian form J of rank M, and a Hermitian target T of size n whose determinant is invertible in Ev. Why can one compact ball determine an entire function? On a shell R < ‖b‖ ≤ 2R, change variables by b ↦ (1+x)∗b(1+x), with x in a small additive matrix ball of radius C/R. These changes preserve the shell, its Haar measure, the lattice index and the integral containing the positive Gram phase. The negative target phase acquires a term linear in x and a term quadratic in x. The latter has norm bounded by a constant times R(C/R)², so the character is trivial on it for large R. Invertibility of T lets one choose C so that at every b in the shell the linear term gives a nontrivial character of the x-ball. Its Haar integral is zero. Averaging the change of variables therefore kills the shell integral for every z, with one cutoff valid for all z. At a singular target the linear term can lose this oscillation, and the stated density hypothesis covers only the regular set. Choose a ball B₀ beyond that cutoff and double its radius successively. Adding any of the resulting shells leaves the integral over B₀ unchanged. On B₀ the index and its logarithm are bounded, so one can differentiate the compact integral with respect to z. This gives an entire Wv, with no poles. A separate absolute estimate identifies it with the original integral on a right half-plane. For radii growing by a fixed local dilation, the jth outer shell has r(b) ≥ 2j and volume at most a constant times κj. Thus the integral of the absolute value has a geometric majorant when Re z is large. The statement requires existence of a real threshold depending on the data. How does the test enter the value? Let g be the supplied continuous density of the pushforward of Φ dμ by X ↦ X∗JX on the open set of invertible targets. At z = s₀ the lattice weight is one. Integrating over a large additive ball Λ in b retains those X whose Gram matrix belongs to T+Λ⊥. Indeed, the average of the phase on Λ is one when the difference annihilates Λ and zero otherwise. The annihilator shrinks into the regular set around T, and self-duality gives ν(Λ)ν(Λ⊥) = 1. The integral is therefore an average of g near T. By continuity it approaches g(T). Compute the entire factor for one residue class at 3

Take E = ℚ(i), F = ℚ and the inert prime 3. Let J be the two-dimensional identity, n = 1, T = 1 and L₁ = L₂ = 𝒪Eᵥ. Use a character of conductor ℤ₃. The standard integral lattices have self-dual volume one here. Let Φ be the indicator of X ≡ (1,0) modulo 3. There are four scalar coordinates over ℤ₃, so this class has mass 1/81. Its Gram value N(X₁)+N(X₂) belongs to 1+3ℤ₃. For each lift to the next power of 3, the derivative in the real coordinate of X₁ is 2 modulo 3. For each choice of the other three digits, exactly one digit of this coordinate gives the required next Gram digit. Thus the mass is uniform on 1+3ℤ₃, a set of volume 1/3, and the density there is 1/27.

Integration of the character on this coset, including the negative target phase, gives u(b) = (1/81) · 𝟙3⁻¹ℤ₃(b). On ℤ₃ the lattice index is one. On the shell 3⁻¹ℤ₃ ∖ ℤ₃, of volume two, its value is nine because the residue field of Ev has nine elements. In this example the original integral has compact support and converges at every z.

W₃(z) = (1 + 2 · 9−(z−1/2))/81,   W₃(1/2) = 1/27.

Now keep J = I₂ and T = 1, and restore the full-lattice test. There are 24 norm-one columns modulo 3. At every such column at least one scalar derivative of the norm is nonzero modulo 3, so each residue class contributes the same density 1/27. Summing the 24 contributions gives 24/27 = 8/9, the good-place answer in these same dimensions.

Which domain reaches the global coefficient? The local proof uses no rank inequality to construct the entire function once a density is given. The global theorem supplies these densities from the normalized Gram mean when n < M and either n+2 ≤ M or J is anisotropic. It chooses a finite set S outside which the forms, measures, lattices and test meet the good-place conditions. On Re z > 0 multiply the archimedean function, the finitely many entire factors Wv for v ∈ S, and the convergent good-place product. Farther right this equals the adelic integral for the full-rank Fourier coefficient. The point s₀ lies inside the global half-plane by the strict rank inequality. Substituting the local density values gives the normalized Gram mean there. The resulting global continuation is holomorphic on Re z > 0.
