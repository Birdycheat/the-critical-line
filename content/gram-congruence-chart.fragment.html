How do the entries of a Hermitian matrix give local coordinates?

Let R be a completion of the totally real field F, and K = R ⊗F E for the CM extension E/F. At a real place K = ℂ. At a finite place it is a quadratic local field or the product R × R with the two factors interchanged. Write * for transpose followed by conjugation. Fix an invertible Hermitian matrix S of size n. We seek a continuous choice of q near the identity that solves q*S q = T as T varies near S.

First choose coordinates in which the centre is diagonal. Even an invertible matrix can have a zero first diagonal entry, as does the two-by-two matrix with ones off the diagonal and zeros on it. A nonsingular Hermitian form of positive dimension has a vector of nonzero length. Indeed, if every length vanished, expanding the lengths of v + w and v + αw, with α an invertible scalar whose conjugate is −α, would force both the sum and the difference of a pairing and its conjugate to vanish. Every pairing would be zero. A vector of nonzero length splits off an orthogonal line. To do this, subtract from any vector its projection onto that line, dividing by the length. The orthogonal complement is nonsingular, so repeating gives S = b*D b with b invertible and D = diag(d₁,…,dₙ), each dᵢ in R×. At a finite place this means nonzero in the local field. The length may have positive valuation.

Triangular coordinates near D. Write the first row of a nearby Hermitian matrix as (p, r), and let C be its lower-right block. If p ≠ 0, direct multiplication gives

(prr∗C)=N1∗diag(p,C−r∗r/p)N1,   N1=(1r/p0I).

The next pivot is the first diagonal entry of the smaller matrix C − r*r/p. At D the successive pivots are exactly the nonzero dᵢ. All these computations are continuous where the earlier pivots are nonzero, so every pivot stays nonzero on an open neighborhood of D. If N′ is the triangular matrix for the smaller block, the full triangular matrix is diag(1,N′)N₁, with N₁ the first upper triangular factor in the displayed identity. Recursion uniquely recovers the pivots pᵢ and an upper triangular matrix N with diagonal entries one. The inverse map sends these coordinates to N* diag(p₁,…,pₙ) N. Both directions are continuous. At size zero there is one empty matrix and no pivot to choose.

How does a chart select one change of basis?

Choose an open subgroup U ⊂ R× on which squaring maps homeomorphically onto an open subgroup U². For real R one may use the positive numbers. Restrict the triangular chart to pᵢ ∈ dᵢU². This is open and contains D. Each ratio pᵢ/dᵢ now has a unique square root uᵢ in U, depending continuously on the pivot. With A = diag(u₁,…,uₙ)N, multiplication gives

A*D A = N* diag(d₁u₁²,…,dₙuₙ²) N.

These matrices A form a subgroup L because triangular multiplication multiplies diagonal entries, and inversion inverts them. In the original basis take Q = b−1L b. Then q = b−1A b satisfies q*S q = b* (A*D A) b. Thus q ↦ q*S q is a homeomorphism from Q onto an open neighborhood of S. Its inverse is the required section, with value 1 at S. Congruence here is a right action on forms. In the fibre integration argument one uses the left action in which h sends a tuple X to Xh−1 and its Gram matrix T to (h−1)*T h−1. Inversion in Q relates the two parametrizations.

Which square root does the chart take above 2?

At any finite place the derivative of u ↦ u² at 1 is 2. The local field has characteristic zero, so this derivative is invertible there, also when 2 has positive valuation. The inverse function theorem gives a continuous inverse on a sufficiently small neighborhood. A small closed ball about 1 of radius less than one is open in the ultrametric topology and forms a subgroup. The chart can use such a ball inside the inverse-function neighborhood. Glöckner, generalized implicit function theorem 5.2.

One can see the permitted size directly. Normalize the valuation by ord(π) = 1 for a uniformizer π, set e = ord(2), and take an integer k > e. For u = 1 + πkz, its square is 1 + 2πk(z + αz²), with α = πk/2 in the maximal ideal. The difference of z + αz² at z and z′ is (z − z′)(1 + α(z + z′)). The second factor is a unit. The map therefore preserves distances and is injective modulo every πj. Each residue ring is finite, so the map is also surjective there. The unique solutions for successive precisions are compatible, and completeness gives a unique solution in the local integers 𝒪. Thus (1 + πk𝒪)² = 1 + 2πk𝒪.

For R = ℚ₂ and n = 1, centre the chart at S = 1 and take U = 1 + 4ℤ₂. Its square image is 1 + 8ℤ₂. The target 9 has square roots 3 and −3 in ℚ₂. The section takes −3 because −3 ≡ 1 modulo 4. At the centre it takes 1.
