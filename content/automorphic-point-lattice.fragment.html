How do the rational vectors recover the finite lattice?

The filtration uses rational sublattices, although a point of the adelic quotient specifies a lattice at every finite place. Let E/F be the CM extension, h the nondegenerate Hermitian form on En, and L the fixed, finitely generated 𝒪E-lattice spanning that space. Write 𝔸F,f for the finite adeles of F and A = E ⊗F 𝔸F,f. Inside A, let S be the ring generated by 𝒪E and the integral finite adeles of F. Embed rational vectors diagonally and write S·L for the S-span of L. For the finite component gf of the point x, put

M = gf(S·L),   Λx = En ∩ M,   S·Λx = M.

For the last equality, choose nonzero a, b ∈ 𝒪F with aSn ⊆ M ⊆ b−1Sn. A set of generators of L gives an upper denominator, and expressing the coordinate vectors in their E-span gives a lower multiple. Clearing the entries of gf and gf−1 transports both bounds to M. Since E ∩ S = 𝒪E, these bounds show that Λx is a finitely generated lattice of full rank. Now take u ∈ M. Finite approximation gives u = q + aw with q ∈ En and w ∈ Sn. The lower bound puts the error in M, so q ∈ Λx. Each a times a coordinate vector also lies in Λx. Their S-linear combinations include aw, proving the equality.

Why is a diagonal element of the integral ring an algebraic integer?

Choose an F-basis bj of E consisting of algebraic integers and let b′j be its trace-dual basis. For e ∈ E ∩ S, each TrE/F(ebj) is integral at every finite place. Indeed ebj lies in S, whose elements are sums of products of an algebraic integer and an integral finite adele of F. The trace of an algebraic integer belongs to 𝒪F. A diagonal element of F integral everywhere finite has no prime in its denominator. Thus the trace coordinates of e belong to 𝒪F. The module E ∩ S lies in ∑j 𝒪Fb′j and is finitely generated over ℤ, since the ring of integers is a finite ℤ-module. It contains 1 and is preserved by multiplication by e. The finite-module criterion makes e integral over ℤ. The opposite inclusion follows from the definition of S. Why can the error be put in the prescribed multiple of the integral ring?

For one finite coordinate u clear a nonzero denominator d ∈ 𝒪F, so du ∈ S. Modulo da, an integral finite adele of F is represented by one element of 𝒪F. Indeed only the prime powers dividing da impose conditions, and each local residue lifts to the local ring by density in its completion, and the Chinese remainder theorem supplies one global representative. As S is generated by these adeles and 𝒪E, there is an r ∈ 𝒪E with du − r ∈ daS. Dividing by d gives the required approximation by r/d. Residues at one prime power · Chinese remainder theorem.

Why is the lattice at the point self-dual?

Assume now that L is self-dual for h and that gf preserves h. Here h is conjugate-linear in its first argument. Self-duality means that testing the pairings against lattice vectors decides membership. For the rational intersection this gives

y ∈ Λx   ⇔   y ∈ En and h(z,y) ∈ 𝒪E for every z ∈ Λx.

First, S·L is self-dual over S. Its pairings are integral because those on L are integral and conjugation preserves S. Conversely, suppose v pairs integrally with S·L. Choose a nonzero m ∈ 𝒪F with mSn ⊆ S·L and write v = q + mw as above. For z ∈ L, subtracting h(z,mw) from h(z,v) puts h(z,q) in S ∩ E = 𝒪E. Self-duality of L gives q ∈ L, hence v ∈ S·L.

The isometry gf transports this equality with the dual to M. A rational y pairs integrally with Λx exactly when it pairs S-integrally with its full finite span M. Self-duality of M now gives the displayed test. A right factor stabilizing S·L preserves M, so the rational lattice depends only on the finite coset.

Where does the isometry hypothesis enter?

In the Gaussian hyperbolic plane take h((a,b),(c,d)) = ad + bc and 𝒪 = ℤ[i]. Start from 𝒪². At the place over 3, scale the first coordinate by 3 and the second by ⅓. Use the identity elsewhere. The rational intersection is 3𝒪 × ⅓𝒪. Its two basis vectors test 3y₂ ∈ 𝒪 and y₁/3 ∈ 𝒪, exactly the two membership conditions. Scaling only the first coordinate gives 3𝒪 × 𝒪. Its dual contains (1,0), which the lattice omits.

Which positive norms accompany the Hermitian lattice?

Fix a real place of F. It has a pair of conjugate embeddings of E, since E/F is totally imaginary quadratic. At the chosen embedding let H represent h and choose the frame P of the datum with P*HP = D, where D is diagonal with entries ±1. The archimedean component U of the point in this frame preserves D. Set X = PU. At the conjugate embedding use the conjugate frame. In either case X*HX = D for the corresponding embedded form.

vy = X−1w(y),   |w(h(y,z))| = |vy*Dvz| ≤ ‖vy‖ ‖vz‖.

Here w is the embedding used for the coordinates. The diagonal signs preserve Euclidean length, so projecting one vector onto the line of the other gives the inequality. In particular the norm in the frame is positive even on nonzero isotropic vectors. An archimedean compact factor on the right sends X to Xk, so vy changes by the Euclidean unitary transform k−1. Their lengths are therefore well defined on the quotient. The filtration uses these positive norms to measure covolumes, and the perpendicular step uses the integral pairing on the same rational lattice.
