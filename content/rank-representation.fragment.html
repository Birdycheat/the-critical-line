Why can we adjoin every group matrix and keep scalar corners?

So far the algebra A is generated by the tangent space. To obtain a representation of Γ from block coefficients, we need an algebra containing ρ(Γ). We use the row identity to enlarge A while retaining the two properties used to construct matrix units.

Take R to be the linear span of all products aρ(g). It contains the identity, A and ρ(Γ). It is closed under multiplication because ρ(g)bρ(g)−1 belongs to A for each b ∈ A. Each generator also preserves every Γ-invariant subspace. To compute its diagonal corner, write aρ(g) = ρ(g)b, with b = ρ(g)−1aρ(g) ∈ A. If the corner is nonzero, the row identity replaces piρ(g) by piw. The corner becomes piwb pi, a scalar multiple of pi by the product calculation. The zero corner is already scalar. Taking linear combinations proves the same assertion on R.

We also need to detect nonzero elements of R. Decompose V into irreducible Γ-subspaces using semisimplicity. If x ∈ R is nonzero, choose a summand W and v ∈ W with xv ≠ 0. Since R preserves W, the vector xv lies in W. Its Γ-translates span a nonzero invariant subspace of W, hence span W. In particular some linear combination b of group matrices sends xv to v. We have b ∈ R and bx fixes the nonzero vector v. No power of bx can be zero. This proves the nilpotence test used to construct the matrix units below.

How do the blocks give a representation of dimension m?

Proposition. The semisimple representation ρ contains an m-dimensional representation r and embeds in a finite direct sum of copies of r. Apply the block coefficient construction to the algebra R just obtained.

The identity has coefficient matrix Im. The displayed rule therefore makes r(g) = (cij(ρ(g))) a representation on Km, with inverse r(g−1). It is continuous for the ℓ-adic topology when ρ is continuous. For a nonzero block, choose an entry where Eij is nonzero. Dividing the corresponding entry of piρ(g)pj by that fixed entry expresses the coefficient as a continuous linear function of ρ(g).

Let rC denote the action on the coordinates of class C. Choosing a nonzero vector v ∈ Vb gives one copy of rC inside V. Its coordinate vectors are the independent vectors uiv in the distinct spaces Vi, and the formula for ρ(g) on them is exactly the coefficient matrix. Taking a whole basis of Vb gives a direct sum of dC = dim Vb such copies and spans all the corresponding eigenspaces. Thus

r = ⊕C rC,   ρ ≃ ⊕C rC⊕dC.

Every dC is positive. Taking one copy per class gives r ↪ ρ. With k the largest multiplicity, assign the copies of each class to the first dC of the k available slots to get ρ ↪ r⊕k. Since any subrepresentation of a semisimple representation is semisimple, the first injection also gives semisimplicity of r. Indeed, the intersection with r of an invariant complement in ρ to any subrepresentation of r is a complement in r.

For an example with unequal multiplicities, let Γ = GL₂(ℤ₅) × ℤ₅×. Its element (A, u) acts on a five-dimensional space by diag(A, A, u). The three-dimensional r is diag(A, u). The first injection sends (v, z) to (v, 0, z). The second sends (v, w, z) to ((v, z), (w, 0)). The two-dimensional action occurs twice in ρ and the character occurs once. At the elements A = diag(1, t), u = t⁴ for t ∈ ℤ₅×, these are the weights 0, 1, 4 seen above. In the geometric application the two injections transfer different local information.
