Why does the target of a tested point extend over the valuation ring?

The construction of the target projection uses two valuative inputs. For the integral model, the base R is a characteristic-zero discrete valuation ring with q in its maximal ideal. A test uses a discrete valuation ring V over the PEL base with fraction field K. Every integer prime to q is a unit there, so the residue characteristic of V is zero or q. Fix N ≥ 3 prime to q. A marked object o over V consists of an abelian scheme with its coefficient action, polarizing bundle rigidified along both axes, and level structure satisfying the Lie and Tate conditions. Let ψK : oK → o′ be a polarized isogeny of multiplier c = qe. It is compatible with the coefficients and the level structure, pulls the polarization of o′ back to c times that of oK, and has a companion ψ′ with ψ′ψ = [c] and ψψ′ = [c]. Then o′ is the generic fibre of a marked object over V, and ψK extends.

The quotient. The companion makes ψK finite, flat and surjective, and puts its kernel Z inside AK[c]. Let 𝒢 be the schematic closure of Z in A. It is flat over V, it is a subgroup scheme, and it is killed by c because its generic fibre is schematically dense. Its inclusion in the finite scheme A[c] makes 𝒢 finite. The quotient by this finite flat subgroup is an abelian scheme B = A/𝒢. To see where invariant functions enter, take an affine open in the target of [c]. Its inverse image under this finite map is affine and stable under 𝒢. The invariant rings on these inverse images give quotient charts that glue. The flat surjections AK → BK and ψK have the same kernel, so BK is identified with the target of ψK.

The quotient has the correct generic fibre. To obtain a point of the fine moduli scheme we must also extend the coefficient action, the polarizing bundle and the level. The extension of the marking uses the invertible discriminant to preserve the Lie ranks and prime-to-q torsion to preserve the Tate condition.

Extension of the marking on the quotient

Each coefficient endomorphism extends uniquely, by the extension of homomorphisms over a normal base. The polarizing bundle extends from the generic fibre because the total space is regular and integral. A bundle that is trivial on the generic fibre of an abelian scheme over V is trivial, so the rigidifications, the symmetry and the Rosati isomorphisms extend as well. For an extended bundle on the square of an abelian scheme over a discrete valuation ring, relative ampleness of its diagonal restriction extends from the generic fibre. The polarization kernel is finite and flat, so the integer prime to q that kills it generically kills it over V.

Each level section extends uniquely, since B(V) = B(K) for the proper scheme B. The discriminant of ω is a unit and the differences of the distinct embedding characters generate the unit ideal. The Lie condition therefore separates their eigensummands over V with the prescribed generic ranks. For the Tate condition, prime-to-q torsion is finite étale. After choosing a geometric specialization, its points and their Weil pairings specialize isomorphically. The companion also extends by the homomorphism extension theorem. Its two composites are [c] by uniqueness, and comparison of the polarizing bundles extends the multiplier identity.

The two inputs. Let y ∈ X(V) have generic point s0(x) with x ∈ Y0(K). Pull back the universal object along y and the marked isogeny of the correspondence along x. The extended target is classified by a unique z ∈ X(V), and uniqueness over K gives z|K = t0(x). This is the first input. For the reverse input, the companion only satisfies ψ′∘η′ = η∘[c] on level structures. Choose f with qe+f ≡ 1 mod N and replace the level of the target object by its composite with [qf]. Write SfB for the target with that marking. It already extends in the reverse test. The companion is a marked arrow SfB → A since ψ′η′(qfx) = η(qe+fx) = η(x). The extension lemma now gives the original source over V. For N = 5, q = 2 and e = 1 one takes f = 3, since 2⁴ ≡ 1 mod 5. The good reduction of the target follows from the quotient construction.

What does the quotient look like for an elliptic curve?

Let E be an elliptic curve over V and C ⊂ EK a subgroup of order q. Its closure C̄ lies in E[q], so it is finite and flat of order q, and E/C̄ is an elliptic curve over V. If the special fibre has characteristic q and is supersingular, the geometric special fibre of the subgroup has only the identity as a point. Its local algebra has length q, so the q-th power of each element in its maximal ideal is zero. Relative Frobenius kills this subgroup. It has the same order as the Frobenius kernel and therefore equals it. On this geometric fibre the quotient is the Frobenius twist of the elliptic curve.

Why are there only finitely many target specializations?

Fix a point u of X. Consider all tests: a discrete valuation ring V, a point x of Y0 over its fraction field, and V-points y′, z′ extending s0(x) and t0(x), with y′ ending at u. The images in X of the closed points of Spec V under these z′ form a finite set. Finiteness of the reverse set, for a fixed endpoint of z′, is used for the finite fibres of t.

Points where q is invertible. If u lies in U = X[1/q], so does all of y′. The source map of the correspondence is finite, so the valuative criterion lifts y′ to l ∈ Y0(V) through x. Separatedness then gives z′ = t0∘l, so the endpoints lie in the finite set t0(s0−1(u)).

Points of characteristic q. Otherwise the previous section extends the marked isogeny over V. At geometric points over the two closed points it gives a polarized isogeny of multiplier c, from the object of u to the object of the endpoint. Choose one geometric witness for each candidate endpoint. Embed their residue extensions of κ(u) compatibly in a single algebraically closed field k of characteristic q. The pullbacks then have the same marked source. Two such isogenies with the same kernel factor through each other, and descent along the isogeny identifies their polarizations. Uniqueness in the fine moduli problem then identifies their targets. It therefore suffices to show that a fixed marked abelian variety A over k has finitely many possible kernels.

Finitely many kernels. Write tr = ω + ω̄ and nm = ωω̄. The defining quadratic splits modulo q. Choose an integer r whose image in k is the selected root ρ, and put u1 = ω − r and u2 = ω − (tr − r), so that u1u2 ∈ q𝒪F. A kernel lies in A[qe], where c = qe. Take lifts εi of the local idempotents of 𝒪F/(qe+1). Let Gi be the common kernel in A[qe+1] of u1e and 1 − εi. Its tangent space at the identity has dimension at most one. On the Lie algebra, a vector killed by a power of u1 lies in the eigenspace ω = r, because tr − 2r is a unit modulo q. The Lie condition splits that eigenspace into lines indexed by the embeddings, since the multiplicity is one. The idempotent keeps at most one of them, because the embeddings stay distinct modulo q.

A subgroup H ∩ Gi is determined by two things: its k-points, a subset of the finite set A[qe+1](k), and its ideal in the local ring of Gi at the origin. That local ring is Artinian with tangent space of dimension at most one. Its maximal ideal is therefore principal by Nakayama’s lemma, and its only ideals are the powers of the maximal ideal, finitely many. Translation by its geometric points identifies each local component of the subgroup with the component at the identity. The reconstruction from these pieces shows that two kernels with the same data agree on every test scheme. Finitely many choices on the finitely many pieces therefore give finitely many kernels.

How do the local pieces determine the subgroup scheme?

To see that the pieces determine the whole kernel, compare two kernels with the same intersections on every piece. For a point x of the first kernel over any test scheme, the ideal of a ∈ 𝒪F with xa in the second kernel contains three elements: u2e by the pieces, u1e by Rosati duality, and qe.

Since q does not divide the discriminant tr² − 4nm = (u2 − u1)² − 4qq0, with q0 = (r² − tr r + nm)/q, the elements q and (u2 − u1)² generate the unit ideal. If e = 0 the ideal contains 1. Otherwise every term in (u2 − u1)2e−1 contains u1e or u2e. Thus both q and u2 − u1 are nilpotent modulo the ideal. Since they generate the unit ideal, the quotient is zero. The point x belongs to the second kernel, and symmetry gives equality on every test scheme. For ω = i, q = 5 and r = 2 one has q0 = 1 and 1 = (1 − 4)·5 + 4².

The reverse direction. Fix instead the endpoint z. Over U the target map is finite and the same argument applies. In characteristic q, extend only the homomorphism between the two pulled-back objects over V. Here the target B must stay fixed with its original marking. The companion gives an arrow B → SeA, since ψ′η′(x) = η(qex). The forward count gives finitely many rescaled targets SeA. Applying Sf recovers the original source A because qe+f ≡ 1 mod N.

Why is the normal model reduced?

The source map of Y0 is étale over X[1/q]. Since X is smooth over the reduced base R, the composite is smooth and Y0 is reduced. Since R is a domain of characteristic zero and Y is flat over R, multiplication by q is injective on the functions of Y. Let g be a nilpotent function on an affine open subset of Y. Where q is invertible this open subset lies in Y0, which is reduced, so g vanishes after inverting q. Thus qkg = 0 for some k, and g = 0.
