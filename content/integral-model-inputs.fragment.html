Why does the target of a tested point extend over the valuation ring?

The construction of the target projection uses two valuative inputs. Both follow from one statement about a discrete valuation ring V with fraction field K, of any residue characteristic. Let o be a marked object over V: an abelian scheme A with its coefficient action, rigidified polarizing bundle and level structure, satisfying the Lie and Tate conditions of the moduli problem. Let ψK : oK → o′ be a polarized isogeny of multiplier c = qe. It is compatible with the coefficients and the level structure, pulls the polarization of o′ back to c times that of oK, and has a companion ψ′ with ψ′ψ = [c] and ψψ′ = [c]. Then o′ is the generic fibre of a marked object over V, and ψK extends.

The quotient. The companion makes ψK finite, flat and surjective, and puts its kernel Z inside AK[c]. Let 𝒢 be the schematic closure of Z in A. It is flat over V, it is a subgroup scheme, and it is killed by c because its generic fibre is schematically dense. So 𝒢 is closed in A[c], which is finite and flat, and 𝒢 is finite and flat. The quotient B = A/𝒢 is an abelian scheme: over an affine base its ring is the ring of 𝒢-invariant functions, and these pieces glue. The flat surjections AK → BK and ψK have the same kernel, so BK is identified with the target of ψK.

The marking. Each coefficient endomorphism extends uniquely, by the extension of homomorphisms over a normal base. The polarizing bundle extends from the generic fibre because the total space is regular and integral. A bundle that is trivial on the generic fibre of an abelian scheme over V is trivial, so the rigidifications, the symmetry and the Rosati isomorphisms extend as well. Relative ampleness is read on the generic fibre. The polarization kernel is finite and flat, so the integer prime to q that kills it generically kills it over V. Each level section extends uniquely, since B(V) = B(K) for the proper scheme B. The Lie condition survives because tr² − 4nm and the integers prime to q are units in V. The Tate condition at a special geometric point follows by reducing torsion of order prime to q. That reduction is bijective and respects the Weil pairings.

The two inputs. Let y ∈ X(V) have generic point s0(x) with x ∈ Y0(K). Pull back the universal object along y and the marked isogeny of the correspondence along x. The extended target is classified by a unique z ∈ X(V), and uniqueness over K gives z|K = t0(x). This is the first input. For the reverse input, the companion only satisfies ψ′∘η′ = η∘[c] on level structures. Choose f with qe+f ≡ 1 mod N and replace the level of the target object by its composite with [qf]. Then ψ′ becomes a polarized isogeny of multiplier c back to the source, and the same statement applies. For N = 5, q = 2 and e = 1 one takes f = 3, since 2⁴ ≡ 1 mod 5. Néron models, semistable reduction and properness of X are not used. The good reduction of the target comes from the explicit quotient.

What does the quotient look like for an elliptic curve?

Let E be an elliptic curve over V and C ⊂ EK a subgroup of order q. Its closure C̄ lies in E[q], so it is finite and flat of order q, and E/C̄ is an elliptic curve over V. If V has residue characteristic q and the special fibre is supersingular, then C̄ reduces to the kernel of Frobenius. The special fibre of the quotient is then the Frobenius twist of that of E. The analogue of the general statement is formalized only for the unitary moduli problem.

Why are there only finitely many target specializations?

Fix a point u of X. Consider all tests: a discrete valuation ring V, a point x of Y0 over its fraction field, and V-points y′, z′ extending s0(x) and t0(x), with y′ ending at u. The closed points of the z′ form a finite set. Finiteness of the reverse set, for a fixed endpoint of z′, is used for the finite fibres of t.

Points where q is invertible. If u lies in U = X[1/q], so does all of y′. The source map of the correspondence is finite, so the valuative criterion lifts y′ to l ∈ Y0(V) through x. Separatedness then gives z′ = t0∘l, so the endpoints lie in the finite set t0(s0−1(u)).

Points of characteristic q. Otherwise the previous section extends the marked isogeny over V. At geometric points over the two closed points it gives a polarized isogeny of multiplier c, from the object of u to the object of the endpoint. Embed all residue fields in one algebraically closed field k of characteristic q. Two such isogenies with the same kernel factor through each other, and descent along the isogeny identifies their polarizations. Uniqueness in the fine moduli problem then identifies their targets. It therefore suffices to show that a fixed marked abelian variety A over k has finitely many possible kernels.

Finitely many kernels. Let r be the integer congruent to the selected root ρ, and put u1 = ω − r and u2 = ω − (tr − r), so that u1u2 ∈ q𝒪F. A kernel lies in A[qn], where c = qn. Split the part killed by u1n with the idempotents of 𝒪F/qn+1 attached to the primes above q. Each piece Gi has tangent space of dimension at most one. On the Lie algebra, a vector killed by a power of u1 lies in the eigenspace ω = r, because tr − 2r is a unit modulo q. The Lie condition splits that eigenspace into lines indexed by the embeddings, since the multiplicity is one. The idempotent keeps at most one of them, because the embeddings stay distinct modulo q.

A subgroup H ∩ Gi is determined by two things: its k-points, a subset of the finite set A[qn+1](k), and its ideal in the local ring of Gi at the origin. That local ring is Artinian with tangent space of dimension at most one. Its maximal ideal is therefore principal, and its only ideals are the powers of the maximal ideal, finitely many. The pieces determine the kernel. If x lies in the first kernel, the ideal of a ∈ 𝒪F with xa in the second kernel contains three elements: u2n by the pieces, u1n by Rosati duality, and qn. Since q does not divide the discriminant tr² − 4nm = (u2 − u1)² − 4qq0, with q0 = (r² − tr r + nm)/q, the elements q and (u2 − u1)² generate the unit ideal. High powers of both lie in the ideal of x, so that ideal is everything. For ω = i, q = 5 and r = 2 one has q0 = 1 and 1 = (1 − 4)·5 + 4².

The reverse direction. Fix instead the endpoint z. Over U the target map is finite and the same argument applies. In characteristic q, extend only the homomorphism between the two pulled-back objects over V. Its companion is a polarized isogeny of multiplier c from the fixed object to the source object, with its level rescaled by qf as in the previous section. The rescaled objects are finitely many by the forward argument. Rescaling is injective, because qe+f ≡ 1 mod N.

Why is the normal model reduced?

The local rings of Y are normal, but the formal proof uses only the following short argument. The generic correspondence Y0 is smooth over R, hence reduced. Since R is a domain of characteristic zero and Y is flat over R, multiplication by q is injective on the functions of Y. Let g be a nilpotent function on an affine open subset of Y. Where q is invertible this open subset lies in Y0, which is reduced, so g vanishes after inverting q. Thus qkg = 0 for some k, and g = 0.
