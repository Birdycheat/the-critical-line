How can one coefficient measure a simultaneous character space?

Let C be a commutative ring, finite free over ℤ, and k an algebraically closed field of prime characteristic q. The regular representation of C is its action on S = k ⊗ℤ C by multiplication on the second factor. Let ρ be a unital action of C on a finite-dimensional k-space V. For a ring character χ ∶ C → k, define Vχ to consist of the vectors v for which, for every c ∈ C, some power of ρ(c) − χ(c) kills v. Define Sχ in the same way for the regular action. The powers in this definition retain vectors in nilpotent chains.

Multiplicity lemma. Let h ≥ 0 be an integer. Suppose that, for every c ∈ C, the characteristic polynomial of ρ(c) equals the hth power of its characteristic polynomial on S. Then, for every χ,

dimk Vχ = h · dimk Sχ.

Decomposing the two representations. On each space split one coefficient operator into its generalized eigenspaces. These are the images of the polynomial projectors obtained from the relatively prime powers of the linear factors of its minimal polynomial. Every other coefficient commutes with these projectors and preserves the summands. Whenever another coefficient has two eigenvalues on a summand, split it again. Each split increases the number of nonzero summands, so the procedure stops in finite dimension. On a resulting summand, each coefficient acts by one scalar plus a nilpotent. These scalars form a character. Indeed, commuting nilpotents generate a nilpotent ideal in the algebra they generate when there are finitely many of them. Applied to two coefficients, this proves that the unique eigenvalues add and multiply under sums and products, and 1 acts as the identity. Distinct summands have distinct characters because some split separated them. The Stacks Project, Lemma 10.15.4, Chinese remainders and the associated projectors.

Isolating χ. List the characters on the nonzero summands of both representations and adjoin χ itself. Each character factors through C/qC, a finite ring, so its image in k is a finite field and its kernel is maximal. In C/ker χ choose a nonzero generator of the multiplicative group. By Chinese remainders choose c with that residue at ker χ and residue zero at every distinct kernel on the list. Any character with another kernel now takes value zero at c, whereas χ(c) ≠ 0. Characters with the same kernel give embeddings of the same residue field. Agreement on its multiplicative generator forces these embeddings to agree everywhere. Thus χ alone on the list can take the value χ(c). Milne, Fields and Galois Theory, Proposition 4.19 and the Chinese remainder theorem.

On the χ-summand, ρ(c) − χ(c) is nilpotent. On a summand with character ψ ≠ χ, it is a nonzero scalar plus a nilpotent and has an inverse given by a finite geometric series. The full generalized eigenspace of this one operator at χ(c) is therefore Vχ. Its contribution to the characteristic polynomial is (X − χ(c))dim Vχ, as is seen in a basis adapted to the kernels of successive powers of the nilpotent. All other summands contribute multiplicity zero there. The same argument reads dim Sχ from the regular polynomial, including the case of an absent character. The polynomial power in the hypothesis multiplies this root multiplicity by h.

Why do Frobenius-conjugate character spaces have the same dimension?

Return to the marked abelian variety and its primitive cohomology P. Thus C = 𝒪F, the field k is algebraically closed of characteristic q, the algebra degree is m = 1 and the level N ≥ 3 is prime to q. The labelled character χ has kernel 𝔴, ramification index r = v𝔴(q) and residue degree f ≥ 1. Set χj(c) = χ(c)qj for j ≥ 0.

Equal-multiplicity theorem. The generalized character spaces Pχj all have the same dimension. This equality supplies the equal dimensions used in the construction of a Frobenius preimage.

First compute on the regular representation. On S = k ⊗ℤ C the scalar Frobenius T takes λ ⊗ c to λq ⊗ c. This is a semilinear ring automorphism because every element of k has a unique qth root. It fixes 1 ⊗ c. Consequently, for every integer b ≥ 1, it carries the equation (1 ⊗ c − χ(c))bx = 0 to the corresponding equation for χ₁ and Tx. Its inverse carries that equation back. Thus T takes any basis of Sχ to a basis of Sχ₁, and iteration gives their common dimension throughout the orbit.

Compare the characteristic polynomials. Let Λ be the integral lattice of the moduli datum and n its rank parameter. The action of C makes Λ ⊗ ℚ a vector space over F. The prescribed ℤ-rank is mn[F ∶ ℚ], so an F-basis identifies the rational action of each c with mn copies of multiplication by c on F. Taking characteristic polynomials gives the same equality over ℤ, since both polynomials are integral. Write pc(X) for the integral characteristic polynomial of multiplication by c on C.

Choose an auxiliary prime ℓ ≠ q. For every b ≥ 1 the marking identifies Λ/ℓbΛ with A[ℓb](k) compatibly with the coefficient action. Hence the characteristic polynomial on the Tate module agrees with that on Λ modulo every ℓb. This determines it over ℤℓ. The characteristic polynomial theorem for abelian varieties identifies it with the integral characteristic polynomial of the endomorphism [c]. Milne, Abelian Varieties, Proposition 10.20.

In characteristic q the crystalline characteristic polynomial theorem gives that same integral polynomial on the rational Dieudonné module of A[q∞], after extending scalars. Its integral Dieudonné module is free over W(k). Hence its polynomial already agrees there, and reducing a basis modulo q reduces the polynomial coefficient by coefficient. The de Rham comparison identifies the reduced action with the action on P. Passing to its transpose on P∨ preserves the determinant defining the characteristic polynomial. The input before reduction is the equality of the crystalline characteristic polynomial with the polynomial whose value at an integer b is deg([b] − [c]), with value zero for a non-isogeny. Kass, Notes on Abelian Schemes, Corollary 2. For the identification after reduction, see Oda, The first de Rham cohomology group and Dieudonné modules, Corollary 5.11.

charpol(c ∣ P) = (pc mod q)mn,   charpol(c ∣ S) = pc mod q.

The second identity follows by extending an integral basis of C to k. Apply the multiplicity lemma to each character. It gives dim Pχj = mn · dim Sχj. The right side is constant by scalar Frobenius. The geometric Frobenius on P can kill Hodge classes. Its injectivity between the particular spaces used for the preimage requires the separate Hodge-kernel argument.

Two characters with a nilpotent direction. Consider the coefficient algebra 𝔽₂[t]/((t² + t + 1)²). Over k containing 𝔽₄, write ω² + ω + 1 = 0. Chinese remainders split the algebra into two planes with respective bases (1, t − ω) and (1, t − ω²) in their local factors. On the first plane multiplication by t − ω sends 1 to t − ω and then to zero. Multiplication by t has an ordinary eigenspace of dimension one at ω and a generalized eigenspace of dimension two. Squaring the scalars sends this chain to the chain in the second plane and fixes the polynomial variable t. In h copies of the regular representation each generalized dimension is therefore 2h.
