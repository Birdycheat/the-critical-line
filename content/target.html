A single vertical line is supposed to contain every nontrivial zero of a function built from all the positive integers. Why should estimates for primes have anything to say about a point in the complex plane?

From a series to a function

Write s = σ + it, where σ and t are real and i² = −1. In the half-plane σ > 1 the sum ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 exp(−s log n) converges absolutely, since the absolute values sum to ∑ n−σ. Multiplying the geometric series in p−s over primes lists each integer exactly once by unique factorization. This gives the Euler product. For example, multiply just the factors for 2 and 3. The term from the second power of 2 and the first power of 3 is 12−s. No other choice gives 12. Absolute convergence allows the finite prime sets to increase until every integer appears. [1] DLMF §25.2

ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 n−s = ∏p prime (1 − p−s)−1   (Re s > 1).

Could the infinite product vanish although every factor is nonzero? This can happen for products in general. Here the logarithms are absolutely summable. For σ > 1 their absolute sum is at most ∑p ∑j≥1 p−jσ/j ≤ (1 − 2−σ)−1 ∑n≥2 n−σ. Exponentiating the convergent sum of logarithms gives a nonzero product.

At s = ¾ the original series is a divergent sum of positive terms. The expression ζ(¾) nevertheless has a precise meaning. There is a unique meromorphic function on the plane agreeing with the series throughout Re s > 1. Meromorphic means holomorphic except at isolated poles. Here the sole pole is at 1, and the continued function is regular at ¾. It is this continued function that the theorem studies. DLMF §25.2, definition and continuation

We can even determine the sign of ζ(¾). Let η(s) = 1 − 2−s + 3−s − 4−s + ⋯. For Re s > 1, changing the sign of the even terms subtracts twice their sum from ζ(s), giving η(s) = (1 − 21−s)ζ(s). The identity continues to Re s > 0. At s = ¾ every consecutive pair in η is positive, while 1 − 2¼ is negative. Hence ζ(¾) < 0. The same sign argument works at every real point between 0 and 1. A hypothetical zero with real part ¾ would therefore have a nonzero imaginary part. NIST DLMF, equation 25.2.3.

Why the alternating series continues across the line Re s = 1

Pair terms numbered 2j − 1 and 2j. Their difference is s times the integral of x−s−1 from 2j − 1 to 2j. On a compact subset where Re s ≥ δ > 0 and |s| ≤ C, the absolute value of the pair is at most C(2j − 1)−1−δ. These bounds have a finite sum. The paired series therefore converges locally uniformly to a holomorphic function there, and an unpaired last term tends to zero. The product (1 − 21−s)ζ(s) also extends holomorphically through 1, since the factor has a simple zero at the simple pole of ζ. Both functions agree on Re s > 1. Uniqueness of analytic continuation gives the identity throughout Re s > 0.

Which zeros?

The zeros at −2, −4, −6, … are called trivial. The remaining zeros lie in the strip 0 < σ < 1. Reflection pairs zeros across the line σ = ½. A pair at σ = 0.4 and σ = 0.6 would respect this symmetry, so symmetry alone leaves the main question open. [2] DLMF §25.10(i)

If ζ(s) = 0, s ≠ 1 and s ∉ {−2, −4, −6, …}, then Re s = ½.

The exception at 1 removes the pole of ζ from the statement about its zeros. At zero the value ζ(0) = −½ eliminates another possible endpoint. [3] Two public zeta theorems

Why the book follows characters

A Dirichlet character modulo a positive integer N is a multiplicative character on its invertible residue classes, extended by zero on nonunits. Its values on units are roots of unity. Inserting χ(n) in the series for ζ weights each residue class by a phase. The resulting function L(s,χ) agrees with ζ when N = 1. The principal character at a larger modulus drops all integers with a common factor with that modulus. For example at modulus 2 its series is (1 − 2−s)ζ(s) for σ > 1. At s = 2, deleting the even terms leaves ¾ζ(2). Changing their signs as in the alternating series gives ½ζ(2).

The proof first obtains right-half-plane nonvanishing for every χ and every nonzero modulus. More precisely, σ > ½ and (χ ≠ 1 or s ≠ 1) imply L(s,χ) ≠ 0. Fixing the modulus-one character gives right-half-plane nonvanishing for ζ. The functional equation deals with the other side. The hard part is producing the prime-sum inequalities that lead to this nonvanishing. Character averages enter those inequalities before the specialization to ζ. [4] Exact scopes · [5] Proof bodies

Check the role of modulus one

What can be concluded at a hypothetical point ρ with Re ρ = ¾ and ζ(ρ) = 0? Take N = 1 and its unique character. Then L(ρ,χ) = ζ(ρ), and ρ ≠ 1 follows from its real part. The character nonvanishing theorem contradicts this zero. At Re ρ = ¼ the same substitution does not meet the right-half-plane hypothesis. The reflection chapter constructs a point with real part ¾ to which it can be applied.
