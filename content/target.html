A single vertical line is supposed to contain every nontrivial zero of a function built from all the positive integers. Why should estimates for primes have anything to say about a point in the complex plane?

From a series to a function

Write s = σ + it, where σ and t are real and i² = −1. In the half-plane σ > 1 the sum ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 exp(−s log n) converges absolutely, since the absolute values sum to ∑ n−σ. Multiplying the geometric series in p−s over primes lists each integer exactly once by unique factorization. This gives the Euler product. Its logarithm converges there, so its exponential, ζ, is nonzero. The same function extends meromorphically to the complex plane with a simple pole at 1. This continuation is the function whose zeros enter RH. [1] DLMF §25.2

ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 n−s = ∏p prime (1 − p−s)−1   (Re s > 1).

Which zeros?

The zeros at −2, −4, −6, … are called trivial. The remaining zeros lie in the strip 0 < σ < 1. Reflection pairs zeros across the line σ = ½. A pair at σ = 0.4 and σ = 0.6 would respect this symmetry, so symmetry alone leaves the main question open. [2] DLMF §25.10(i)

If ζ(s) = 0, s ≠ 1 and s ∉ {−2, −4, −6, …}, then Re s = ½.

This is Mathlib’s proposition RiemannHypothesis. A Lean function from complex numbers to complex numbers has a value at every input. Ordinary assertions about a meromorphic function at its pole concern a punctured neighborhood. The explicit exception at 1 lets us read the theorem in that ordinary sense. At zero the value ζ(0) = −½ eliminates another possible endpoint. [3] Two public zeta theorems

Why the book follows characters

A Dirichlet character modulo a positive integer N is a multiplicative character on its invertible residue classes, extended by zero on nonunits. Its values on units are roots of unity. Inserting χ(n) in the series for ζ weights each residue class by a phase. The resulting function L(s,χ) agrees with ζ when N = 1. The principal character at a larger modulus drops all integers with a common factor with that modulus. For example at modulus 2 its series is (1 − 2−s)ζ(s) for σ > 1.

The proof first obtains right-half-plane nonvanishing for every χ and every nonzero modulus. Formally, σ > ½ and (χ ≠ 1 or s ≠ 1) imply L(s,χ) ≠ 0. Fixing the modulus-one character gives right-half-plane nonvanishing for ζ. The functional equation deals with the other side. The hard part is producing the prime-sum inequalities that lead to this nonvanishing. Character averages enter those inequalities before the specialization to ζ. [4] Exact scopes · [5] Proof bodies
