Why does a product of local Gram densities give a global mean?

At one finite place a density measures how often a tuple has a prescribed Gram matrix. A rational tuple must satisfy the same equations at every place. To bring these two descriptions together the proof averages over all adelic isometries of a single rational Hermitian space. Let E/F be the CM extension, J the nondegenerate Hermitian form on EM, and T a rational nonsingular Hermitian matrix of size n. The theorem assumes n < M, with either n+2 ≤ M or J anisotropic over E. Anisotropic means that a rational vector of norm zero must itself be zero. The test Φ has a Schwartz factor at infinity and locally constant, compactly supported factors at finite places. Almost all finite factors are the characteristic function of the standard lattice. At every place the two Hermitian matrices come from these same rational forms, and each local additive character comes from one global character. Why can grouping the terms change their weights?

A finite group makes the Haar weights visible. Take H to be the six permutations of three letters and put Γ = K = {id,(12)}. A function constant on ΓhK has just two values. The class of the identity contains two permutations. The class of (13) contains the remaining four. Indeed the size of ΓhK is |Γ||K|/|Γ ∩ hKh−1|, and the two intersection sizes are 2 and 1. If the function takes values 2 and 5, the normalized Haar average is (2·2 + 4·5)/6 = 4. Giving both classes weight ½ would give 3½. The same issue arises when a quotient integral is grouped into classes, whose weights come from their volumes. The definition above keeps that weighting by integrating on a fundamental domain.

How is the scalar fixed in an anisotropic plane?

One vector in an anisotropic binary space needs a different normalization argument. The proof combines local mass and compactness estimates with a representation principle and invariance under similitudes. A rational similitude transports a common scalar κ across the represented nonzero lengths. A counting theorem compares two limits for a test of volume V with 0 < V < ∞ and gives V = κV. Thus κ = 1. A second binary result supplies a bound on the error in the local lattice masses. In higher rank the resulting bound outside finitely many places is |massv−1| ≤ C/qv², where qv is the residue size. The sum of these errors converges, which controls the Euler product of the standard-lattice masses.
