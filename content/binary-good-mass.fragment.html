How much mass does one length carry at a good prime?

A Hermitian vector in a plane has two coordinates over E. Each has two coordinates over F. At a finite place v, fixing its length therefore imposes one quadratic equation on four coordinates over Fv. Fix the basis and lattice used in the product formula. Write 𝒪 for the local integers, π for a uniformizer, and q for the number of elements of the residue field. Normalize scalar Haar measure by vol(𝒪) = 1 and use its fourth power on the source. Let gv(s) be the mass on the length-s fibre of the standard lattice.

Good-place mass estimate. Remove the finitely many places where 2 or the determinant of the trace form fails to be a unit, or an entry of its matrix fails to be integral. At each remaining place, for s ≠ 0 one has

gv(s) = 0 for s ∉ 𝒪,   |gv(s) − 1| ≤ 3/q for s ∈ 𝒪,   |gv(s) − 1| ≤ 1/q² for s ∈ 𝒪×.

In the chosen coordinates the length is Q(x) = ½xtBx, where B is the symmetric matrix of the trace of the Hermitian pairing. The factor ½ occurs because the field trace doubles a scalar from the base field. The local Gram-fibre integration formula identifies gv on nonzero lengths with the continuous density of the pushforward of volume on 𝒪⁴ by Q. In particular, if the ratio of the inverse-image volume to target volume is constant on all sufficiently small target balls about s, it equals the fibre mass there. Integrality of B and ½ confines the support to 𝒪. The zero-length vectors also have source volume zero. Choose a vector on which Q is nonzero and take it as the first vector of a basis over the local field. For each choice of the other three coordinates the equation Q = 0 has at most two roots in the first. Singletons have Haar measure zero, so Fubini gives zero volume. An invertible linear change of basis preserves null sets. Thus integrating the regular density gives the entire volume vol(𝒪⁴) = 1.

How is a measure on a single length defined?

Even when a vector of length s exists, the set of all vectors of that length has zero four-dimensional volume. We can still assign it a three-dimensional measure. At a good finite place, choose a primitive residue class C = a + π𝒪⁴. Since B is invertible modulo π, some coordinate of B a is a unit. Keep the other three coordinates y as parameters and use the length in place of that coordinate. For any s in I = Q(a) + π𝒪 and any compatible y, there is exactly one remaining coordinate in its residue class. Indeed, given a solution modulo πk, changing its next digit changes the next length digit by multiplication by that unit. Every required digit has a unique lift, and completeness gives the coordinate. Call this vector x(s,y).

For each fixed y this correspondence preserves one-dimensional Haar measure. It gives a bijection of the residue balls at every precision, and corresponding balls have the same volume. Integration in the length and the three free coordinates now gives, for a locally constant test function φ on C,

∫C φ(x) dx = ∫I (∫y ≡ afree mod π φ(x(s,y)) dy) ds.

The inner integral defines the contribution of this class to the length-s fibre. For φ = 1 it equals q−3 throughout I. For example, over ℚ₃ take the sum of four squares and the class (1,0,0,0) modulo 3. Each of the last three coordinates ranges over 3ℤ₃. The contribution is 1/27 at every length congruent to 1. Integrated over that target ball it gives (1/27)(1/3) = 1/81, precisely the volume of the source class.

Every nonzero integral vector has a unique expression πjz with j ≥ 0 and z primitive. On this shell four-dimensional volume scales by q−4j and length by π2j, so the fibre measure scales by q−2j. At a fixed nonzero integral s only 2j ≤ ord(s) can occur. The shell sum for φ = 1 thus defines a locally constant density near s. It agrees pointwise with the continuous regular Gram-fibre mass used in the product formula. Both integrate to the same volume on every sufficiently small target ball, and every such ball has positive measure.

Why is the error smaller at a unit length?

First count primitive vectors, meaning that some coordinate is a unit. For a residue value a, let N(a) be the number of solutions of the reduced equation Q(x̄) = a in the four-dimensional residue space. Delete the zero vector if a = 0, and write N×(a) for the resulting count. Every remaining solution has nonzero gradient Bx̄ because B is invertible modulo π. Suppose a solution modulo πk is lifted to x + πky. The next digit of the length is determined by the nonzero linear functional y ↦ (Bx̄)·y. Exactly q³ choices give any prescribed next digit. A target ball modulo πk has volume q−k. Its primitive preimage consists of N×(a) q3(k−1) balls of volume q−4k. The ratio is independent of k and equals N×(a)/q³.

Residue count. For a nonsingular quadratic form in four variables over a field of odd cardinality q, one has |N(a) − q³| ≤ q if a ≠ 0, and |N(0) − q³| ≤ q². For a unit length, every solution is primitive. Dividing the first bound by q³ proves the unit estimate. For any integral length the primitive density P(s) therefore satisfies |P(s) − 1| ≤ 1/q + 1/q³, where the last summand allows for deleting the origin.

Why does a nonzero residue save one power of q?

Choose a nontrivial additive character ψ of the residue field and write G(u) = ∑x ψ(uQ(x)). Character orthogonality gives qN(a) = q⁴ + ∑u≠0 ψ(−ua)G(u). Indeed summing ψ(ut) over u gives q for t = 0 and zero otherwise, since translation by an element with character different from one preserves that sum.

Over a field of odd characteristic, completing squares diagonalizes the nonsingular form with four nonzero coefficients. If χ is the quadratic character extended by χ(0) = 0, the equation y² = z has 1 + χ(z) solutions. In each of the four one-variable character sums, changing u from 1 to a nonzero value consequently multiplies the sum by χ(u). The four factors give χ(u)⁴ = 1, so G(u) = G(1). Its modulus is q². To check this, square its modulus and replace the first argument in the double sum by x + y. The sum over x of ψ(xtBy) vanishes unless y = 0, when it contributes q⁴. Finally ∑u≠0 ψ(−ua) equals −1 for a ≠ 0 and q − 1 for a = 0. Dividing by q gives the two count estimates.

What do vectors divisible by the prime contribute?

Write k = ordv(s) for a nonzero integral length. If k is 0 or 1, every vector of length s is primitive, since Q(πy) = π²Q(y). If k ≥ 2, the nonprimitive part lies in π𝒪⁴. Passing from y to πy multiplies source volume by q−4. The matching target ball is scaled by π², which multiplies target volume by q−2. Taking the ratio gives the recurrence

gv(s) = P(s) + q−2gv(s/π²)   (k ≥ 2).

Each application lowers the valuation by two. To verify the uniform error, put w = 1/q ≤ ½ and assume the lower-valuation density differs from one by at most 3w. The primitive bound and the recurrence give an absolute error at most w + w³ + w²(1 + 3w) = w + w² + 4w³ ≤ 3w. The two base valuations already obey this bound. This proves it for every nonzero integral length.

For example, take the positive Hermitian form |x₁|² + |x₂|² over ℚ(i) at 3. The scalar form in four coordinates is a sum of four squares. Modulo 3, each nonzero coordinate contributes 1. To represent 1 there must be one or four nonzero coordinates, giving 4·2 + 2⁴ = 24 solutions. To represent 0 with a primitive vector requires three nonzero coordinates, giving 4·2³ = 32. Thus g₃(1) = 24/27 = 8/9 and g₃(3) = 32/27. At length 9 the vectors divisible by 3 first contribute, giving g₃(9) = 32/27 + (1/9)(8/9) = 104/81.

The reason for keeping both error bounds appears in the average over lengths. At units the error is already at most q−2. The larger bound 3/q occurs only on the nonunits, which have local measure 1/q. Their contribution to the mean absolute error on 𝒪 is at most 3/q². In the global argument, divisibility of a rational length by several primes is counted by the norm of their product ideal.
