How much volume is lost when one Hermitian direction is removed?

The residual rectangle argument uses the volume of the smaller unitary group. To compare it with the original group, remove one direction at a time. Let E/F be the chosen CM extension, f = [F:ℚ] and νE = |disc(E)|1/(2f)/2. Assume the full signature (p,q) is the same at every real place. Write X for the original gauge-normalized volume in rank M ≥ 3, and Y for the corresponding volume in rank M−1. Fix a sign σ equal to +1 or −1 that occurs in this signature. The classification of the self-dual models lets us work in a signed diagonal model of this signature and carry its integral lattice to a representative in the genus. Choose a coordinate vector of the required sign. Its norm is a unit, so projection along it splits the lattice integrally and leaves a self-dual orthogonal complement. Thus Y is the volume for an actual complementary model. This comparison requires the unramified finite-place hypotheses of the flag theorem.

Consider the Gaussian sum over lattice vectors of norm σ, integrated over the original quotient. In both evaluations multiply that same integral by the product of its real gauge factors and divide by the finite measure of its compact level K. At each real place write Q(z) = ∑ εa|zₐ|² and let dᵥ be the density at σ obtained by pushing exp(−∑|zₐ|²) dz forward by Q. The density is positive. A small patch around the chosen unit vector has positive Gaussian mass and a nonzero derivative in its radial direction. Put d = ∏ dᵥ. In applying the one-vector mean, first take the indicator of the self-dual lattice at finite places and smooth compactly supported functions at the real places. Then increase the real cutoffs to the Gaussian. The terms in the lattice sum are nonnegative, so monotone convergence passes through the sum and the quotient integral. At the nonzero target σ, the densities of the cutoffs converge to dᵥ on each regular quadric. The resulting integral equals X m d, where the product of the local unit densities gives the arithmetic scalar m = (2νE)f/2νE−Mf/LM. The Euler product LM converges to a positive finite value and has one factor at each base prime.

Now mark the unit vector before integrating. Its rational stabilizer acts on the orthogonal complement. The finite representatives containing the chosen vector form one stabilizer–level double coset. The induced level is the stabilizer of the complementary lattice. In unfolding the markings the finite measure ratio is μf(K)/μ′f(K′), where K′ is that complementary level. The numerator cancels the original gauge denominator. What remains is the normalized complementary volume Y. At a real place, the remaining integration is over the quadric Q = σ. Its Haar quotient measure is 2M times the density measure of Q. To see the constant, calculate at the chosen coordinate vector. A tangent vector in the complement of its stabilizer has one imaginary axial coordinate and M−1 free complex coordinates. In the infinitesimal isometry matrix each free coordinate occurs twice, paired with a signed conjugate. The squared Hilbert–Schmidt norm thus has coefficient two on its 2(M−1) real coordinates, giving a volume factor 2M−1 relative to Euclidean tangent area on the quadric. For the density of Q, use Q as the normal coordinate. At the unit vector its derivative in the outward radial coordinate has absolute value two. Changing variables thus gives half the tangent area per unit of Q. Both measures are invariant under unitary changes of frame. The group preserves Q and has absolute real determinant one. Their ratio at one point holds on the whole orbit. For a definite ternary form this calibration can be checked on the ordinary sphere. The ball of squared radius t in ℂ³ has volume π³t³/6, by elementary polar integration in the three complex coordinates. At t = 1 its Gaussian density is therefore π³/(2e), and its quotient integral is 8 times that value. In the indefinite case the Gaussian test itself stays inside both integrals. Multiplying the real constants and comparing evaluations gives

X m d = Y 2Mfd,   X = Y rM,   rM = (2νE)f(M−1/2)LM.

Indeed m rM = 2Mf, and m and d are positive and finite. Choosing an absent sign would make the quadric empty and defeat this cancellation.

Which local factors survive along the rank interval?

Let 𝔭 be a prime of the totally real base field with residue field of size Q. The extension is unramified at finite places. If 𝔭 is inert there is one prime above it of norm Q², fixed by conjugation. If 𝔭 splits there are two prime ideals of norm Q and conjugation exchanges them. In the product over prime ideals of the CM field each member of the split pair has weight (1−Q−M)−1/2. Multiplying the two members yields the factors indexed by base primes:

LM = ∏𝔭 split (1−Q−M)−1 ∏𝔭 inert (1−(−1)MQ−M)−1.

For a numerical check of these local formulas set Q = 2. In rank three the split and inert possibilities give 8/7 and 8/9. In rank four both give 16/15. At every step used here M ≥ 3. There are at most [F:ℚ] base primes above each rational prime p, and Q ≥ p. Thus the sum of the absolute logarithms of the factors is bounded by a constant times ∑n≥2 n−M. This proves the strict positivity and finiteness used in cancelling m.

Write (p₀,q₀) for the target signature and suppose its rank is at least two. While a positive direction is still to be removed, take σ = +1; once the positive target is reached, take σ = −1 for each remaining negative direction. Each step starts in rank at least three. The next factor depends only on the field and current rank, so their product is ∏p₀+q₀<M≤p+qrM. At the final signature the rational isometry given by classification and the finite-adelic change of lattice representatives transport the stabilizer and its level to any of the specified models; the gauge-normalized volume is the same. For an isotropic flag of total short rank J we remove J directions of each sign. For instance (3,1) to (2,0) uses r₄r₃. This gives the denominator RJ of the probability bound.

Why is there just one finite orbit for the unit vector?

Fix a rational vector x of Hermitian norm σ = +1 or −1, a reference finite representative xf whose lattice contains it, and the full finite stabilizer K of the reference lattice. Let H be the group of isometries fixing x. If a second finite representative g gives a lattice containing x, the assertion needed for unfolding is

g ∈ H(𝔸fin) xf K.

Work first over the local integer ring at one finite place. On a self-dual lattice L containing x, the coefficient of the projection of y onto its line is ⟨x,y⟩/σ. It is integral, since σ is a unit. Subtracting that multiple of x gives the integral decomposition L = 𝒪x ⊕ B, where B is the orthogonal complement in L. A vector in the rational complement that pairs integrally with B pairs integrally with the whole lattice, hence belongs to B by self-duality. Thus B is itself self-dual. The local classification of self-dual lattices identifies the complements for g and xf by an integral isometry between them. Extend it by the identity on x. Outside finitely many places the two original lattices already agree, so these maps can be chosen to be the identity there. Their product h is an adelic isometry fixing x and taking one local lattice family to the other. Then (hxf)−1g belongs to K, as claimed. Conversely, multiplying on the right by K fixes the lattice, and multiplying on the left by h preserves the condition that it contains x.

This argument also identifies the level. An isometry fixing x preserves L exactly when it preserves B. For an integral modulus c, the image of h−1 on L is just its image on B. As this lies in the perpendicular space, containment in cL is equivalent to containment in cB. This identifies the subgroup induced on the complement by a congruence condition. The double coset above uses the full lattice stabilizer. For example, −1 preserves the reference lattice. At principal level 3 it takes a primitive unit vector to a different residue class, so one may have more than one finite orbit at that finer level. The same calculation applies over the two factors at a split place and over an unramified extension in residue characteristic two.

One residue calculation illustrates the dyadic case. Over 𝔽₄ with conjugation z ↦ z², take the standard Hermitian form in three coordinates and x = (1,1,1). Its norm is 1. Its complement has basis b₁ = (1,1,0), b₂ = (1,0,1). Each basis vector has norm zero and their pairing is 1. For ω²+ω+1 = 0, the norm of b₁+ωb₂ is ω+ω² = 1. The projection along x and this choice of a nonsingular direction both make sense in characteristic two.
