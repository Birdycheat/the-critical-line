What fixes the measure on one Gram fibre?

A global product uses the local integral at a specified target T. An identity averaged over T leaves open what happens at an individual target of measure zero. The extra condition that fixes the answer is continuity of the fibre integrals on the nonsingular targets.

Regular Gram-fibre theorem. Work at a completion R of the base field F of the CM extension E/F. Let V be the space of n-tuples of vectors for the scalar extension to R ⊗F E of a nonsingular Hermitian form J on EM. The Gram map G sends a tuple to its matrix of pairings in the space H of Hermitian n × n matrices. Let B be the open set where the determinant is invertible and fix additive Haar measures μ on V and ν on H. There is a unique family of measures mT carried by G−1{T} for T ∈ B, set equal to zero outside B, with the following two properties.

∫G−1(B) φ dμ = ∫B (∫ φ dmT) dν(T)   for every nonnegative measurable φ.

For every real continuous φ compactly supported inside G−1(B), the inner integral is finite and depends continuously on T ∈ B. An empty fibre carries the zero measure. The integration formula concerns the part of the source lying over B.

How one open set of targets acquires its measures

Fix a nonsingular target T₀. The local congruence-section theorem supplies a subgroup L of changes of basis for the tuple such that h ↦ h·T₀ is a homeomorphism onto an open neighborhood of T₀. Here the target action is congruence, and G(h·z) = h·G(z). A tuple above this neighborhood therefore has unique coordinates (h, z) with z in the reference fibre G−1{T₀}. Recover h from the target and act by h−1 on the tuple to recover z.

Write χV(h) and χH(h) for the factors by which pushing forward μ and ν under h multiplies them. Pull μ back to the product coordinates and multiply by the density χV(h). Translating the group coordinate now preserves the result. Applying the same correction χH(h) to the target measure gives a left Haar measure λ on L. The corrected source measure is λ times a measure m₀ on the reference fibre. Indeed, for each relatively compact measurable fibre set A, the masses of rectangles U × A give a locally finite left-invariant measure in U. Haar uniqueness makes it a constant times λ. A fixed compact neighborhood K of the identity gives that constant as the mass of K × A divided by λ(K). This defines m₀, and exhaustion gives the product identity. Undoing the corrections gives mh·T₀ = [χH(h)/χV(h)] h∗m₀. For a compactly supported continuous test, continuity follows by integration on the fixed reference fibre. Near any fixed h, the translated test supports meet the fibre in a common compact set. Sutherland, Theorem 13.3 on Haar measure.

Why do two choices agree at every regular target?

Take two such neighborhoods and a nonnegative continuous test supported compactly over their overlap. Write u(T) and v(T) for its two fibre integrals. On every compact target set K inside the overlap, both integrate to the same quantity, the source integral of the test over G−1(K). If u(T) exceeded v(T) at one point, continuity would give a compact neighborhood on which their difference stays bounded below by a positive number. That neighborhood has positive Haar measure. Its two integrals would differ. Thus u = v throughout the overlap. These tests determine a locally finite measure there: compact subsets can be bounded by continuous cutoffs in arbitrarily small open neighborhoods. Fibre support then gives equality of the two measures at each target.

Choose the measure from any chart containing T. To check the integration formula for the resulting family, take a countable subcover B₁, B₂, …, which exists by second countability. Give each target to the first chart containing it. The measurable pieces Bj ∖ ⋃i<j Bi are disjoint, so their integration formulas add without counting overlaps twice. For continuity near a chosen target, multiply the test by a continuous cutoff in the target that equals one near that point and is supported inside one chart. This leaves the nearby fibre integrals unchanged and gives continuity by the local construction.

One exceptional value. For the complex line with G(z) = |z|², use area on ℂ and length ds on ℝ. In polar coordinates area is ½ ds dθ, so each circle s > 0 has fibre mass π. Doubling just the measure at s = 1 would preserve the integration formula because {1} has length zero. Take a continuous test equal to one near the unit circle and supported in a compact annulus. Its fibre integral would jump from π to 2π at 1. This is precisely the ambiguity that continuity removes. The chart for a scalar length at a finite place uses the same uniqueness argument.

One example checks the direction of the transport factor. For G(z) = ‖z‖² on ℂ² and r > 0, dilation z ↦ rz multiplies the length by r². Its pushforward on the four real source coordinates is r−4 times volume, and on the real target it is r−2 times length measure. The mass transported from the fibre at s to the fibre at r²s therefore acquires the ratio r². Directly, the real ball of radius √s has volume proportional to s². Differentiating with respect to s gives a fibre mass proportional to s, as predicted.

How the source and target normalizations enter

Replace μ by aμ and ν by cν with a, c > 0. Multiplying every fibre measure by a/c preserves support and continuity, and both sides of the integration formula are multiplied by a. Pointwise uniqueness forces this scaling at each regular T. It accounts for the ratio between coordinate and Fourier measures in the global product.
