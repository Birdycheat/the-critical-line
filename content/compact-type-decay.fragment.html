How does a gap between two weights make a matrix coefficient small?

The compression identity expresses the coefficient as an average over the compact group K. The part of its integrand that changes as we move away from K is a product of determinants. We first estimate this product at each point of the average.

Write J for the diagonal matrix of signs defining the unitary group in dimension m, and q for the smaller of the numbers of positive and negative signs. Suppose q ≥ 1. Choose a Hermitian matrix X with JX = −XJ. For k ∈ K put Y = k−1Xk and t = ‖X‖F = ‖Y‖F. The matrix Y also interchanges the two sign spaces. Let Δℓ(s) be the determinant on the first ℓ rows and columns of exp(sY), numbering coordinates in decreasing order of the weights λ₁ > ⋯ > λm. Exp(sY) is positive definite, so all these determinants are positive for real s. For consecutive weights set bℓ = λℓ − λℓ+1 + 1. Suppose the weight difference across each pair of opposite signs is at least h ≥ 0 in absolute value. Then

∏ℓ=1m−1 Δℓ(1)−bℓ ≤ exp(−(h/8) min{t², t/√(2q)}).

What one cut measures. Let P be orthogonal projection onto the first ℓ coordinates, and let A be the corresponding diagonal block of Y. Every diagonal entry of Y vanishes, so Δℓ′(0) = tr A = 0. Expanding the determinant of the block of I + sY + s²Y²/2 through second order gives Δℓ″(0) = tr(PY²P) − tr(A²). Hermitian symmetry cancels the entries with both indices in the block. What survives is the squared size of the entries crossing its boundary, Vℓ = Σj≤ℓ<i |Yij|².

To turn this derivative into a bound at s = 1, diagonalize Y in an orthonormal basis. Cauchy–Binet applied to the compressed exponential writes Δℓ(s) = ΣI wI exp(s yI), where yI is the sum of the ℓ eigenvalues selected by I. The weights are squared absolute minors of the change of basis. They sum to Δℓ(0) = 1. Thus the first two derivatives give a random variable with mean zero and second moment Vℓ. The off-diagonal sign blocks have rank at most q each. At most 2q eigenvalues are nonzero and their squares sum to t². Cauchy–Schwarz gives |yI| ≤ R = √(2q)t.

If t = 0 the product is 1. Otherwise put a = min{1, 1/R}. On [−1,1] the cubic Taylor lower bound for the exponential gives exp(x) ≥ 1 + x + x²/3. Averaging at x = a yI yields Δℓ(a) ≥ 1 + a²Vℓ/3. The added term lies in [0,1/3], where log(1+x) ≥ 3x/4 by integration of 1/(1+x). Concavity of x ↦ xa gives Δℓ(a) ≤ Δℓ(1)a. Together these give log Δℓ(1) ≥ aVℓ/4.

Why the weights add up. An entry in row i and column j with j < i contributes at cuts ℓ = j, …, i−1. The corresponding weights sum to λj − λi + i − j. For a nonzero entry the two coordinates have opposite signs, so this sum is at least h. Each conjugate pair has one entry below the diagonal, giving Σℓ bℓVℓ ≥ ht²/2. Multiplication by a/4 proves the product bound, since at² = min{t², t/√(2q)}. For example, an entry at (4,2) appears at cuts 2 and 3. The intermediate weight λ₃ cancels.

A two-cut check. In dimension three take signs (+,+,−), weights (2,1,0), and place log 2 at entries (1,3) and (3,1) of Y, leaving all other entries zero. Exponentiation gives cosh(log 2) = 5/4 on the first and third diagonal entries and 1 on the second. Both leading minors are 5/4 and both weights bℓ equal 2. Their product with negative exponents is (4/5)⁴. This small gap tests the determinant calculation before the large-gap hypothesis is needed for compression.

Why does the compact average preserve that decay?

Let π be the unitary discrete series with admissible parameter λ, and let S be its lowest K-type. Write d = dim S, choose a unit highest-weight vector w in S and give K Haar measure of mass one. The compression identity applies when h ≥ 120m q3/2. Its scalar factor at k−1exp(X)k has absolute value equal to the preceding product. The full determinant contributes 1 because tr X = 0. Denote the uniform product bound by D(t).

Write fx(k) = ⟨x,π(k)w⟩. Substituting X = 0 and testing the compression identity against x in both slots gives d∫K|fx|² = ‖x‖² for every x ∈ S. For two vectors u, v ∈ S, take absolute values in the integral at X and apply Cauchy–Schwarz to the two functions on K. Each function has norm ‖fx‖₂ = ‖x‖/√d. Hence

|⟨v,π(exp X)u⟩| ≤ dD(t) ‖fv‖₂ ‖fu‖₂ = D(t) ‖v‖ ‖u‖.

This also controls every group element g. The positive matrix g*g satisfies J(g*g)J = (g*g)−1. Its positive square root is exp X, with X Hermitian and JXJ = −X by diagonalization. Then k = g exp(−X) is both unitary and signed unitary, so k ∈ K. Moving π(k) to the first slot replaces v by a vector of the same norm in S. The singular values sj of g are the eigenvalues of exp X, so the argument of D is t = (Σj (log sj)²)1/2.

How many powers of the Frobenius norm are available for integration?

The integral over the stabilizer uses the ordinary Frobenius norm F = ‖g‖F. The preceding bound uses logarithms of singular values. Here is the conversion, with the constants later spent in the restricted period integral.

Lowest-type coefficient estimate. Suppose m ≥ 3 and the opposite-sign parameters of π are separated by at least h = 1000m³. For any unit vector v₀ in its lowest K-type and any g in the signed unitary group,

|⟨v₀,π(g)v₀⟩| ≤ exp(100m³) F−40m².

First take q ≥ 1. The stated h exceeds 120m q3/2 since q ≤ m. Set M = min{t², t/√(2q)}. The estimate on the compact average gives log of the upper bound equal to −125m³M. Each singular value is at most exp(t), hence F² = Σ sj² ≤ m exp(2t). Also t ≤ 1 + mM. This is immediate when t ≤ 1. For t ≥ 1 both mt² and mt/√(2q) are at least t. Using log m ≤ m, we obtain

40m² log F ≤ 20m³ + 40m² + 40m³M ≤ 100m³ + 125m³M.

Rearranging and exponentiating proves the estimate. If q = 0 the group is compact and all singular values are 1. Then F = √m, and the right-hand side is at least 1 by log m ≤ m. Unitarity bounds the coefficient by 1. This argument uses one representation. The choice of a translate with a large projection needs a second representation on the stabilizer.
