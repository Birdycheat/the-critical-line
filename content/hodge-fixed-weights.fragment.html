Which roots enter the weight of a marked point?

At a base coincidence the period chart singles out a line. The action on that line has one eigenvalue μ. Marking a root means remembering this eigenvalue when we calculate the contribution of the fibre. We will sum over the possible markings only after computing one of them.

Let F be the coefficient field, a CM field, and let χ be the monic degree-n polynomial of the action in this chart. Here χ has distinct roots and nonzero constant term. At every embedding τ of F into ℂ write Xτ for its list of roots, obtained by applying τ to the coefficients first. Write 0 for the distinguished embedding and 1 for its complex conjugate. Number their lists so that x1j is the complex conjugate of x0j. The marking picks an index i with μ = x0i. If the global element has polynomial P, the action in this chart uses its inverse. After transporting the coefficients, χ(X) = XnP(1/X)/P(0). If the distinguished embedding agrees with the chosen representative of the infinite place under this identification, a global mark ν becomes 1/ν. For the conjugate representative it becomes 1/ν.

There are n − 1 tangent directions on the base. In period coordinates they are the linear coordinates from each of the unmarked eigenlines to the marked line. On such a coordinate the action multiplies the output by μ and the input by the inverse of x0j. The eigenvalue is μ/x0j. The base factor in the trace of one fibre is therefore

Bi(y) = ∏j ≠ i (x0j − yμ)/(x0j − μ).

On one torus factor labelled by α, the eigenvalues have the form τ(α)xτj. The signature specifies which eigenvalues act on the holomorphic tangent space. At embedding 0 it selects the marked entry, and at 1 it selects the conjugates of the unmarked entries. At each remaining τ it selects either the whole list or the empty list. Write ετ = 1 or 0 respectively. Exactly one embedding of each remaining conjugate pair selects the whole list. These choices account for every entry once when the holomorphic tangent and its complex conjugate are taken together.

To keep track of form degree, multiply each selected entry by y and leave every other entry as it stands. Call the resulting list X(i)τ. The factor y in the fibre determinant belongs to its holomorphic part. The conjugate factor is evaluated at 1. With D(a,X) = ∏j(1 − a xj), the whole local term for a tuple α of k labels is

Bi(y) ∏s=1k ∏τ D(τ(αs), X(i)τ).

The denominators in Bi are nonzero because the roots are distinct. For the fibre take αs = 2βs with βs integral. The isogeny and the integral coefficient action preserve the period lattice in this chart. If the resulting fibre operator had eigenvalue 1, the operator with label βs would have eigenvalue 1/2. Its characteristic polynomial in a lattice basis is monic and integral. Substituting 1/2 and clearing the denominator would equate an odd integer to zero. This proves transversality on the fibre. At y = 1 the base factor is 1 and the product counts its coincidence points.

How can finitely many integral labels select a weight?

For each embedding τ fix a partition λτ, a weakly decreasing list of n nonnegative integers. The associated Schur polynomial sλτ(Xτ) gives the character in these variables. Our goal is to obtain the product of these characters by combining fibre maps with different labels. We first treat all the roots as independent variables. This will give one choice of coefficients valid at every point.

Write ed(X) for the sum of the products of d distinct variables in X, and put e₀ = 1. Expanding the determinant gives D(u,X) = ∑d=0n(−u)ded(X). By the fundamental theorem of symmetric polynomials every Schur polynomial is a polynomial in the ed. Choose k at least as large as the number of elementary factors in every monomial occurring at any one embedding. Any larger k works by padding with e₀. R. P. Stanley, Enumerative Combinatorics, vol. 2, §7.4.

Give each slot (s,τ) a separate variable uτs. In ∏s,τ D(uτs,Xτ), prescribing the power of that variable selects one elementary factor and its sign. Hence a finite linear combination of coefficient extractions gives precisely the desired product of Schur polynomials. It remains to realize those extractions by evaluations at actual integral labels.

Let d = [F : ℚ]. In an integral basis ω₁,…,ωd, the vector of embeddings of an integer is the image of its coordinates in ℤd under the matrix (τ(ωj)). Its determinant squared is the nonzero discriminant of the basis. The doubled lattice is therefore an invertible complex linear image of an integer grid. A polynomial vanishing on this grid vanishes identically. For one variable this follows from the finite root bound. For more variables fix all but one coordinate, apply that bound, and apply induction to every coefficient. The same argument works for k independent labels. J. S. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, Propositions 2.26 and 2.29.

We can require every label to be nonzero. If a polynomial vanished at all the allowed points, multiplying it by ∏τ,suτs would make it vanish on the entire doubled grid. The product of variables is a nonzero polynomial, so the original polynomial must vanish identically. In the finite-dimensional space of polynomials of degree at most n in each slot, evaluations at allowed points thus span the dual space. Indeed, a proper span would annihilate a nonzero polynomial. Each coefficient extraction is consequently a finite linear combination of such evaluations. Applying that combination to our determinant product proves the identity

∑α cα ∏s,τ D(τ(αs),Xτ) = ∏τ sλτ(Xτ).

For example take F = ℚ(i) and n = k = 1. Write Qa = (1 − aX)(1 − aZ). The difference Q−2 − Q₂ is 4(X + Z). The difference Q−2i − Q2i is 4i(X − Z). Dividing by 8 and 8i respectively and adding gives X. Thus four nonzero even labels select the distinguished variable. In every rank, the coefficients are fixed before inserting the lists at a marked point. They depend on the weights and the fibre power and work for every marked root, every y and every admitted Hecke test.

Why does summing over the marked roots select individual form degrees?

After interpolation, the summand for mark i is Bi(y) ∏τsλτ(X(i)τ). Write |λ| for the sum of the entries of a partition, and number them from 0 to n − 1. Outside the distinguished pair every variable at τ was multiplied by yετ. Homogeneity gives the common exponent C = ∑τ ≠ 0,1 ετ|λτ|. It remains to see how a choice of one root becomes a choice of one exponent.

Use the bialternant formula for Schur polynomials. Set V(X) = ∏j<k(xj − xk). With exponents dr = λr + n − 1 − r, we have sλ(X)V(X) = det(xjdr)r,j. Subtracting the degrees of the two determinants gives homogeneity of degree |λ|. When every λr equals m, factoring xjm from each column gives sλ(X) = (∏jxj)m. R. P. Stanley, Enumerative Combinatorics, vol. 2, §7.15, Theorem 7.15.1.

Constant weight at embedding 1. Write its entries as m. Exactly n − 1 entries of its root list were scaled, giving the common factor y(n−1)m. At embedding 0, scaling column i of the root list changes the Vandermonde determinant by the factor Bi(y). Thus the marked Schur polynomial times Bi is the alternant with the scaled root, divided by the original nonzero V. In each permutation term of the determinant, column i receives one row exponent. As i ranges over the columns, every row exponent occurs once. Summing multiplies the original alternant by ∑rydr. Consequently the sum over all marks is the original character product times

∑r=0n−1 yC + (n−1)m + λ0r + n−1−r.

Constant weight at embedding 0. Its marked factor supplies ym. Here the unitary comparison gives all distinguished roots the same nonzero absolute value, so x0jx1j is independent of j. Substitution into the base factor gives Bi(y) = ∏j≠i(x1i − yx1j)/(x1i − x1j). At embedding 1 every column except i is now scaled. The pairs involving column i supply this base factor in the Vandermonde ratio. Every pair of other columns supplies another factor y, giving a common power y(n−1)(n−2)/2.

In a permutation term omitting column i from the scaling omits just one exponent dr. Each omission occurs once. Removing the common power from the Vandermonde leaves ∑j≠rdj − (n−1)(n−2)/2 = |λ₁| − λ1r + r. We cancel the common power in the polynomial identity before taking any value of y, so the formula includes y = 0. In this case the original character product is multiplied by

∑r=0n−1 yC + m + |λ₁| − λ1r + r.

For a two-variable calculation, take X₀ = (1,i), X₁ = (1,−i), λ₀ = (2,0) and λ₁ = (0,0). The character at embedding 0 is 1 + i + i² = i. Scaling its first column can select exponent 3 or 0 according to the permutation. Scaling its second selects the other exponent in the same term. Their sum is i(1 + y³). In general the first exponent list strictly decreases and the second strictly increases, since each weight list is weakly decreasing. Thus any listed degree occurs exactly once. The definite pair comparison identifies the coefficient that multiplies this list for a Hecke test.
