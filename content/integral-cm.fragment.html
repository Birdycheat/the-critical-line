Why does the CM action have integral coefficients in the curve coordinate?

The Frobenius calculation distinguishes the linear multiplier α from its conjugate. The next argument uses a formal power series with this linear term. Let λ be the formal logarithm of the good elliptic curve and e its inverse. In characteristic zero, multiplication by α must act in the curve coordinate T as M(T) = e(αλ(T)). Even λ can have a denominator divisible by p. We need to know why every coefficient of this particular composition belongs to the valuation ring R = {x : |x|p ≤ 1}.

Take a CM lattice stable under ω = (1 + √−d)/2, with d ≡ 3 mod 4, and a good model at an inert prime p ≥ 5 with p ∤ d. The model supplies a rescaled lattice with the same CM action. Put n = (d + 1)/4 and let α be the image of ω. Its equation gives α(1 − α) = n. If p divided n, then −d ≡ 1 mod p, contradicting inertness. Both factors are integral roots of the monic quadratic, and their product is a unit. Thus α and 1 − α are units. Including the origin, the kernel of multiplication by ω on the complex torus has exactly n points. What enters the coordinate formula? Choose representatives of its n − 1 nonzero points. For a representative P, ωP is a lattice point, and so is ω²P by stability. Their difference is nP. The prime-to-p torsion estimate therefore applies. In that estimate the product of max(1, |xQ|p) over the nonzero n-torsion representatives is at most 1. Every factor is at least 1, so each xQ is integral. Periodicity gives the same for xP. The good Weierstrass equation then bounds the other coordinate yP = ℘′(P)/2. For example, at d = 23 and p = 5, the five nonzero kernel points are all killed by 6. This distinction matters because the product bound uses the full 6-torsion, while the endomorphism formula sums over just those five points. How does a squared coordinate determine every coefficient?

The curve’s x-coordinate has a double pole at the origin. Write A(T) = T²x(T) for the regularized coordinate, with constant term 1. The addition formula expresses the coordinate after applying the isogeny in terms of its translates by the kernel points. Clearing the pole gives an integral series V with V(0) = 1. In detail, the correction to A is T²(∑P(x+P(T) − xP) − r). Here x+P is the coordinate after translation on the curve, and r is the integral constant from the change of Weierstrass coordinates. Since M(0) = 0 and M′(0) = α ≠ 0, write M = αTg with g(0) = 1. For d = 3 the good j = 0 model has equation y² = x³ − 1/4. Its logarithm has terms only in degrees 6j + 1. The multiplier satisfies α² − α + 1 = 0, hence α⁶ = 1. In every surviving degree α6j+1 = α, so λ(αT) = αλ(T). Applying the inverse series gives M(T) = αT exactly. For instance λ₂₅ = 99/1280 has a denominator divisible by 5, while the coefficient of degree 25 in M is zero. The explicit logarithm is calculated in the good-model example. For a general CM lattice the coefficient induction replaces this sixth-root symmetry.

The height-two proof uses two facts about this same series. Its coefficients are integral, so one can reduce it at p. Its derivative α is exchanged with 1 − α under Frobenius. The resulting difference is a unit, which is used in the two Frobenius steps.
