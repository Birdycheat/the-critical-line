Why is there a canonical flag below ½?

At the point x the data are a self-dual Hermitian lattice Λ in an n-dimensional space over the CM field, and a positive definite metric at each real place. A lattice size is a normalized covolume. Its rank enters the exponent so that scaling lengths by a factor c scales the size by c. For a nonzero lattice M, its largest size is the supremum of the sizes of its nonzero quotients M/K, where K is saturated and the quotient carries the quotient metric. Saturation means that the quotient has no torsion. The least size τ of Λ is the infimum of the sizes of its nonzero sublattices.

For t > 0, let F≤t be the sum of all nonzero sublattices whose largest size is at most t, and define F<t using a strict inequality. The Harder–Narasimhan filtration theorem for Hermitian lattices, derived below, supplies the following size properties. Both sums are saturated. When nonzero, the closed step has largest size at most t and the open step has largest size strictly below t. Every nonzero lattice in Λ/F≤t has size strictly greater than t, and every nonzero lattice in Λ/F<t has size at least t. In positive rank the closed step is zero precisely when t < τ and the open step precisely when t ≤ τ.

A jump is a size t for which F<t ⊊ F≤t. Its quotient has size exactly t. Indeed the open-step inequality bounds its size below by t, and the largest size of the closed step bounds it above by t. Every nonzero closed step occurs at a jump. To see this, take s equal to its largest size. The lattice is included among the generators at s and s ≤ t, so F≤s = F≤t. Its largest size equals s, while a nonzero open step at s must have largest size strictly below s. The two steps at s are therefore distinct. The same argument at the largest size of a nonzero open step locates its last jump strictly below its endpoint.

At each jump the rank increases because the quotient of the two saturated steps is nonzero and torsion-free. If s < t, then F≤s ⊆ F<t. Thus distinct jumps have distinct resulting ranks in {1,…,n}. Listing the jumps below ½ in increasing order gives the finite flag. When τ < ½ the first jump is τ, since its open step is zero and its closed step is nonzero. The last lattice is exactly S = F<½. Any jump at ½ itself falls outside this list.

Why do all pairings on the short part vanish?

For a saturated sublattice M, write M⊥Λ = Λ ∩ (span M)⊥. This operation reverses inclusions and takes a step of rank r to one of rank n−r. In the self-dual lattice, successive perpendicular quotients are Hermitian duals of the original quotients, with reciprocal normalized sizes. For example, the quotient dual to M′/M is M⊥Λ/(M′)⊥Λ. The reciprocal formula follows from the determinant pairing, using the dual metrics and the integral Hermitian dual, as shown below. Reversing a semistable filtration preserves semistability after dualizing its pieces. A lower bound on the size of every sublattice in a semistable piece of size s gives an upper bound s on the size of each nonzero quotient, by multiplicativity of determinant volumes. The dual sublattices consequently have size at least 1/s. Thus reversing the canonical list of sizes and inverting each size gives the canonical list again. The endpoint convention is important.

(F<t)⊥Λ = F≤1/t,   (F≤t)⊥Λ = F<1/t.

Indeed the jumps remaining after the strict step have sizes at least t, so the corresponding dual jumps have sizes at most 1/t. At t = ½ this gives S⊥Λ = F≤2. Monotonicity gives S ⊆ F≤2, so every pair of vectors of S has Hermitian pairing zero. The same implication works at any cutoff 0 < t ≤ 1. The choice ½ also ensures that the next endpoint l used in the gap estimate satisfies l² ≤ ¼.

The size inequality for a morphism answers a related question about a specified integral map. If the largest size of its domain is at most H, the least size of the target is at least L, and the map is nonzero, the geometric mean of its local operator norms is at least L/H. Thus it detects when a proposed small map must vanish. The reciprocal-step identity above identifies the whole orthogonal lattice and gives isotropy directly by inclusion.

To bound the rank, extend the rational span of S to one real place of the base field, hence to a complex Hermitian space of signature (pᵥ,qᵥ). Rationally independent vectors remain independent after this scalar extension. In coordinates in which the form is ‖z+‖²−‖z−‖², projection to either coordinate space is injective on an isotropic subspace. A vector in the kernel of either projection has zero norm also in the other coordinate, and hence is zero. Consequently

a ≤ rank S ≤ min(pᵥ,qᵥ) at every real place v.

Here a is the number of jumps below ½. This proves the inequality a ≤ q used to obtain the exponent 1/q in the orbit radius.

See the strict endpoint on a Gaussian hyperbolic plane

Let Λ = ℤ[i]e ⊕ ℤ[i]b, with ⟨e,b⟩ = 1 and both diagonal pairings zero. Use the compatible positive metric ‖z e + w b‖² = |z|²/16 + 16|w|². The line ℤ[i]e has normalized size ¼. The quotient by it has normalized size 4. Since the full lattice has a quotient of size 4, its largest size is at least 4. Thus the saturated step below ½ contains the first line, has rank at most one, and equals that line. At its first jump F<¼ = 0 and F≤¼ is the line. Correspondingly F≤4 is the full lattice while F<4 is the line. In fact any proper nonzero saturated sublattice is generated by a primitive vector (z,w), whose length is at least ¼. Its quotient has reciprocal size because Λ has normalized determinant volume one. The largest quotient size is therefore 4, attained for w = 0.
