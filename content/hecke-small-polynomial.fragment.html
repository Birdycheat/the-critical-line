How can a polynomial equal one on all the bad parameters and have small mean square?

At a split place of rank a a spherical system has an unordered list of a nonzero complex Satake parameters. A given nonzero polynomial Φ can depend on their order. The local test fails if some ordering makes Φ vanish. The operator used in the counting proof must then act as the identity. Its coefficient bound will come from an integral over the unit torus 𝕋a, with each circle carrying its uniform probability measure.

Polynomial estimate. Given any δ > 0, there is a symmetric Laurent polynomial p with p(z)=1 at every failed list z ∈ (ℂ×)a and ∫𝕋a |p(z)|² dz ≤ δ. A Laurent polynomial allows both positive and negative integer powers. Symmetry makes its value independent of how the parameters are ordered.

Form the product P of Φ over all permutations of its variables. Permuting the coordinates permutes the factors. Each factor is a nonzero polynomial, so their product is nonzero. A tuple fails the test precisely when P vanishes there. If P = ∑ν cνzν, let P∗ be the Laurent polynomial with conjugate coefficients and opposite exponents. On the unit torus each inverse coordinate is its complex conjugate, so there P∗(z) is the complex conjugate of P(z). Let M be the maximum of |P| on the compact torus and set c = 1/(1 + M²). For positive integers k put

pk(z) = (1 − cP(z)P∗(z))k.

Both factors of the product PP∗ are symmetric. At every zero of P the displayed value is exactly one for every k, including zeros away from the unit torus. On the torus the quantity being raised to the kth power is the real number 1 − c|P(z)|². It lies between zero and one, and it is strictly less than one wherever P(z) ≠ 0.

Why do the exceptional points have measure zero? In one variable a nonzero polynomial has only finitely many zeros. In several variables expand in the last variable and select a coefficient that is a nonzero polynomial in the others. By induction it vanishes on a set of measure zero. Outside this set, the one-variable slice again has only finitely many zeros. Integrating the indicator of the full zero set one coordinate at a time therefore gives zero. Now |pk|² tends to zero almost everywhere and is bounded by one on a probability space. Dominated convergence makes its integral tend to zero. Choose k large enough to reach δ. S. Axler, Measure, Integration & Real Analysis, Theorems 5.28 and 3.31.

For example take one parameter and Φ(z) = z − 2. Its zero lies off the circle. Here M = 3, and the first power is p1(z) = (5 + 2z + 2z−1)/10. Direct substitution gives p1(2) = 1. On the circle the mean square is (25 + 4 + 4)/100 = 33/100, since all nonconstant integer powers of z integrate to zero. Higher powers keep the value at 2 and drive the mean square to zero. The local Plancherel formula turns that mean square into an estimate for the coefficients of the operator.
