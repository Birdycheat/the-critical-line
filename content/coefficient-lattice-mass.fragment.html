How much do all coefficient lines contribute?

One block in the marked-slice bound has rank j. For a full lattice Λ in Ej, let b(Λ) be the mass of its unit logarithmic determinant slab. Let Vⱼ be the sum of these masses, choosing one lattice per class under GLj(E). For example, an ideal I and the lattice 2I give the same class. Summing over all their integral multiples would count one positive contribution infinitely often. The first calculation determines V₁. The next two determine how it changes when the rank grows.

Put f = [E:ℚ]/2 and Δ = 2−f√|disc(E)|, the Euclidean covolume of the integer ring in ℂf. Thus Δ = νEf in the flag notation. A full lattice on a line is a fractional ideal. Two such ideals give the same class precisely when one is a nonzero field multiple of the other. All have the same automorphism group. Indeed, for an invertible ideal I, multiplying uI = I by I−1 gives u𝒪E = 𝒪E. Each of the h ideal classes therefore has the same slab mass.

Write zᵥ in polar coordinates and put tᵥ = log |zᵥ|². The Haar measure dxᵥ dyᵥ/|zᵥ|² becomes ½ dtᵥ dθᵥ. The slab imposes 0 ≤ ∑ tᵥ < 1. By Dirichlet’s unit theorem the logarithms of the units form a lattice in the hyperplane ∑ tᵥ = 0. In the first f−1 coordinates its fundamental parallelepiped has volume R, the regulator. Replacing the last coordinate by ∑ tᵥ has Jacobian one. Reducing the first f−1 coordinates modulo the free units leaves w roots of unity acting freely on the angles. Their quotient has 1/w of the full angular volume. Including the factors of ½ and the h classes gives

V₁ = h πf R/w = Δ Ress=1 ζE(s).

The second equality is the analytic class number formula for a totally complex field. For ℚ(i) the Gaussian integers admit Euclidean division by rounding the two real coordinates, so there is one ideal class. Here R = 1, w = 4 and Δ = 1. The answer π/4 recovers the Haar mass of the Gaussian unit slab.

Which ideal does a marked vector remember?

To compare ranks, mark one nonzero vector x of a rank-j coefficient lattice Λ. The saturated line through x consists of the multiples c x that still lie in Λ. Its coefficients form a fractional ideal I containing 1. Thus I = 𝔞−1 for a unique nonzero integral ideal 𝔞. For a primitive vector I = 𝒪E. For x = 2e₁ in the standard lattice, the coefficient 1/2 is allowed, so I = ½𝒪E and 𝔞 = 2𝒪E.

Use x as the first rational basis vector, and project to the other k = j−1 coordinates. The image Λ′ is a full torsion-free module over the integer ring. Every finitely generated torsion-free module over this Dedekind domain is projective, so the projection admits an integral linear section on Λ′. Its first coordinate extends to an E-linear functional ℓ on Ek. The shear (c,y) ↦ (c−ℓ(y),y) sends this section to the last k coordinates and fixes the marked vector. The pair now has the form

(Λ,x) ≃ (𝔞−1 ⊕ Λ′, e₁).

Every displayed pair exists, since 1 belongs to 𝔞−1. A change of basis fixing e₁ fixes its scalar multiples pointwise and induces a rational change of basis on the quotient. It therefore preserves the actual ideal 𝔞 and the class of Λ′. Conversely, a change between two quotient lattices extends by the identity on the marked line. This proves that the labels are exact. In rank one without a marking we summed over ideal classes. Marking a vector fixes the scale on its line, so here even two distinct principal ideals can supply different terms.

Why does an ideal of norm N receive weight N−j?

Start with one pair (𝔞−1 ⊕ Λ′, e₁). Move its lattice by g−1 at the complex places. Take j ≥ 2 and two nonnegative measurable functions. Test the position of the marked vector with Φ and the covolume of the moved lattice with w. Write N = |𝒪E/𝔞|, α = Δ/N for the covolume of the inverse ideal, and κ′ for the covolume of Λ′. The covolume before moving is κ = ακ′.

The vector coordinate. At one place put x = g−1e₁. On each patch where a selected coordinate of x is nonzero, complete x to a matrix s(x) of determinant one. This can be done by appending coordinate vectors and rescaling the last column. Partition by the first nonzero coordinate to use just one completion per vector. Write g−1 as s(x) times a block matrix with first column e₁, upper row (1,v) and lower-right block H. At fixed x the change on the last k columns preserves ordinary volume since det s(x) = 1. The matrix Haar density is |det H|−2j. Factoring out the rank-k Haar density leaves |det H|−2. Inversion H ↦ H−1 preserves the Haar measure of GLk(ℂ). Thus for h = H−1 and T = −v, the measure across all places is dx dT D(h) dμk(h), with D(h) = ∏ |det hᵥ|². This explains both the ordinary vector integral ∫Φ and the extra determinant.

The two covolumes. The stabilizer of the marked vector has a quotient-block automorphism and a row mapping Λ′ into 𝔞−1. Reduce the block modulo its automorphisms, then reduce the row modulo this lattice of maps. Translating the row leaves the chosen quotient block fixed. Distinct translates of the resulting coordinate domain overlap only on sets of measure zero. The rectangular Hom-lattice formula gives the row volume αkΔk/κ′. The extra determinant multiplies the block measure b(Λ′) dD/D, leaving b(Λ′) dD. The covolume input for w is t = κ/D, so this integral becomes b(Λ′) κ ∫₀∞ w(t) dt/t². The two appearances of κ′ cancel in their product.

(αk Δk/κ′)(ακ′) = Δ2j−1/Nj,   j = k+1.

For ℤ[i], take j = 2, 𝔞 = (1+i) and Λ′ = 2ℤ[i]. Then α = 1/2 and κ′ = 4. The row area is 1/8 and the full covolume is 2, giving 1/4. Varying Λ′ in the experiment changes both factors. Their product depends only on the ideal norm and rank.

Adding these contributions over the pair labels counts the original integral of the sum over nonzero vectors. Indeed each marked pair occurs once up to rational change of basis, and translating a fundamental domain by that change preserves Haar measure. Tonelli’s theorem allows the countable reordering. The sum over Λ′ gives Vj−1. The sum over 𝔞 gives ζE(j) by the convergent ideal series for j ≥ 2. The resulting constant for marked lattices is Cⱼ = Vj−1Δ2j−1ζE(j). We still need the mass of the lattices after forgetting the vector.

How can a count with a marked vector determine an unmarked volume?

In each coefficient class integrate over the real matrix quotient, now allowing every determinant, and sum these integrals. Write ⟨·⟩ for this sum of integrals. A function of the moved lattice’s covolume t alone has integral Vⱼ ∫₀∞ w(t) dt/t by the slab formula and the substitution t = κ/D. With a sum over nonzero vectors it has the marked coefficient Cⱼ and covolume measure dt/t². For every nonnegative measurable vector test Φ the marked formula reads

⟨w(t) ∑0≠x∈L Φ(x)⟩ = Cⱼ (∫ Φ(x) dx) (∫₀∞ w(t) dt/t²).

A Gaussian lets us evaluate both kinds of integral in a single identity.

For a moved lattice L, let A(L) be ∑0≠x∈L exp(−π‖x‖²). The Gaussian has integral one. Write L∨ for the dual under the pairing ∑v,a Re(xᵥₐyᵥₐ). Conjugating the second argument converts this pairing to the ordinary Euclidean one and preserves the Gaussian. Poisson summation gives 1 + A(L) = (1 + A(L∨))/t. The two ones are the zero vectors on the two sides. The dual has covolume 1/t. Dualizing the arithmetic lattice sends full classes bijectively to full classes. The pairing of embedded field vectors is half the rational trace of their dot product, and taking the integral dual twice returns the original lattice. On moving matrices the change is inverse transpose, which preserves matrix Haar measure. Thus integrating a function of L∨ over all classes gives the same value as integrating it on L.

For t > 1 multiply the Gaussian identity by 1/t and gather the zero-vector terms on the left. This gives (t−1−t−2) + t−1A(L) = t−2A(L∨). Integrate only over t > 1. The first term is unmarked, giving Vⱼ∫₁∞(t−2−t−3) dt = Vⱼ/2. The second is marked, giving Cⱼ∫₁∞ t−3 dt = Cⱼ/2. On the right use the dual lattice as variable. Its covolume u = 1/t satisfies 0 < u < 1, so its marked integral is Cⱼ∫₀¹ u² du/u² = Cⱼ. Consequently

Vⱼ/2 + Cⱼ/2 = Cⱼ,   Vⱼ = Cⱼ.

The previous rank has finite mass by induction from V₁, and ζE(j) is finite for j ≥ 2. The right side is therefore already finite when the identity is used to determine Vⱼ.

Where do the class sums enter the flag probability?

Each increase from rank r−1 to rank r contributes Δ2r−1ζE(r). The line contributes Δ times the residue at one. Adding the odd exponents 1+3+⋯+(2j−1) = j² gives the total:

Vⱼ = Δj² Ress=1 ζE(s) ∏2≤r≤j ζE(r).

For one marked flag the block of rank jᵢ contributed 2f jᵢ²b(Λᵢ). Its metric factor is independent of Λᵢ. Enlarge the set of realized markings to all tuples of coefficient classes and residual classes. Every summand is nonnegative. Summing first over each coefficient class therefore bounds the combined block contribution by ∏i 2f jᵢ²Vjᵢ. With two ordered blocks of the same rank, the two slots range independently in this upper bound and supply the square of the one-block factor. For the residual Hermitian class the sum of stabilizer covolumes is bounded by its own group volume. That is the volume that cancels in the normalized flag probability.
