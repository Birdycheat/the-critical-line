Why does the crystalline correction reduce to the identity?

The change of height keeps the matrices ε(g) of the Galois action on the period basis. We still need to know how the initial frame acquires the crystalline condition. Write u for the variable of the coefficient ring 𝔖 and E for its Eisenstein polynomial. Let v be the image of u in Ainf. The condition we need is that every entry of ε(g) − I belongs to vAinf, for every g. The divisibility by E used in changing the height answers a different question.

On the closed disc of radius ℓ−1/deg E we use two rings of functions. Write DS for the coefficient disc formed from 𝔖 and DA for its counterpart over Ainf. Evaluation gives a map from the first to the second. Both allow division by ℓ. We use the same letters for elements and their images when taking a quotient of the second ring.

In the crystalline case the initial period matrices on the disc may be chosen with trivial Galois correction κ(g) = I. More precisely, the crystalline period construction supplies matrices satisfying the Galois identity without a correction. To use them as semistable input, insert I in that identity. The required bounds on poles persist after passage to the disc because any extra power of ℓ in them is a unit there. The approximation required of κ holds at every order with the constant matrix I. The existence of those initial crystalline periods is an input to the single-layer theorem.

The matrix ε(g) is written in a new basis. To compare it to κ(g), the construction supplies a square matrix X over 𝔖, a companion X′, and an invertible matrix Z over DS. They satisfy XX′ = ℓcE2aI for some nonnegative c, a. Set P equal to the evaluated product XZ. For each g its action extends to the disc. Changing bases in the seed identity gives

g(E)a P ε(g) = Ea κ(g) g(P)   in DA.

Reduce this equation modulo v. To see what reduction does to g(P), first take a single function on the coefficient disc. Every such function has the form c + u f, with c in the completed coefficient field. On a power series the decomposition removes its constant term and shifts the remaining coefficients by one. The shift has operator norm at most the reciprocal of the disc radius, so the same decomposition holds on the completion. The local Galois group fixes the coefficients and carries v to a multiple of v. Consequently a function and its conjugate have the same value modulo v after evaluation. This applies to the entries of P and to E.

Modulo v, the Eisenstein polynomial equals its constant coefficient, ℓ times a unit. Its class is a unit since ℓ is invertible on the disc. The identity for XX′ now gives a right inverse for the reduction of X after division by a unit. A square matrix with a right inverse over a commutative ring is invertible, by taking determinants. Thus P is invertible modulo v as well. Substituting κ(g) = I into the displayed identity and cancelling the units Ea and P gives ε(g) ≡ I in the disc quotient.

To return to Ainf we use the following integral comparison. If the image on this closed disc of an element of Ainf is divisible by v, then that element is divisible by v already in Ainf. The comparison also holds for divisibility by each power of the image of E. Apply the first assertion to each entry of ε(g) − I. This proves the crystalline test on the integral frame.

How the integral comparison recovers a quotient

The disc comparison uses a topological basis of Ainf over 𝔖. Its coefficient maps extend to the closed disc and take the coefficients of any disc element to zero outside finite sets in the disc norm. For the divisor f = u or Es, divisibility of a coefficient in the disc of 𝔖 descends to 𝔖. For u this follows by testing the constant coefficient. For E it follows by Weierstrass division and uniqueness of the remainder, followed by induction for powers. These are the coefficient comparison inputs.

Apply the coefficient maps to the equation “image of x = f times y” on the disc. The coefficient of x at each basis vector has an integral quotient by f. Its image on the disc is the corresponding coefficient of y, by cancellation. By the norm comparison on integral coefficients, these quotients tend to zero for the powers of J = (ℓ, E) in 𝔖. In Ainf use the ideal generated by ev(J). Modulo each power of this ideal only finitely many remain. Summing those finitely many terms gives compatible approximations to a quotient in Ainf. Completeness gives their limit. Multiplying it by the evaluated f recovers x at every precision, hence exactly by separatedness.

How do we construct a de Rham witness at a prescribed precision?

Let ε(g) be the integral Galois correction of a frame obtained from period data on the closed disc. We now treat an arbitrary initial correction κ(g). Write δ(g) = φ(ε(g)), where φ is Witt Frobenius, and write η for the image of E in Ainf. Given n ≥ 0, we want an integral matrix β over 𝔖 that is a scalar modulo E and whose failure of equivariance is divisible by ηn. The scalar may depend on n. The same β must work for every g.

β ≡ ℓkI (mod E),   δ(g) g(ev β) ≡ ev β (mod ηn)   for every g.

Transporting the correction. On the disc the Frobenius comparison supplies an invertible matrix U over the coefficient disc whose evaluation satisfies Uδ(g) = κ(g) g(U) for every g. More explicitly, the Frobenius matrix of the seed times the Frobenius transform of the basis change is a unit there. Multiplying this product by φ(E)−a, where a is the seed height, cancels the two scalar factors in its Galois identity. Here φ(E) is invertible on the chosen disc. This gives U and, by multiplying on the left by U−1 and on the right by g(U−1), the useful identity δ(g) g(U−1) = U−1κ(g).

Work to precision m = n + 1, so reduction modulo E is available even if n = 0. The initial period data supply a matrix Λ over 𝔖 with Λ ≡ ℓa₁I modulo E and κ(g) g(Λ) ≡ Λ modulo Em on the disc. We use two approximations from integral power series. First choose a matrix S over 𝔖 that agrees with ℓa₂U modulo E on the coefficient disc and whose evaluated entries are Galois invariant modulo ηm. Next choose β over 𝔖 approximating the product below, to order m on the coefficient disc. We write the images under evaluation implicitly in these disc congruences.

β ≡ ℓa₃ U−1 Λ S   (mod Em),   k = a₁ + a₂ + a₃.

The order of the three matrices explains both conditions. Modulo E the middle matrix is scalar and the last is ℓa₂U. Hence the two copies of U cancel and the product is ℓkI. Divisibility by E reflects back to 𝔖. Modulo Em the Galois action preserves the ideal, since it carries η to a multiple of η. Multiplying the conjugate of the displayed product by δ(g) gives ℓa₃ U−1[κ(g) g(Λ)] g(S). The seed condition replaces the bracket by Λ, and the choice of S replaces its conjugate by S. We obtain δ(g) g(β) ≡ β on the disc. The integral disc comparison gives the same divisibility by ηm in Ainf, and therefore by ηn.

Where the two integral approximations come from

For a scalar disc function x, Weierstrass division at this radius gives ℓbx = Eq + r, with q a disc function and r an integral polynomial of degree below deg E. Inductively approximate q modulo Em−1 after multiplying by another power of ℓ. The equation then approximates x modulo Em. For a matrix, add the finitely many exponents needed by its entries and multiply each approximant by the missing power. This proves the approximation used for β.

The lift used for S also has to be nearly invariant. At precision m, the approximate-root theorem for the Eisenstein polynomial supplies an integral replacement z for ℓbu modulo E. The images of z under the local Galois group agree modulo ηm. The independence theorem for these approximate roots initially allows a power of ℓ on their difference. The saturation of the ideal (ηm) removes it. Put e = deg E. The residue of an integral power series modulo E is a polynomial ∑0≤j<e cj uj with coefficients in the Witt coefficient ring. Replace it by ∑0≤j<e cj ℓb(e−j)zj. Its residue is ℓbe times the original residue, and all evaluated terms are invariant modulo ηm. Applying this construction to an integral approximation to U modulo E gives S with the two required properties.

A short calculation illustrates the transport step. On ℚ[t] let g send t to −t. Let J have its sole nonzero entry, a 1, in the upper right corner of a two-by-two matrix. Put U = I + tJ and κ(g) = I. Because J² = 0, δ(g) = U−1 g(U) = I − 2tJ. With Λ = S = I and β = I − tJ, reduction at t = 0 gives β = I and direct multiplication gives δ(g) g(β) = β. The integral construction above obtains the corresponding identities to each requested order from approximate data.

What changes if the matrices are multiplied in reverse order?

Keep U and g from the calculation above. Set L = Jt, Λ = I + tL, κ(g) = Λ g(Λ)−1 and δ(g) = U−1κ(g)g(U). The product β = U−1Λ satisfies δ(g) g(β) = β by cancellation. In the reversed product B = ΛU−1 the first row is (1, −t) and the second is (t, 1 − t²). The second row of δ(g) is (2t, 1 − 2t²). Multiplying it by the first column (1, −t) of g(B) gives t + 2t³. Subtracting the lower left entry of B leaves 2t³. The reversed product therefore already fails at precision t⁴.

Why can every layer use the same frame height?

Single-layer theorem. Let K be a finite extension of ℚℓ, with the integral and rational period rings and evaluation maps described above. Let ρ be a continuous action of its absolute Galois group on a free ℤℓ-module of rank d. Assume its rationalization is semistable, and that its decreasing de Rham filtration is the whole period space at −H and zero at H + 1, with H ≥ 0. It has an integral frame of height H satisfying the de Rham witness condition. If the rationalization is crystalline, the frame can be chosen to satisfy the crystalline congruence as well.

There are two independent choices to make on this layer. The filtration tells us the height we want. Semistability gives a de Rham representation. After extending scalars from the maximal unramified subfield to K, the semistable periods inject into the de Rham periods. Their dimension is already d, the upper bound for the target dimension. The adapted basis now has degrees in [−H, H]. We also need a first set of period matrices for the given integral lattice. O. Brinon and B. Conrad, CMI Summer School Notes on p-adic Hodge Theory, Lemma 9.2.13.

Initial period data. The crystalline lattice-period theorem starts with a basis of invariant crystalline periods for the rational representation and a basis of the given integral lattice. Clearing denominators in the crystalline period ring and modifying the lattice according to its de Rham filtration produces data on the coefficient disc with trivial initial correction. In the general semistable case the corresponding theorem starts with a period matrix at logarithm zero, its monodromy operator and the additive change of the logarithm under Galois action. A finite exponential in the nilpotent monodromy operator supplies κ(g). The integral period theorem provides bounds on its poles and, for each prescribed order, a matrix Λ that is invariant to that order after twisting by κ. These two period-existence theorems are inputs here. Both concern the same representation and the same lattice for which the frame is required.

From the data to an initial height. Denote the height of the initial period data by a. The finite-height lattice theorem supplies Y over Ainf, an invertible change Z on the coefficient disc, a nonnegative integer b, and matrices C, C′ over 𝔖. In the Witt ring the image of Y is invertible. On the disc, the seed period matrix times the evaluated Z is the image of Y. The two algebraic relations we need are CC′ = EbI and φ(Y) = Y ev(C). Let t be the Witt image of the chosen period τ. It is a unit and φ(t) = evW(E)t, where evW means evaluation followed by the Witt embedding. Set T equal to the Witt image of Y times t−a. Substitution in the Frobenius identity and cancellation of ta gives evW(E)aφ(T) = T evW(C).

N = a + b,   A = EbC,   A′ = E2aC′,   AA′ = E2NI.

Indeed the exponent in the product is b + 2a + b. Multiplying the preceding Frobenius identity by evW(E)b gives the required one at height N. The seed invariance on the subgroup fixing the tilt parameter gives ρ(g) g(T) = T on that subgroup. For arbitrary g, the integral Galois descent theorem for these disc data supplies ε(g) in Ainf with ρ(g) g(T) = T ε(g) after Witt embedding. It supplies the accompanying disc identity between κ and ε used in the crystalline test. The transport of the de Rham witnesses gives the other period condition.

The first height N is now available. The height-change theorem replaces it by H and preserves T and every ε(g). In the crystalline case we chose the crystalline input at the outset, so both tests pass for this one frame. On the tower of local layers, the common bound on the filtration degrees gives the same H before choosing any of these frames. Each layer can have its own rank, initial height, correction and witnesses. The passage to the target still needs a comparison between different layers with one bound on the loss of precision.
