What does the rank of convolution say about the sum of its coefficients?

Let H be a group of order N equal to a power of the prime ℓ. A function a on H prescribes the coefficients of a sum of translations. On a constant function this operator acts by the single number S = ∑u∈H a(u). The rank of the operator bounds the ℓ-adic size of S.

Group determinant bound. Take coefficients in a field k with an ultrametric absolute value, so |x+y| ≤ max(|x|, |y|). Suppose |a(u)| ≤ 1 for every u. Form the matrix M with rows and columns indexed by H, and let r be its rank. Then

Mh,g=a(hg−1),   |S|N ≤ |ℓ|N−r.

For each u let Lu carry the basis vector at g to the one at ug. The only translation carrying g to h has u=hg−1, so M is the sum of a(u)Lu. We first change coordinates for the integer matrices Lu modulo ℓ. In any nonzero quotient by an invariant subspace of the permutation representation over 𝔽ℓ, count the vectors in H-orbits. Each orbit has size a power of ℓ by the orbit–stabilizer formula. The number of fixed vectors is therefore congruent modulo ℓ to the total number of vectors, itself a positive power of ℓ. Since zero is fixed, there must also be a nonzero fixed vector. Its line is invariant. Continuing on successive quotients and lifting the fixed vectors gives a basis in which every Lu is upper triangular with ones on the diagonal. K. Conrad, Group actions, Theorem 4.1.

Lift this basis matrix and its inverse separately to integer matrices P and Q. The entries of QLuP below the diagonal are multiples of ℓ, and its diagonal entries are 1 modulo ℓ. Put A=QMP over k. There is a matrix B with entries of absolute value at most one such that A + ℓB is upper triangular with every diagonal entry equal to S. Indeed, on and below the diagonal each translated matrix differs from the identity by integer multiples of ℓ. Subtract these multiples there before summing with weights a(u). Every integer has absolute value at most one, so the ultrametric inequality bounds all entries of the correction B and of A. The rank of A is at most r by composition.

Expand the determinant of A + ℓB by multilinearity in the rows. A term choosing fewer than N−r rows from ℓB uses more than r rows of A and vanishes by linear dependence. In each surviving term one can factor out at least N−r copies of ℓ. The determinant left after extracting all chosen factors has absolute value at most one by the permutation formula. Since |ℓ| ≤ 1, the entire sum has absolute value at most |ℓ|N−r. The determinant is SN by triangularity.

How do bounds on linear tests produce one approximating function?

On the functions kX on a finite set X, measure distance by the largest absolute difference of the values. A linear test with coefficients ai evaluates v by ∑ aivi. Call it integral when every |ai| ≤ 1. A test of this kind that vanishes on a subspace P distinguishes a vector from that subspace by at most their distance. The converse gives an approximant at the specified radius.

Finite separation lemma. Suppose every integral test annihilating P evaluates on v to a number of absolute value at most R, where R ≥ 0. Then some c ∈ P satisfies |vi−ci| ≤ R at every coordinate.

Let d be the dimension of P. When d = 0, take each coordinate as a test and choose c = 0. Otherwise write a basis of P as the columns of a matrix. Select d rows indexed by I for which the determinant has largest absolute value. That determinant is nonzero because the columns are independent. Every other row j is a linear combination of the selected rows, with coefficients bji. Replace row i of the selected determinant by row j and expand in that row. All terms except the one with coefficient bji have a repeated row. The new determinant divided by the selected determinant is therefore bji, so |bji| ≤ 1.

The invertible selected matrix gives a unique c ∈ P with ci=vi for every i ∈ I. For a remaining coordinate j, the test

x ↦ xj − ∑i∈I bjixi

is integral and vanishes on P by the row relation. Its value on v is exactly vj−cj because c matches v on I. The hypothesis bounds every remaining coordinate for this same c. This finite construction works over the algebraic closure of ℚℓ.

Why does half the space suffice to approximate the constant function?

Finite-group approximation theorem. Let H be a finite ℓ-group, N its order and P a subspace of the functions on H over the algebraic closure of ℚℓ. Assume that P contains the right translate h ↦ c(hg) of each of its functions c for every g ∈ H. If its dimension d satisfies 2d ≥ N, there is a function φ ∈ P with

maxh∈H |1 − φ(h)|ℓ ≤ ℓ−1/2.

Take any integral test a that annihilates P. Its value on the constant function is S = ∑ a(h). For Mh,g=a(hg−1) the coordinate at g of Mtc is ∑h a(hg−1)c(h) = ∑i a(i)c(ig). This is zero because the translated function belongs to P. Thus P lies in the kernel of the transpose and rank M ≤ N−d. Since |ℓ| = 1/ℓ, the group determinant bound gives |S|N ≤ |ℓ|d. Taking the Nth root gives |S| ≤ |ℓ|d/N ≤ ℓ−1/2, including the case d=N/2.

Apply the finite separation lemma to v = 1 and R = ℓ−1/2. It gives one φ meeting the bound at every group element. The automorphic forms have complex coefficients with a fixed field identification between ℂ and the algebraic closure of ℚℓ. Transport the subspace and its basis along this identification. Right translation only permutes coordinates, so both the dimension and the translation hypothesis are preserved. Transferring φ back gives the multiplier in the Hecke correction.
