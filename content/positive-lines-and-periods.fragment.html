Which complex structures correspond to positive lines?

The standard polarisation gives one complex structure in diagonal coordinates. The criterion for a fixed point ranges over a positive line ℓ that can vary. Fix the rational polarised form B and its Hermitian matrix M at the chosen infinite place. Write h(x,y) = y∗Mx, linear in the first argument. Fix a sign s = ±1 and put t = s i. For the unitary datum this is the sign of the CM type at the place, positive when its embedding takes the distinguished scalar to the prescribed value.

Positive-line parametrisation. If h has signature (1,n−1), its positive complex lines correspond bijectively to complex-linear operators J satisfying J² = −I, h(Jx,Jy) = h(x,y) and Re(−t h(Jx,x)) > 0 for every nonzero x. The line is the t-eigenspace. For any generator z of it, the inverse map is

Pℓx = [h(x,z)/h(z,z)]z,   Jℓ = t(2Pℓ − I).

Proof. The identity J² = −I splits each x into its two components (x − tJx)/2 and (x + tJx)/2, in the eigenspaces for t and −t. The two eigenspaces are orthogonal because applying J to both arguments multiplies the pairing between them by t·conj(−t) = −1. There the positivity condition becomes, respectively, h(x,x) > 0 and −h(x,x) > 0 for nonzero x. The positive index is one, so the first space is exactly a line.

Conversely, split x into its components x+ and x− along ℓ and ℓ⊥. The projection gives x+. Replacing z by cz for c ≠ 0 leaves the projection unchanged. Multiplication by t on ℓ and −t on ℓ⊥ preserves h, squares to −I, and yields the positive expression h(x+,x+) − h(x−,x−). At a negative-definite place the positive eigenspace is zero, so the sole admissible operator is multiplication by −t.

For the form with diagonal (1, −1), the line spanned by (1, α) is positive exactly when |α| < 1. In the disc diagram the boundary is where the denominator of the projection vanishes.

A nonreal line in dimension two

Take h(x,y) = x1conj(y1) − x2conj(y2). At α = i/2 let z = (1,i/2) and w = (−i/2,1). Their values under the Hermitian form are 3/4 and −3/4, and h(w,z) = 0. Here h(x,z) = x1 + (i/2)x2. For s = +1, the two rows of 3Jℓ are (5i,−4) and (−4,−5i). Thus Jℓz = iz and Jℓw = −iw. At s = −1 the same Hermitian line gives the negative of this matrix.

When do two rational periods give the same positive line?

Fix a linked unitary datum of rank n with a = m = 1 and common signature embedding τ₀. Write F for its CM field, V for its rational space and B for a rational polarised form in the prescribed class. In archimedean coordinates θ, the realification of V is a product of spaces ℂn, one at each infinite place. The alternating form E of B is the sum of the forms Re(−sv′ i hv′) with positive real coefficients. The real equivalence in the definition of B preserves the standard signatures. There is one place v of positive index one, and the other spaces are negative definite.

Classification of rational periods. Fix an order action ρ on ℂg of the prescribed signature. A rational period is an additive map Q from V to ℂg that intertwines the order actions, has image spanning the target over ℝ, and pulls the imaginary part of a positive Hermitian form back to E. The periods up to complex-linear change of target are in bijection with the positive lines for hv.

From a period to its line. The map Q extends uniquely to a real-linear isomorphism R on the product, since its image spans and the real dimensions agree. Pull multiplication by i back through R, giving J. It commutes with the CM field and therefore acts complex-linearly on each place. The polarisation gives E(Jx,Jy) = E(x,y) and E(Jx,x) > 0 for nonzero x. On a single summand, testing the first identity also at ix gives both parts of the Hermitian identity. The positive eigenspace at v supplies the line ℓ. In period coordinates its vectors satisfy R(iz) = sv iR(z).

From a line to a period. Give the product the operator J determined by ℓ at v and the unique admissible scalar elsewhere. The signature of ρ gives an isomorphism R from this complex space to ℂg intertwining the order action. For product vectors x and y, set

H(Rx,Ry) = E(Jx,y) + iE(x,y).

Alternation and J-invariance give Hermitian symmetry and complex linearity in the first variable. The real part on the diagonal is positive by the placewise calculation. Thus Q = Rθ has the required polarisation and gives ℓ. If two periods give the same line, their pulled-back operators agree at every place. Then R′R−1 is a complex-linear change of target. Conversely, any such change preserves J and hence ℓ.

Why the signature supplies the required change of coordinates

Choose a primitive element of F. Its distinct complex roots index the embeddings of the field. Over ℝ its conjugate pairs give complex factors by the Chinese remainder theorem. The factor idempotents separate the places, and multiplication by i inside each complex factor explains why an operator commuting with F acts complex-linearly there. The primitive element theorem. The Chinese remainder theorem.

The integers of F map onto the acting order, with involution induced by conjugation. The signature prescribes their traces under ρ. Its embedding multiplicities are 1 at τ₀, n−1 at its conjugate, and n at the chosen member of each other conjugate pair, zero at the other. Indeed, the trace extends to the complexified field algebra, and evaluating it at a factor idempotent counts the dimension of that factor. A basis of ℓ followed by one of ℓ⊥ exhibits exactly these multiplicities in the complex space defined by J. At a place where J acts by −i on a component, conjugating its coordinate gives the conjugate embedding. Matching the equal-dimensional embedding spaces gives the required R.

For a rational similitude γ of B with positive multiplier, transport by Q′(γw) = Q(w) sends ℓ to γvℓ. Indeed the transported operator is γJγ−1. This gives the archimedean coordinate of a complex point. The two finite markings specify the integral lattice and its level data. Their simultaneous equivalence with the period identifies the full moduli point.
