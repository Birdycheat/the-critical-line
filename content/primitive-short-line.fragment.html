How short a vector does every integral line contain?

Let E be the CM field in the rank-one row count, unramified at finite places over its real subfield. Write 2f = [E ∶ ℚ], Δ = |disc(E)| and ρ = Δ1/(2f), the root discriminant. Fix a one-dimensional E-space with a Hermitian form h positive at every archimedean place. For an integral full lattice M in it put n = |M∨/M|. There exists a nonzero x ∈ M satisfying the following bound at every embedding σ. The expression on the left is the squared length at that embedding.

σ(h(x,x)) ≤ B,   B = ρ n1/(2f).

To keep track of a possibly nonprincipal lattice, choose any nonzero m ∈ M, put b = h(m,m) and write x₀ = m/b. The algebraic integer b is totally positive and fixed by conjugation. Since h(m,x₀) = 1, each element r x₀ of M has integral coefficient r. These coefficients form a nonzero integral ideal I. Thus M = I x₀, and h(x₀,x₀) = 1/b. Pairing with every element of I x₀ gives M∨ = b Ī−1x₀. Here Ī is the conjugate ideal. The ratio of the covolumes of these two ideals yields n = N(I)²/N((b)), where N denotes absolute ideal norm.

Choose one embedding in each of the f conjugate pairs and consider a product of open complex disks of radii Rσ = √(Bσ(b)). The product of their squared radii is Bf√N((b)) = √Δ N(I). Under the ordinary real and imaginary coordinates of these embeddings, the ideal lattice has covolume 2−f√Δ N(I). The volume of the disks is πf√Δ N(I), strictly greater than 22f times that covolume. Minkowski’s convex body theorem therefore gives a nonzero a ∈ I in the disks, by shrinking them slightly to closed disks first. Take x = a x₀. Its squared length at σ is |σ(a)|²/σ(b) < B. Conjugate embeddings give the same value. The ideal covolume calculation uses the same real measure. J. S. Milne, Algebraic Number Theory, theorem 4.19.

How does one short representative bound the number of lines?

Let L be an integral full lattice in a totally positive definite Hermitian E-space of rank k ≥ 1. We count the rational lines U for which L ∩ U has discriminant order n ≥ 1. This intersection is a full lattice on U. It is finitely generated as a submodule of L, and clearing the denominators of any vector of U brings a nonzero scalar multiple into L. The short-vector theorem applied to the intersection gives a nonzero x ∈ L spanning U over E. Thus the number of these lines is at most the number of vectors of L satisfying the bound at all embeddings. Several short vectors can span the same line.

Use the real Euclidean norm ‖x‖2 = TrE/ℚ(h(x,x)) on the real vector space of dimension 2kf obtained from the rational lattice. For every nonzero difference of lattice vectors, its squared Hermitian length is a totally positive algebraic integer. The product of its 2f conjugates is a positive integer, hence at least one. Their arithmetic mean is at least their geometric mean. Consequently distinct lattice points are at distance at least √(2f) in this norm. Every vector with squared length at most B at all places lies in the ball of radius √(2fB).

Put disjoint open balls of radius √(2f)/2 around these lattice points. They all lie in the ball with that radius added to √(2fB). Comparing volumes bounds their number by (1 + 2√B)2kf, including the origin. Since B ≥ 1, each factor 1 + 2√B is at most 3√B. Substitution now gives

# {U of order n} ≤ (9ρ)kf nk/2.

The rank-one row bound takes n = d². The last factor becomes dk. The estimate holds for each individual lattice in the genus, so summing with weights 1/|Aut(L)| multiplies the uniform bound by the genus mass. Rank zero has at most the single zero subspace.

A line whose chosen basis vector is too long. In E = ℚ(ζ₁₂) with real field ℚ(√3), the two absolute discriminants are 144 and 12. The discriminant tower formula gives relative discriminant norm 144/12² = 1, so this example satisfies the finite unramified hypothesis. Write ε = 2 + √3, a unit with inverse 2 − √3. In the standard coordinate lattice 𝒪E² put the Hermitian Gram matrix diag(2ε²,1). On the first coordinate line the intersection is 𝒪Ee₁. Its dual is (2ε²)−1𝒪Ee₁, so n = 16. Here ρ = √12 and B = 2√12. At one place the vector e₁ has squared length 14 + 8√3, greater than B. The single vector ε−1e₁ lies in the same lattice and has squared length 2 at both places. Its trace squared norm is 8, the sum over four embeddings. Milne, §§2, 3 and 6 on discriminants and cyclotomic integers.
