Why do the conjugate forms fit in one finite-dimensional space?

Let f be a nonzero complex-valued automorphic form on the definite unitary group of rank n attached to the CM extension E/E+. View the group over ℚ by restriction of scalars from E+. Write Γ for its rational points, U for its compact group at infinity and Gf for the finite adelic group. Choose a compact open level K fixing f on the right. For a field automorphism σ of ℂ, the conjugate form fσ must satisfy fσ(s) = σ(f(s)) for every s ∈ Gf, where the component at infinity is the identity. This prescription determines a continuous automorphic form uniquely. Indeed, left Γ-invariance gives its values on ΓGf, which is dense. The density follows from the rational Cayley parametrization at infinity, since the finite component can be chosen arbitrarily.

Proposition. There is a degree D depending on f such that all the forms fσ exist and belong to one complex vector space M. Let h be the number of classes in Γ ∖ G(𝔸) / (U K). The finite class number theorem gives h < ∞. With b = [E ∶ ℚ]n², a common bound is

dimℂ M ≤ h · (b + D)! / (b! D!).

The degree at infinity. Take the matrix entries on U at every complex embedding τ of E. There are b labelled coordinates, including both embeddings of each conjugate pair. Let PD be the span of the products of at most D such coordinates, including the constant 1. The archimedean right translates of f span a finite-dimensional space by the definition of an automorphic form. Choose a basis e1, …, em of this space. Expanding each translate in that basis gives

f(s x) = ∑j=1m ej(s) pj(x),   s ∈ Gf,   x ∈ U.

The functions pj are matrix coefficients of this finite-dimensional action. Each is continuous and its right translates span a finite-dimensional space. Every ej has level K because the finite and infinite translations commute.

Why is each coefficient a polynomial? Let v be such a coefficient and V the span of its right translates Ryv(x) = v(xy). For a coordinate polynomial p, integrate these translates against p(y) using normalized Haar measure on U. The resulting function belongs to V. It is also a coordinate polynomial in x. Indeed, substitution u = xy gives the integrand v(u)p(x−1u). Since unitary inversion is conjugate transpose and the coordinates include conjugate entries, the last factor expands into a finite sum of polynomials in x times polynomials in u.

If v lay outside the span of these integrals, a linear functional L on V would vanish on all of them and have L(v) ≠ 0. Then g(y) = L(Ryv) would integrate to zero against every coordinate polynomial. These polynomials contain the constants, separate the points of U and are closed under complex conjugation. The complex Stone–Weierstrass theorem therefore gives uniform polynomial approximations to the complex conjugate of g. Passing to the limit yields ∫ |g|² = 0. Haar measure gives positive mass to every nonempty open set, so continuity forces g(1) = L(v) = 0. This proves that v itself is a polynomial. Taking the largest degree in the finite list p1, …, pm gives D. M. H. Stone, The Generalized Weierstrass Approximation Theorem.

What conjugation preserves. In the expression for pj, apply σ to the coefficients and replace each label τ by σ ∘ τ, keeping its two matrix indices. The resulting polynomial pj,σ still has degree at most D. At the infinite component of a rational matrix γ, its labelled entry is τ(γab), so the new polynomial has value σ(pj(γ∞)). Define

fσ(sx) = ∑j=1m σ(ej(s)) pj,σ(x).

The finite coefficients are locally constant on right K-cosets, and the polynomials are continuous in x. Thus this formula is continuous even for a discontinuous σ. It agrees with σ(f) on ΓGf. There it is left Γ-invariant, and continuity extends that invariance everywhere. Right translates on U substitute linear forms for the matrix entries, so the space of polynomials of degree at most D remains stable and the constructed form is finite under archimedean translations. Conjugation can permute the embeddings that describe the weight. The finite level and the degree bound hold for every σ.

For example, take E = ℚ(i) in rank one. On the unit circle the two embedding coordinates are z and its complex conjugate. A field automorphism sending i to −i interchanges their labels. At the rational point (3 + 4i)/5 the first coordinate becomes (3 − 4i)/5. Both coordinates lie in the same three-dimensional polynomial space of degree at most one, spanned by 1, z and z−1.

Counting the remaining freedom. Take one purely finite representative s for each of the h classes, absorbing its infinite component into U. Let M consist of left Γ-invariant, right K-invariant functions whose restrictions x ↦ F(sx) belong to PD for each representative. This restriction map into h copies of PD is injective because every group element has the form γsxk. The scalar coordinates commute. A monomial is specified by b nonnegative exponents of sum at most D. Adding the unused degree as one more exponent gives (b + D)! / (b! D!) possibilities. Relations among entries or symmetries of a representative may lower the dimension. The displayed bound uses only the form and its chosen level. For a rational Hecke operator, a finite rational sum of translates commutes with σ on the finite adelic values. Uniqueness then gives the conjugate eigenvalue on the constructed form, as required in the common coefficient field argument.
