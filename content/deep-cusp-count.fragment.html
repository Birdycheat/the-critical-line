How much of the trace can lie deep in the cusp?

On the deep part of the fundamental domain two quantities enter the dimension estimate. The integral of the constant identity term is its value times the volume. The integral of the projection diagonal is the sum of the integrals of |φᵢ|² on that region, one for each orthonormal basis function φᵢ. A small region can carry the whole mass of a unit vector. In the two-point example below, choose the values (0, 1) before normalizing the vector to length one. Shrinking the measure of the deep point increases the diagonal there by exactly the amount needed to keep its trace share equal to one.

Deep-region trace bound. Use the hypotheses for the indefinite count, including the spectral gap, level and signature assumptions. Here n ≥ 8 is the dimension of the Hermitian space, f ≥ 1 the number of real places, q ≥ 1 the negative index with q² ≤ n, d ≥ 1 the root discriminant of the CM field, g ≥ 1 the congruence level, and b ≥ 2 the counting parameter. Set N = n²f and X = nf d g b. Let τ(x) be the least normalized sublattice size at x. A point of the fundamental domain is deep when 0 < τ(x) < X−11q. For the rank-D cuspidal projector the assertion is

∫deep Re K(x,x) dμ(x) ≤ D exp(−N).

The proof uses both cuspidality and a bound on variation. Along the unipotent orbit attached to a short isotropic subspace, every basis function has mean zero. Deep in the cusp, that entire orbit can be represented within a small neighborhood of the point. A function with small variation there and with mean zero must itself be small. Applying this observation also to its successive derivatives gives the power of τ needed to control the diagonal.

How mean zero bounds the value at a single point

At a fixed deep point x write v(x) for the vector of the D values of the continuous cuspidal representatives. The orbit uses its invariant probability measure ν. The constant term vanishes in each coordinate. Strong approximation for the unipotent group brings orbit representatives to the small neighborhood modulo rational translations and the finite level, and continuity gives the zero average at the chosen point. If every orbit value differs from v(x) by at most a, then

‖v(x)‖ = ‖∫ [v(x) − v(u x)] dν(u)‖ ≤ ∫ ‖v(x) − v(u x)‖ dν(u) ≤ a.

For any point y of the neighborhood the same variation estimate gives ‖v(y) − v(x)‖ ≤ a. Adding the value at the center costs a second a. This factor of two makes the bound valid everywhere it will be used at the next step.

How many times can the variation estimate be applied?

Fix x and abbreviate τ(x) to τ. The unipotent orbit covering theorem supplies a neighborhood of radius ρ = n²√f g d (2τ)1/q/4. The filtered space of test vectors of order ≤ P is obtained by applying at most P archimedean derivatives to the lowest-type vectors. A derivative therefore takes a test vector to the next space. With a bound M per unit vector on that next space, the derivative estimate gives variation at most ρ√f βM on the orbit and in the neighborhood. The preceding argument replaces M by 2ρ√f βM one order lower. Use the common upper bound β = √n b² + (3/2)n3/2 + 3(8qN + N), valid at every order required here.

The initial bound at order 8qN is a local Sobolev evaluation estimate. Descend to order zero in 8qN applications. At a unit lowest-type vector, the squared norm of its vector of values is the projection diagonal. Squaring the repeated factor gives (2ρ√f β)16qN. In particular its power of τ is 16N. The initial squared evaluation bound contains (17√n/τ²)2N, which costs τ−4N. The product thus gains τ12N. As a scale check, halving τ while holding the counting data fixed multiplies this bound by 2−12N.

From a pointwise bound to the trace

The last step cancels the explicit volume and level factors in the estimate. The dimension and the definition of the deep region can still depend on the level. Write P∞ for the product of the archimedean gauge constants and kg for the positive Haar volume of the level subgroup. The pointwise and volume estimates, with the same normalizations, are

Re K(x,x) ≤ D e−N P∞/(g2NXNkg)   on the deep region,
μ(F) ≤ XNg2Nkg/P∞.

The second inequality comes from the absolute covolume bound together with the index estimate for the level subgroup. Integrate the first inequality and use μ(deep) ≤ μ(F). Every scale factor cancels, leaving De−N. The separate small-volume estimate enters when integrating the identity term on the same deep region. These are the two deep-region contributions in the four-term dimension bound.

How the threshold pays for the constants

Before normalization, the factor from differentiating and evaluating the vector is

A = (2ρ√f β)16qN(6β)Nef(17√n/τ²)2N.

We need A g2NXN ≤ e−N. In the stated range X ≥ 16. From the displayed formula for β one obtains β ≤ 30X³ ≤ X⁶ and n²f g dβ/2 ≤ X⁶. For the latter, n²f g d ≤ X² and 15 ≤ X. Substitute the expression for ρ and put Y = Xq. The Nth root of the left side is at most

2¹⁶ · 6 · 289 · e · Y⁹⁶ τ¹² · βn g²X.

Indeed f ≤ N and (17√n/τ²)² = 289n/τ⁴. The threshold gives τY¹¹ ≤ 1, so Y⁹⁶ τ¹² ≤ Y−36. Since βn g²X ≤ X¹⁰ ≤ Y¹⁰, at least Y−26 remains. Finally Y ≥ 16 and 2¹⁶ · 6 · 289 · 3² < 16²⁶. Using e < 3 makes the whole root at most e−1, as required.
