Chapter 9 · The one-sided window theorem

How derivatives concentrated on distant intervals exclude a pole. (6 novel-core · 0 rebuilt-classical · 0 open formal interfaces)
Contents & tracks · ← Ch. 8 · Ch. 10 → · Single-page book · Audit or improve Ch. 9

Suppose the arithmetic argument repeatedly finds a stretch of scales on which a centered prime sum stays below a small power of the scale. A zero of an associated L-function would force a pole of its transform. How can a bound on scattered stretches rule out even one pole? High derivatives let us choose where the integral sees the function.

9.1The hypothesis in logarithmic coordinates

Fix 0 < θ < 1 and δ > 0. Write g(u) = GF(exp u) for a real weighted sum from a finite character family F. The abstract theorem below allows any real measurable g with |g(u)| ≤ C0 exp(2u) for u ≥ 0. Its transform Φ(s) agrees with ∫0∞ g(u) exp(−su) du for Re s > 2. Assume it extends holomorphically in the right half-plane outside a set Z of simple poles, every one of which has positive real part. Every point of the half-plane must have a punctured neighborhood free of Z, including points outside Z. At each pole the residue is nonzero, and at each real pole it is real and strictly positive.

Put U = log Y. Then [Y, Y1+δ] becomes I = [U, (1 + δ)U]. For every η > 0 and every lower limit U0, suppose there exist U ≥ U0 and a majorant R on I with R ≥ max(g, 0), such that R(u) exp(−θu) is integrable there and

∫I R(u) exp(−θu) du ≤ exp(ηU).

Theorem. Every ρ ∈ Z satisfies Re ρ ≤ θ. The window and its majorant may depend on η and U0. Between windows the growth estimate still holds. An arithmetic upper bound GF(y) ≤ C yθ on arbitrarily distant windows suffices. Take R(u) = |C| exp(θu). Its weighted integral equals |C|δU, which is at most exp(ηU) once U is large.

Test the hypothesis on a single pole. Take θ = ½, δ = ½ and g(u) = exp(¾u). Its Laplace transform is 1/(s − ¾). On [U,3U/2] even the smallest admissible majorant has weighted integral 4(exp(3U/8) − exp(U/4)). For η = ⅛ this eventually exceeds exp(ηU) on every window. Change the sign of g and R = 0 works on every window, although the transform still has a pole at ¾. Its residue is now −1. This example explains the sign hypothesis at real poles and why the arithmetic only needs an upper bound.

9.2Choosing a derivative that sees the window

For Re z = σ > 2, define Ak(z) = (−1)kΦ(k)(z)/k!. In a small neighborhood with Re z ≥ 2 + α, α > 0, the differentiated integrand is bounded by C0uk exp(−αu), an integrable function. Repeated differentiation under the integral therefore gives

Ak(σ + iγ) = ∫0∞ g(u) exp(−iγu) Kk,σ(u) du,   Kk,a(u) = ukexp(−au)/k!.

The kernel is nonnegative, has integral a−k−1, and for k > 0 peaks at u = k/a. At σ + iγ the triangle inequality gives ∫|g|K as an upper bound. Adding the real-center integral with the same derivative order k leaves ∫(|g| + g)K = 2∫g+K, where g+ = max(g, 0). On I, apply vk/k! ≤ exp(v) at v = (σ − θ)u. Off I, use the growth bound. This yields

|Ak(σ + iγ)| + Re Ak(σ) ≤ 2 exp(ηU)/(σ − θ)k + 2C0 ∫(0,∞) ∖ I Kk,σ−2(u) du.

The integrand in this tail contains σ − 2 because |g| can grow as exp(2u). Estimating the tails of Kk,σ alone would lose that factor.

9.3A uniform estimate for the tails

For s > λ ≥ 0 and V = (1 + δ)U, multiply the integrand on u > V by exp(λ(u − V)), and on u < U by exp(λ(U − u)). Both multipliers are at least one in their respective regions. Extending the resulting integrals over the positive half-line gives

∫(0,∞) ∖ I Kk,s ≤ exp(−λV)/(s − λ)k+1 + exp(λU)/(s + λ)k+1.

Fix the center of the transform in terms of δ, then choose the derivative order for the window. Take λ = 2 + 6/δ, M = λ(1 + δ), σ = M(2 + λ), s = σ − 2, and k = ⌈(1 + δ/2)σU⌉. For U ≥ 1 we have k ≥ U and s > λ. To bound the first term after multiplication by σk, set a = s − λ. The inequality log(σ/a) ≤ (σ − a)/a = 1/(M − 1), together with (1 + δ/2)σ = M(M − 1), gives −λV + k log(σ/a) ≤ 1. Its contribution is at most exp(1)/a.

For the second term set b = s + λ. Then log(σ/b) ≤ −(λ − 2)/b. Since (1 + δ/2)(λ − 2) = λ + 1 and λ(σ − 2 + λ) ≤ (λ + 1)σ, the lower bound on k gives λU − k(λ − 2)/b ≤ 0. Therefore this contribution is at most 1/b. Both bounds are independent of the chosen window. Combining with the preceding inequality gives a constant C1 such that, for every η > 0 and arbitrarily large U, some integer k ≥ U satisfies simultaneously for every real γ

Locate one of the kernels numerically

Take δ = 1 and U = 10. These choices give λ = 8, M = 16, σ = 160 and k = 2400. The known interval is [10,20], and K2400,160 peaks at 15. Even the kernel after the growth correction peaks at 2400/158 ≈ 15.19. For fixed δ and growing U, how wide is a kernel compared with the interval? Normalizing Kk,a to have integral one gives mean (k + 1)/a and variance (k + 1)/a². Both follow by taking its first two moments with the integral formula above. Thus its standard deviation grows like √U, while the window’s width grows like U. The exponential tail inequalities give the stronger rate needed for the pole argument.

|Ak(σ + iγ)| + Re Ak(σ) ≤ C1 exp(ηU)/(σ − θ)k.

The order k can change each time the arithmetic supplies a new window.

9.4Isolating the pole seen by the derivatives

Suppose a pole has real part greater than θ. All poles lie to the left of σ because the Laplace integral is holomorphic on Re s > 2. Write E = σ − θ. The closed disk of radius E about σ lies in Re s ≥ θ > 0, so it contains finitely many poles by compactness and the punctured-neighborhood assumption. If it contains no poles, take D = E. The hypothesized pole then has horizontal distance strictly less than D and gives the first case below. Otherwise let D be the distance from σ to a nearest pole in the disk. In both cases the open disk of radius D is pole-free.

If a pole ρ1 has σ − Re ρ1 < D, then a disk of radius D centered at σ + i Im ρ1 contains it. Move that center by a sufficiently small vertical amount. Only finitely many poles in a slightly enlarged disk matter, and two distinct poles equally far from the moving center can tie at at most one height. Indeed their squared distances have a difference affine in the height. For equal imaginary parts the equality would also require equal real parts, since both poles lie to the left of σ. Avoiding the finitely many ties gives a center z = σ + iγ with a unique nearest pole ρ at distance d < D. This nearest pole can differ from the one that led us to shift the center. Its real part is at least σ − d > σ − D ≥ θ, so proving a contradiction for it suffices. Choose a radius r with d < r ≤ D isolating this pole.

In the other case all horizontal distances σ − Re ρ are at least D. There must be a nearest pole in the disk because the hypothesized pole has horizontal distance less than E. For any nearest pole the Euclidean distance D is at least its horizontal distance, now at least D. Equality forces the imaginary part to vanish. This identifies one real pole β = σ − D > θ. Every other pole in the finite disk is strictly farther away. Choose r with D < r ≤ E isolating β.

9.5Two incompatible derivative rates

In the first case subtract c/(s − ρ), where c is the nonzero residue. Its subtraction leaves a holomorphic function on the disk after filling the removable value at ρ. For R = (d + r)/2, Cauchy's estimate on the circle of radius R gives

|Ak(z) − c/(z − ρ)k+1| ≤ M1/Rk.

The real center also has a pole-free disk of radius D > R, so |Ak(σ)| ≤ M2/Rk. Substituting these estimates into the upper bound for the derivatives yields

(|c|/d) d−k ≤ (M1 + M2) R−k + C1 exp(ηU) E−k.

For a numerical reading of the last step, suppose d = 2, R = 3 and E = 4. With η = ½ log(3/2), multiplying by 2k leaves a first error proportional to (2/3)k. Since U ≤ k, the window error is at most a constant times (√(3/2)/2)k. Both tend to zero, however sparsely the integers k occur, while the residue contributes |c|/2 every time. The inequalities below apply the same comparison to the actual radii.

Choose 0 < η < log(R/d). Multiply the inequality by dk. Because U ≤ k and E ≥ R > d, the right side tends to zero along the chosen windows. The left side is a fixed positive number.

In the real-pole case take d = σ − β. At the real center the pole contributes c/dk+1 to Ak(σ) with real c > 0. Cauchy's estimate bounds the remainder by M1/Rk for R = (d + r)/2. The nonnegative absolute-value term in the derivative bound can be dropped. The same rate contradiction follows. A negative real residue would reverse the critical sign, which explains the positive-residue hypothesis.

9.6Choosing the exponent after choosing the alleged zero

Let β > 1/2 be the real part being tested. Pick h = ½ min(β − 1/2, 1/2) and θ = 1/2 + h. This ensures θ < min(β,1). Fix a level containing a retained character with the alleged zero. The associated Dirichlet characters, their weights and the function tested for poles are now fixed. The arithmetic construction supplies eD, eL and ε1 > 0 before ε is chosen. Set C = eD + eL + 7 and take ε = min(ε1/2, h/(|C| + 1)). Then Cε ≤ h. Choose the auxiliary arithmetic data for this ε and their large-scale threshold, then choose windows beyond that threshold and beyond 1. These data supply a bound for the weighted sum at the level already fixed. On those windows, the arithmetic exponent can be increased to θ. The theorem excludes a family pole with real part β. For the concrete analytic family, the proof also invokes known nonvanishing at Re s ≥ 1 and uses its pole construction for the remaining open strip.

9.7A zero at 0.60 and the interval that excludes it

Fix the level and its character sum detecting the zero, as in the opening example. With θ = 0.55, choose ε ≤ 0.005 within the arithmetic threshold. The specialized estimate gives ½ + 9ε ≤ 0.545. For any prescribed lower cutoff, the arithmetic construction supplies an interval [Z, Z3/2] beyond it. The auxiliary family chosen for this ε supplies both the interval and the bound. Throughout that interval the sum at the fixed level is at most x0.55. Put U = log Z. The function R(u) = exp(0.55u) bounds the positive part on [U, 3U/2]. Multiplication by exp(−0.55u) leaves the constant one. Its integral is U/2, at most exp(ηU) once U is large, for every prescribed η > 0. The window theorem now bounds every pole of this family by 0.55 in real part. A pole at 0.60 contradicts that conclusion.
