Part I · The analytic language›Ch. 1 · What the Riemann Hypothesis says›§1.1 Follow one possible zero through the proof

A single vertical line is supposed to contain every nontrivial zero of a function built from all the positive integers. Why should estimates for primes have anything to say about a point in the complex plane?

1.1Follow one possible zero through the proof

Suppose, for the sake of following the argument, that ζ had a nontrivial zero ρ with real part 0.60. What would force a contradiction? Start with the zero, then work backwards to the arithmetic estimate needed to exclude it. The number 0.60 is an illustrative choice.

  1. A zero leaves a pole. Near a zero of multiplicity m, write ζ(s)=(s−ρ)mh(s), with h(ρ)≠0. Its logarithmic derivative therefore contains m/(s−ρ). The proof recovers this pole by integrating a function of prime powers against x−s−1. The function uses a ramp weight and subtracts an explicit main term. The arithmetic construction also introduces Dirichlet characters. When those are grouped, the multiplicities at the same zero have positive weights, so the pole survives. Compute its residue.
  2. A bound on distant intervals would exclude this pole. Choose a real exponent strictly between ½ and 0.60, for example 0.55. The analytic theorem excludes poles to its right if the real centered family sum is bounded above by a constant times x0.55 on arbitrarily distant intervals of the prescribed relative width. First fix a level whose weighted character sum sees the proposed zero. For each sufficiently small positive ε the arithmetic construction supplies auxiliary data giving windows for that same sum. Its exponent on the specialized route is ½+9ε. Taking ε ≤ 0.005, subject also to the construction’s threshold, puts the exponent at most 0.545. An exponent of 0.65 would leave a pole at 0.60 outside the reach of the theorem. The full argument makes this choice after the prospective zero. See the quantifiers and check the integral on that same interval.
  3. The interval comes from arithmetic. At one translation, subtract the predicted main term from the observed prime-power count and sum over sampled indices and chain stages. Two parts of the proof constrain this remainder. A differentiated theta coefficient gives a valuation bound on a matrix entry. The Galois representation for the row enters the choice of an arithmetic window. At a prime ℓ and twist j, write hℓ,j for the length of the group of global degree-one classes whose restrictions above ℓ vanish. Its order is ℓhℓ,j, so its logarithmic size is hℓ,j log ℓ. The relevant sum of these logarithmic sizes contributes to the finite-place cost in the lattice comparison. On the chosen window the sum is at most √b, where b is the weight of one measurement. The lattice comparison bounds the remainder from above. Its character expansion then lets us divide by the total weight and bound the same fixed family sum as in step 2. Choosing the chain length near √x balances terms of order x/m and m.

The contradiction rules out the proposed 0.60. Repeating it for every real part above ½ and using the functional equation excludes the other side of the strip. For characters the functional equation changes which character occurs. Follow the reflection step.

For a first reading, the prime-power calculation, the window theorem and reflection explain exactly what the arithmetic must deliver. The reconstruction record gives their current scope. To see the two arithmetic inputs meet in one inequality, follow a single measurement.

Read the arithmetic through two finite counts

In the theta chain, start with two matrices with the same entry valuations. Eliminating one row reveals whether their lattice quotients have 9 or 81 elements. The length formula turns this calculation into two denominator limits for a general matrix. Once a row is fixed, its corner must satisfy a trace equation. The trace-zero calculation counts its solutions at the allowed level. Use the cell experiment to multiply the number of rows by the corners per row and subtract those already counted at shorter lengths. The shell sum weights the result by length to recover part of the Euler factor. At a real place a squared projection norm supplies the positive factor for the chosen vectors. To see how the pairing of two lifts becomes a double integral over their sources, compute it in both orders. Once the doubled theta mean has been identified and the local factors have been combined and continued, the norm identity shows why one lift survives. Keeping distinct labels independent then makes the downward map in the iterated chain surjective.

The Galois row used in choosing the arithmetic window comes from two parallel lifts in a separate construction. Cohomology controls what that window can cost in the bound for one measurement.

In the cohomology argument, average a function on the points of a finite plane over each line. The point-and-line experiment shows how much of a mean-zero function survives. The three projection estimates give a gap on the orthogonal complement of the common fixed functions. Those functions are constant almost everywhere, so the gap applies to coefficients of mean zero. It makes the mean-zero periods decay faster than a nonzero stabilized period could. The split of a finite Hecke average explains that lower limit on the decay. The central average first preserves the periods of the chosen cocycle. The mean of its coefficients gives a fully invariant form. Comparing the decay rates then proves that this form has the original periods, which bound the rank of the detected classes. Compare the two rates.

What does the lift supply, and where does cohomology enter?

In the lift chapter, a squared period is expressed using a doubled theta mean. The Fourier comparison identifies the mean with an Eisenstein value in the lower rank range. The 27 frequencies of a finite model show why equality on invertible indices alone leaves information missing. Two local calculations occur at different steps. To identify the theta mean, its Fourier coefficient uses a count of frames with a fixed Gram matrix. After inserting that identity into the paired lift and unfolding, the local zeta integral instead groups lattice cosets by length to compute an Euler quotient.

In the cohomology chapter, two different bounds protect the same period comparison. The lattice estimate helps bound a partition on the space of forms, so a representative has controlled size. Each point in the partition can have several group labels. The two-centre calculation shows how the normalization accounts for them. The calculation on translations identifies what an arithmetic unit can erase on the stabilizer cycles used in a Hecke average. These bounds enter on the analytic and homological sides of the two-rate comparison.

1.2From a series to a function

Write s = σ + it, where σ and t are real and i² = −1. In the half-plane σ > 1 the sum ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 exp(−s log n) converges absolutely, since the absolute values sum to ∑ n−σ. Multiplying the geometric series in p−s over primes lists each integer exactly once by unique factorization. This gives the Euler product. For example, multiply just the factors for 2 and 3. The term from the second power of 2 and the first power of 3 is 12−s. No other choice gives 12. Absolute convergence allows the finite prime sets to increase until every integer appears. DLMF §25.2[1]

ζ(s) = ∑n≥1 n−s = ∏p prime (1 − p−s)−1   (Re s > 1).

Could the infinite product vanish although every factor is nonzero? This can happen for products in general. Here the logarithms are absolutely summable. For σ > 1 their absolute sum is at most ∑p ∑j≥1 p−jσ/j ≤ (1 − 2−σ)−1 ∑n≥2 n−σ. Exponentiating the convergent sum of logarithms gives a nonzero product.

At s = ¾ the original series is a divergent sum of positive terms. The expression ζ(¾) nevertheless has a precise meaning. There is a unique meromorphic function on the plane agreeing with the series throughout Re s > 1. Meromorphic means holomorphic except at isolated poles. Here the sole pole is at 1, and the continued function is regular at ¾. It is this continued function that the theorem studies. DLMF §25.2, definition and continuation[1]

We can even determine the sign of ζ(¾). Let η(s) = 1 − 2−s + 3−s − 4−s + ⋯. For Re s > 1, changing the sign of the even terms subtracts twice their sum from ζ(s), giving η(s) = (1 − 21−s)ζ(s). The identity continues to Re s > 0. At s = ¾ every consecutive pair in η is positive, while 1 − 2¼ is negative. Hence ζ(¾) < 0. The same sign argument works at every real point between 0 and 1. A hypothetical zero with real part ¾ would therefore have a nonzero imaginary part. NIST DLMF, equation 25.2.3.[2]

Why the alternating series continues across the line Re s = 1

Pair terms numbered 2j − 1 and 2j. Their difference is s times the integral of x−s−1 from 2j − 1 to 2j. On a compact subset where Re s ≥ δ > 0 and |s| ≤ C, the absolute value of the pair is at most C(2j − 1)−1−δ. These bounds have a finite sum. The paired series therefore converges locally uniformly to a holomorphic function there, and an unpaired last term tends to zero. The product (1 − 21−s)ζ(s) also extends holomorphically through 1, since the factor has a simple zero at the simple pole of ζ. Both functions agree on Re s > 1. Uniqueness of analytic continuation gives the identity throughout Re s > 0.

1.3Which zeros?

The zeros at −2, −4, −6, … are called trivial. The remaining zeros lie in the strip 0 < σ < 1. Reflection pairs zeros across the line σ = ½. A pair at σ = 0.4 and σ = 0.6 would respect this symmetry, so symmetry alone leaves the main question open. DLMF §25.10(i)[3]

If ζ(s) = 0, s ≠ 1 and s ∉ {−2, −4, −6, …}, then Re s = ½.

The exception at 1 removes the pole of ζ from the statement about its zeros. At zero the value ζ(0) = −½ eliminates another possible endpoint.

1.4Why the book follows characters

A Dirichlet character modulo a positive integer N is a multiplicative character on its invertible residue classes, extended by zero on nonunits. Its values on units are roots of unity. Inserting χ(n) in the series for ζ weights each residue class by a phase. The resulting function L(s,χ) agrees with ζ when N = 1. The principal character at a larger modulus drops all integers with a common factor with that modulus. For example at modulus 2 its series is (1 − 2−s)ζ(s) for σ > 1. At s = 2, deleting the even terms leaves ¾ζ(2). Changing their signs as in the alternating series gives ½ζ(2).

The proof first obtains right-half-plane nonvanishing for every χ and every nonzero modulus. More precisely, σ > ½ and (χ ≠ 1 or s ≠ 1) imply L(s,χ) ≠ 0. Fixing the modulus-one character gives right-half-plane nonvanishing for ζ. The functional equation deals with the other side. The hard part is producing the prime-sum inequalities that lead to this nonvanishing. Character averages enter those inequalities before the specialization to ζ.

Check the role of modulus one

What can be concluded at a hypothetical point ρ with Re ρ = ¾ and ζ(ρ) = 0? Take N = 1 and its unique character. Then L(ρ,χ) = ζ(ρ), and ρ ≠ 1 follows from its real part. The character nonvanishing theorem contradicts this zero. At Re ρ = ¼ the same substitution does not meet the right-half-plane hypothesis. The reflection argument for ζ constructs a point with real part ¾ to which it can be applied.
