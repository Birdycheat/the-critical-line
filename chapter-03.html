Part II · Constructing the arithmetic inputs›Ch. 3 · From formal logarithms to divisibility›§3.1 A height-two change of coordinate

The proof needs a lower bound on the powers of p dividing derivatives of a theta kernel. A derivative becomes an entry of a linear map between lattices. Divisibility of those entries at many primes contributes to the global height estimate. The local difficulty already appears for one formal variable. Differentiation supplies a factorial, and undoing the formal logarithm introduces denominators. We can calculate both at the first degree where a denominator occurs.

3.1A height-two change of coordinate

The valuation vp(a) counts the powers of p in a nonzero rational a, allowing negative counts for denominators. Set vp(0) = +∞. The inequality v(a + b) ≥ min(v(a), v(b)) will carry estimates through finite coefficient sums. An integral series has coefficients of nonnegative valuation. In formal power series, questions about composition at zero can be answered degree by degree, without an analytic radius of convergence.

A formal group describes a group law by its power series near the identity. A formal logarithm converts its composition law into addition. Complex multiplication (CM) provides endomorphisms beyond integer multiplication. For a complex elliptic curve written as a quotient of the plane by a lattice, a CM scalar preserves that lattice. Here the scalar is (1 + i√d)/2. At a suitable inert prime, the logarithm used for comparison has the following sparse shape. Put q = p².

ℓ(T) = ∑j≥0 Tqʲ/pj = T + Tp²/p + Tp⁴/p² + ⋯ .

The comparison theorem assumes squarefree d ≡ 3 mod 4, CM by this scalar, prime p ≥ 5 with p ∤ d, and no square root of −d modulo p. A good model and a field identification with the completed algebraic closure of ℚp are supplied. The conclusion gives a unit ε in the closed maximal unramified field such that all coefficients of the model’s local parameter series composed with εℓ belong to that field and have norm at most one. The scalar and the direction of this composition matter.

3.2Proving the one-variable estimate

Here is an independent one-variable derivation of the loss. Take any characteristic-zero valued field extending the p-adic valuation, normalized by v(p) = 1. Since ℓ starts with T, it has a unique compositional inverse B with zero constant term and linear coefficient 1. Its coefficient bn satisfies

v(bn) ≥ −(n − 1)/(q − 1),   n ≥ 1.

Indeed B = T − ∑j≥1 Bqʲ/pj. To form degree n in a power with r = qj factors, choose positive indices summing to n. They are all smaller than n. Induction bounds their product after division by pj from below by −(n−r)/(q−1) − j. This is at least −(n−1)/(q−1) because 1 + q + ⋯ + qj−1 ≥ j. For fixed n only finitely many terms occur, and addition preserves the lower bound.

For 1 ≤ r ≤ n, the degree-n coefficient of Br loses at most (n−r)/(q−1). If A has integral coefficients, only these powers contribute to degree n ≥ 1 in A(B), so the loss is at most (n−1)/(q−1). Its n-th derivative at zero is n! times this coefficient. The exact factorial valuation is ∑j≥1⌊n/pj⌋. Each multiple of pj contributes one count at level j. Equivalently the sum is (n−sp(n))/(p−1), by expanding n in base p and summing each digit's geometric series. Since each digit is at most p−1, the deviation from n/(p−1) is at most 1 + logp n.

1/(p−1) − 1/(p²−1) = p/(p²−1) = p⁻¹ + p⁻³ + p⁻⁵ + ⋯ .

The first denominator at p = 5. Put q = 25 and choose the integral series A(T) = T. Up to degree 25, the inverse identity is B + B25/5 = T. As B starts with T, its 25th-power coefficient in degree 25 is 1. Hence [T25]B = −1/5. This attains the lower bound −(25−1)/(25−1) = −1. Its 25th derivative is −25!/5. The factorial has valuation ⌊25/5⌋ + ⌊25/25⌋ = 6, leaving valuation 5. This calculation isolates the inverse logarithm. The theta kernel also carries an exponential factor, whose mixed terms introduce the next loss.

3.3What mixed terms cost

In the multivariable estimate the derivative orders form a multi-index κ. The coefficient is multiplied by ∏i κi!. Set γp = p/(p²−1). With r coordinates and all κi ≤ a, a ≥ 1, the lower bound for the normalized valuation has the form

∑i κi(γp − σi) − r(logp a + 1).

What is a radius here? For the coefficient cν of a monomial Xν, assigning exponents ri measures its weighted valuation v(cν) + ∑νiri. A bound of zero means |cν|p ∏ |p|pνiri ≤ 1. The corresponding coordinate radius is |p|pri. Increasing ri shrinks that radius and makes the exponential easier to control. Recovering the unweighted coefficient of order κ then costs κiri. The baseline ri = 1/(p²−1) pays for the inverse logarithm. The extra σi is a choice.

The exponential explains the target weight 1/(p−1). Write its exponent as F, with F(0) = 0. If every relevant monomial of F has weighted valuation at least 1/(p−1), a term in Fm/m! has weighted valuation at least m/(p−1) − vp(m!) ≥ 0. At a given derivative order κ only monomials componentwise at most κ can enter, and the zero constant term makes the coefficient sum finite. For a mixed monomial XipαXjpβ, the extra weight contributed by the chosen radii is pασi + pβσj. The proof shows that one extra valuation unit is sufficient for each pair failing the baseline test. This gives the inequalities that the optimization below has to satisfy.

The nonnegative corrections σi pay for mixed terms of an exponential factor. Each offending monomial with exponents pα and pβ at coordinates i,j within κ requires pασi + pβσj ≥ 1. Increasing either correction helps attain feasibility and reduces the final gain by κiσi or κjσj. First bound the series at radii 1/(p²−1), increases them by the corrections to control the exponential, multiplies the two bounds, and finally uses the factorial inequality.

There are structural constraints on this optimization. The estimate assumes an odd prime, an even number of cyclic row labels ≥ 2, an ultrametric characteristic-zero field with ‖p‖ < 1, an unramified coefficient subfield, a specified primitive root, a series with coefficients in the unramified subfield, and a row-respecting integral matrix. The series must be invariant under the specified diagonal root-of-unity action. Each nonzero term has positive even-label and odd-label degree, and these two degrees cannot both be one. For each of the two prescribed additive differences of the series, exponentiation followed by substitution of the letter logarithms must give an integral series with constant coefficient one. The estimate composes an integral series with the inverse logarithms and the linear substitution, then multiplies by an exponential with sign ±1. In the frame calculation a bad pair must connect different labels with α + β odd. The radius calculation combines the bounds for individual rows, using the capacity bound on κ in the specially assigned rows. The one-variable calculation accounts for the leading gain. The next chapter identifies the global theta expansion and transfers it to these frame coordinates.

3.4Choosing correction radii on a cycle

The correction attached to a coordinate has two parts to its construction. First assign one price σs to each row label s. Then adjust individual coordinates at the rows distinguished by the frame. The first problem has an exact optimum. For the adjustment, an explicit choice with a bounded total cost suffices.

Take integers p ≥ 3 and even d ≥ 2. The optimization itself imposes no primality condition on p. Labels belong to ℤ/dℤ. Give each label a real capacity Ds ≥ 0. For each odd integer 1 ≤ j < d, an amount xs,j ≥ 0 sent from s to s + j consumes pj units of capacity at its starting point and one unit at its endpoint per unit sent. Thus the load at s is ∑j (pjxs,j + xs−j,j). Require this load to be at most Ds. Write V for the largest total ∑s,j xs,j. The zero flow is feasible. After setting unused coordinates to zero, every used coordinate lies between 0 and ∑s Ds. The continuous objective therefore attains its maximum on a closed subset of a compact box.

A feasible price vector has 0 ≤ σs ≤ 1 and pjσs + σs+j ≥ 1 on every edge. Multiply each edge inequality by the amount sent, sum, and translate the index in the incoming sum. This gives ∑s,j xs,j ≤ ∑s σs load(s) ≤ ∑s Dsσs. Equality of the optimal values yields the gain used in the coefficient estimate, with γ = p/(p²−1).

F(D) = γ ∑s Ds − V = maxfeasible σ ∑s Ds(γ − σs).

To prove equality, let C consist of pairs (b,t) for which a nonnegative flow has load ≤ b in every row and value ≥ t. This is a convex cone. It is closed because for a converging sequence of pairs, their capacities stay bounded. Truncate the witnessing flows, take a convergent subsequence in a common box, and pass the load and value inequalities to the limit. For η > 0, the point (D,V + η) lies outside C. Strict separation gives a linear functional a·b + c t which is nonnegative on the cone and negative at that point. Independent positive capacity increments belong to the cone, so as ≥ 0. Since D ≥ 0 and V + η > 0, negativity forces c < 0. Test a unit flow on each edge. Its load and value give pjas + as+j + c ≥ 0. Dividing by −c supplies prices of cost < V + η.

Clipping each price at 1 preserves every edge inequality. If either endpoint price was clipped, that endpoint alone now contributes at least 1 since pj ≥ 1. Clipping can only lower the cost because D ≥ 0. The feasible prices form a closed subset of the unit cube containing the all-ones vector, so the minimum exists. It is below V + η for every η > 0 and at least V by the load identity. Finally, every positive odd exponent j reduces modulo the even period d to another odd exponent r < d. The endpoint is unchanged and pjσs ≥ prσs. Thus the same optimizer satisfies the inequalities at all odd levels.

For example, let p = 3, d = 2 and D = (1,1). Send ¼ along each of the two edges of length one. Each load is 3/4 + 1/4 = 1. The prices (¼,¼) attain equality on both edges and cost ½, proving V = ½ and F = 2(3/8) − ½ = ¼. If D = (0,1), both flows must vanish because both use the first row. The feasible prices (1,0) have zero cost. The formula still works with a zero capacity.

From an edge to a derivative. In the two-label optimization take p = 5, a single coordinate at each label, no distinguished rows, and derivative orders κ = (500,100). The edge from 0 to 1 represents the mixed exponent pattern X₀⁵X₁. Its constraint is 5σ₀ + σ₁ ≥ 1, hence 500σ₀ + 100σ₁ ≥ 100. The symmetric prices σ₀ = σ₁ = 1/6 satisfy both directed edges with equality. Equivalently, sending 100 along the first edge consumes exactly the capacities (500,100). The minimum cost is 100. With γ = 5/24 the leading gain is 600γ − 100 = 25.

One can check that choice directly on the factorial-weighted coefficient. Suppose its series and frame hypotheses hold for this two-coordinate calculation. Including the baseline 1/24 gives weight 5/24 on each coordinate. A weighted valuation of at least zero permits an unweighted coefficient valuation as low as −600(5/24) = −125. The actual factorials contribute v₅(500!) = 100+20+4 = 124 and v₅(100!) = 20+4 = 24. The derivative therefore has valuation at least 23. The uniform estimate in the theorem gives the weaker 25 − 2(1 + log₅500) because it allows every coordinate order up to 500. The numbers in this example specialize the coefficient inequality. The kernel construction supplies its own diagram and frame.

3.5From a row price to a coefficient bound

In the frame, a coordinate has label s, row index k < N0, and a further index below N1. At each distinguished label s in a set T, the coordinates with k = 0 form its top row. Their total derivative order is ν1(s). The remaining orders sum to Ds, which equals size(s) − ν1(s) on T and size(s) elsewhere. Assume 0 ≤ ν2 ≤ ν1 ≤ size. Define the row cap Cs as ν2(s) on T and ν1(s) elsewhere. A special non-top row carries an origin u ∈ T and a signed odd shift j with s = u + j. The total order assigned the same pair (u,j) is at most Cs. Positive shifts require pj ≤ ν1(u), and negative shifts require p|j| ≤ Ds.

Let εu denote the correction on the top row at u. An ordinary non-top coordinate at s gets the optimal price σs. A special one gets max(σs, 1 − pjεu) for j > 0, and max(σs, p−|j|) for j < 0. Denote the resulting coordinate correction by ri. These maxima enforce the mixed-pair inequalities while increasing the cost only where the row price falls short.

Here is the choice of εu. Set A = ν1(u), cj = Cu+j, and let J be the least positive odd distance from u to a member of T, or ∞ if none exists. Start with the summable budget A p−j at each positive odd j, totaling Aγ. At j < J, reserve (A p−j − cj)+. At j = J reserve zero. At j > J reserve the entire budget. Write Ru for the sum of these reservations over all positive odd j. The amount left at j < J is exactly min(A p−j, cj).

Choose the first odd h < J with A p−h ≤ ch. If there is none, take h = J. Set εu = p−h for finite h, and εu = 0 for h = ∞. Positive special shifts j < h cost at most cj(1 − pjεu)+ ≤ cj, which the leftover budget covers. At finite h that budget covers Aεu, and at every j ≥ h the special surcharge vanishes. At h = ∞ the top price is zero and every used positive shift is covered individually. For finite J we also get εu ≥ p−J.

For the negative shifts out of u, let Wu be the supremum of Cu−j over positive odd j with pj ≤ Du−j, taking zero for an empty set. Each used level costs at most Wup−j. Summing over distinct levels costs at most γWu. The unadjusted non-top sum is exactly F(D) by optimality. Combining the positive and negative costs with the top contributions gives the following lower bound for the corrected gain.

F(D) + ∑u ∈ T (Ru − γWu) ≤ ∑i κi(γ − ri).

It remains to check that these corrections apply to the offending monomials. Their two exponents α, β have odd sum and their labels satisfy s + α = s′ + β in ℤ/dℤ. For two non-top coordinates with α > β, multiply the odd-shift inequality at α − β by pβ ≥ 1. Swap the coordinates when β > α. For a top and a designated non-top row, the two maxima above give the inequality for each sign of α − β. The frame’s cancellation hypothesis bounds the coefficient in the undesignated case. For two top coordinates the larger exponent includes at least the first odd distance to the other top label, and its radius alone contributes at least 1. Thus pαri + pβrj ≥ 1 for every offending pair within κ.

In the odd-prime ultrametric setting of the preceding section, the per-row cap and top-row sum bound each κi by a = max(1, maxT ν1, maxs Cs), taking the maximum over an empty T to be zero. The logarithmic factorial loss on the dN0N1 coordinates is at most dN0N1(logp a + 1). Subtract it from the left side of the displayed inequality to get the exponent in the upper bound for the norm of the factorial-weighted coefficient. This direction follows from 0 < ‖p‖ < 1. Applying this local theorem to geometric data still requires its field, series, frame and cancellation hypotheses.

The local monomial theorem next bounds a differential operator, then its frame coefficients and matrix entries. For the theta function the argument uses a decomposition with an integral factor and an inverse logarithm. It rewrites the entry in those coordinates and applies the matrix-entry theorem with sign −1. The odd-level prices therefore control the actual corrected theta series once those geometric hypotheses have been supplied.

3.6Transporting a theta function

A lattice is the integer span of linearly independent vectors in their real span. Its covolume measures one fundamental cell. A theta function on a complex vector space transforms by prescribed factors on translation by lattice points. That transformation law depends on a Hermitian form, so carrying a theta function between coordinate models requires control of both the lattice and the form.

One transport lemma takes the normalized theta function on the space associated to two admitted lattices. A complex linear isomorphism e identifies their period lattice with the target period lattice and preserves the Hermitian form. Composition with e−1 preserves holomorphicity. Pull back translation by any period x. Integrality makes its real phase parameter an integer m, so exp(πim) = (−1)m. The two translation laws then agree, including their sign. This step assumes the curve ideal, the nonzero twelfth-root normalization, the character, and the isometry. Its conclusion is membership in the target theta space. A global divisibility estimate still needs local coordinates, an integral expansion and a nonzero evaluated map.

3.7Why iterating a lift requires more than a kernel

An automorphic function is, in this setting, a function on adelic group elements with the required invariance under rational group elements and additional regularity. A theta lift integrates a function against a special theta kernel to obtain a function on another group. Write Fk for a fundamental domain and νk for its ambient invariant measure. As a model for repeated lifts, index a sequence of groups by k. One permitted step in this sequence has the form

fk+1(h) = ∫Fₖ Θk(g,h; Φ) fk(g) dνk(g).

The base function is smooth automorphic, square-integrable on the quotient, and belongs there to the prescribed starting subspace. At a step the test datum Φ must be admissible, and the integrand must be integrable for each h. Take the linear span of the resulting functions. It meets a target subspace W when it contains a member whose quotient class is outside W⊥. Even a well-defined sequence of operators can send a nonzero input to zero. This explains why a meeting or nonvanishing hypothesis is mathematically substantial. For the Galois argument one starts twice from the same source representation. In each lift a nonzero theta transform has a nonzero projection onto a target constituent. Its weight and local parameters are then read on that constituent.
